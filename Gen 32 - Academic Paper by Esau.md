---

“Please, God”: Israel as Esauic Innuendo at the Jabbok

The שאל, ברך, Graphic Paronomasia, and the Return of Esau’s Blessing in Genesis 32–33 and Hosea 12

Esau Rothmann
Olde Dominion University[^1]

---

Abstract

This essay argues that the naming of Israel at the Jabbok is constructed from several mutually reinforcing—but not equally certain—layers of wordplay. The explicit etymology of "יִשְׂרָאֵל" remains the one supplied by Genesis itself: “for you have striven” ("כִּי־שָׂרִיתָ") with God and humans and have prevailed. Beneath that official derivation, however, the narrative activates a second lexical field, "שׁאל", “ask, ask for, request, petition,” whose prefix form "יִשְׁאַל" differs graphically from undotted "ישראל" by the insertion of a single resh. Immediately after Israel is named, “Jacob asks” ("וַיִּשְׁאַל יַעֲקֹב"), says “please” ("נָּא"), and is answered with the same root: “Why do you ask?” ("לָמָּה זֶּה תִּשְׁאַל"). The ordinary verb "אמר", “say,” has meanwhile been repeated with almost mechanical density, making the sudden specification of the speech act as "שאל" unusually conspicuous.

The scene is also framed by "ברך". The standard and grammatically required meaning of its Piel forms is “bless”; under the traditional unified-root analysis, however, the Piel is the doubled or intensive formation of the same consonantal complex associated with “knee” and “kneel.” The final sentence—“and he "BRK"-ed him there”—therefore permits a bodily undertone: blessing, bending, and being brought to one’s knees. Genesis 33 then literalizes that undertone without lexical ambiguity: Jacob bows to the ground seven times before Esau, compares Esau’s face to the face of God, and says, with remarkable precision, “Please, take my blessing.” Esau takes it.

A third and more conjectural layer is graphic. In consonantal spelling, "ישראל" (y-ś-r-ʾ-l) stands close to "עשו אל" (ʿ-ś-w-ʾ-l), “Esau–El” or, as innuendo, “Esau is God.” Three positions are identical; the other two require the visually motivated exchanges yod/ayin and resh/waw. Edomite epigraphy demonstrates a mixed Hebrew–Aramaic scribal ecology, and actual inscriptions sometimes leave resh and waw open to alternative readings. The yod/ayin equivalence is less securely established in any single Iron Age hand, so the complete graphic proposal remains a hypothesis rather than a recovered ancient spelling.

Hosea 12 provides the decisive comparative control. Its phrase "וַיָּשַׂר אֶל־מַלְאָךְ", “and he strove with an angel,” becomes the exact consonantal sequence "וישראל"—“and Israel”—when the word boundary is removed. Hosea then adds that someone “wept and supplicated him.” An ancient biblical author thus demonstrably treated Israel as a sentence capable of graphic resegmentation and bound the wrestling tradition explicitly to begging. The cumulative result is an Esauic rereading of the Jabbok: Jacob is forced to state the name he concealed when he stole Esau’s blessing, becomes the one who asks or pleads before God, is bodily humbled, and at sunrise returns the blessing to the brother whose face has become the face of God.

Keywords: Jacob, Esau, Israel, Jabbok, Hosea 12, "שאל", "ברך", Edomite epigraphy, paronomasia, palaeography, blessing, supplication

1. Not an Alternative Etymology, but an Innuendo-Etymology

The argument advanced here does not require the historical derivation of "יִשְׂרָאֵל" from either "שׁאל" or "עשו אל". Neither derivation is defensible as ordinary historical morphology. Israel contains a genuine resh absent from "שׁאל"; its second consonant is historically ś, not š; and no regular sound law turns y-ś-r-ʾ-l into ʿ-ś-w-ʾ-l. The explicit explanation furnished by Genesis is transparent:

«"כִּי־שָׂרִיתָ עִם־אֱלֹהִים וְעִם־אֲנָשִׁים וַתּוּכָל"
“For you have striven with God and with humans, and you have prevailed.”»

That is the scene’s official etymology. The proposal is that it is not the scene’s only wordplay.

Biblical naming narratives frequently operate through suggestive resemblance rather than modern historical derivation. A name can be explained by one root while being surrounded by other roots that make it resonate differently. Such secondary associations need not replace the declared etymology. They can function as what may be called an innuendo-etymology: a second meaning which the text refuses to state directly but teaches its reader to hear or see.

Three levels must therefore be kept distinct:

Level| Proposal| Assessment
Explicit etymology| "ישראל" is explained through "שׂרה", “strive”| Certain
Secondary verbal echo| "ישראל" resonates with "ישאל", “he asks/requests”| Plausible and textually supported
Graphic innuendo| "ישראל" visually flickers toward "עשו אל", “Esau–El”| Speculative but contextually striking
Historical derivation| "ישראל" actually developed from "שאל" or "עשו אל"| Rejected

The case is therefore cumulative. No individual resemblance must bear the entire argument. The question is whether Genesis 32–33 has deliberately assembled name, asking, pleading, blessing, kneeling, Esau, God, faces, and restitution so densely that the proposed secondary readings become more economical than coincidence.

2. The Opponent Enters an Esau-Shaped Narrative

The nocturnal combat does not occur in narrative isolation. Everything preceding it is about Esau.

Jacob sends messengers “to Esau his brother, to the land of Seir, the field of Edom.” He learns that Esau is approaching with four hundred men, becomes terrified, divides his company, and prays specifically for deliverance “from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau.” He then sends waves of property ahead of himself in an attempt to appease Esau before seeing his face.

Most significantly, Jacob scripts in advance what Esau will do:

«"כִּי יִפְגָשְׁךָ עֵשָׂו אָחִי וּשְׁאֵלְךָ לֵאמֹר"
“When Esau my brother meets you and asks you, saying…”»

The questions concern identity, movement, and ownership:

«“Whose are you?”
“Where are you going?”
“Whose are these before you?”»

The combination is exact: "שאל" followed by "אמר"—“ask, saying.”

That formula anticipates the climax at the ford. Before the mysterious man asks Jacob’s name, the narrative has already established Esau as the expected asker of identity questions. Before Jacob asks the mysterious man’s name, the chapter has already joined "שאל" and "אמר" in a sentence explicitly assigned to Esau.

The face-language is equally concentrated. Jacob says of the gift:

«"אֲכַפְּרָה פָנָיו בַּמִּנְחָה הַהֹלֶכֶת לְפָנָי וְאַחֲרֵי־כֵן אֶרְאֶה פָנָיו אוּלַי יִשָּׂא פָנָי"»

Literally and awkwardly, because the awkwardness is the point:

«“I will cover his face with the offering going before my face, and afterward I will see his face; perhaps he will lift my face.”»

The nocturnal encounter will end with Jacob claiming to have seen God “face to face.” The following encounter will culminate in Jacob telling Esau that seeing Esau’s face was like seeing God’s face. The “man” arrives, therefore, inside a corridor already bounded by Esau’s anticipated questions and Esau’s anticipated face.

The opponent is unnamed, but his narrative space is not empty. It is Esau-shaped.

3. Silence, Then "אמר", Then "שאל"

The structure of the Jabbok scene deserves to be read almost mechanically. Verse numbering differs by one between Jewish and many Christian editions; the numbering below follows the Hebrew/Jewish arrangement represented in the cited text. The physical struggle begins in Genesis 32:25. The actual dialogue begins in verse 27.

The first two verses are nearly speechless. They consist of bodily verbs:

- Jacob is left alone.
- A man wrestles him.
- Someone sees that he cannot prevail.
- Someone touches Jacob’s thigh.
- The joint is displaced or strained.

Then speech begins, and the root "אמר", “say,” takes over:

Verse| Verbal architecture
32:27| "וַיֹּאמֶר … וַיֹּאמֶר"
32:28| "וַיֹּאמֶר … וַיֹּאמֶר"
32:29| "וַיֹּאמֶר … יֵאָמֵר"
32:30| "וַיִּשְׁאַל … וַיֹּאמֶר … וַיֹּאמֶר … תִּשְׁאַל … וַיְבָרֶךְ"

The sequence may be displayed more fully:

«Verse 27: “And he said… And he said…”
Verse 28: “And he said… And he said…”
Verse 29: “And he said… your name shall no longer be said/called Jacob…”
Verse 30: “And Jacob asked, and he said… and he said… Why do you ask concerning my name? And he blessed him there.”»

The repetition is not merely an inevitable consequence of dialogue. Verse 30 could have begun with the ordinary formula "וַיֹּאמֶר יַעֲקֹב", “and Jacob said.” Instead, the narrator first classifies the act:

«"וַיִּשְׁאַל יַעֲקֹב"»

Only then does he add:

«"וַיֹּאמֶר"»

The result is close to “Jacob asked, and said.” English normally suppresses one of the verbs because the combination feels redundant. In Hebrew, that redundancy makes the lexical choice visible. After repeated undifferentiated “saying,” the newly named figure does something more specific: he asks. The opponent immediately repeats that marked root—“Why do you ask?”—and the verse ends with the other root on which the confrontation has turned: "ברך".

The chapter has thus constructed two parallel formulas:

Anticipated Esau| Newly named Jacob
"וּשְׁאֵלְךָ לֵאמֹר"| "וַיִּשְׁאַל יַעֲקֹב וַיֹּאמֶר"
“and he will ask you, saying”| “and Jacob asked, and said”

The first concerns the identity of Jacob’s servants and property. The second concerns the identity of the mysterious opponent. Between them stands the opponent’s question concerning Jacob’s own identity.

The "אמר" saturation creates the expectation. The sudden "שאל" supplies the marked variation. The Israel-name stands precisely between them.

4. “What Is Your Name?”: The Reversal of Genesis 27

The question "מַה־שְּׁמֶךָ", “What is your name?”, is not a request for information the narrative lacks. It reopens the central crime of Jacob’s earlier story.

When Jacob entered Isaac’s room to obtain Esau’s blessing, Isaac asked:

«"מִי אַתָּה בְּנִי"
“Who are you, my son?”»

Jacob replied:

«"אָנֹכִי עֵשָׂו בְּכֹרֶךָ"
“I am Esau, your firstborn.”»

Isaac remained suspicious. He touched Jacob, observed that the voice was Jacob’s but the hands were Esau’s, and asked again:

«"אַתָּה זֶה בְּנִי עֵשָׂו"
“Are you really my son Esau?”»

Jacob answered:

«"אָנִי"
“I am.”»

He thereby received the blessing intended for Esau.

Genesis 32 restages the combination of identity interrogation and blessing, but strips Jacob of his disguise. Once again, he is in bodily contact with an unidentified figure. Once again, a blessing is at issue. Once again, he is asked who he is.

This time he says:

«"יַעֲקֹב"
“Jacob.”»

The one-word answer is a confession.

The last time Jacob was required to name himself while obtaining a blessing, he said “Esau.” This time he says “Jacob.” Only after he has admitted the identity he previously concealed is that identity replaced:

«“Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel.”»

On an Esauic reading of the opponent, the scene acquires a severe comic precision:

«“Say your name.”

“Jacob.”

“Good. This time you did not say Esau.”»

The opponent then withholds his own name. Jacob must become identifiable; the other combatant remains suspended among “man,” “angel,” “God,” and—by narrative overlay—Esau. The asymmetry is functional. Jacob’s moral problem is false identity. The opponent’s literary function depends on excess identity.

The renaming is consequently not merely a reward for athletic success. It is a judgment on Jacob’s former use of names. Before Jacob can become Israel, he must finally stop being Esau.

5. "ישאל" and "ישראל": The One-Letter Shadow

In unpointed consonantal writing, compare:

Form| Consonants| Ordinary sense
"ישאל"| y-š-ʾ-l| “he asks / will ask”
"ישראל"| y-ś-r-ʾ-l| “Israel”

The forms are not identical. Israel contains an additional resh, and the historical consonants š and ś were distinct even though both were written with the same undotted sign "ש". Nevertheless, at the graphemic level, "ישאל" becomes "ישראל" through the insertion of one letter:

«"ישאל" → "ישראל"»

That observation becomes more significant because the Northwest Semitic form is not hypothetical. Ugaritic attests the cognate root "šʾl", “ask,” including the written prefix form "yšʾal". Its documented semantic range includes asking and formally requiring a reply.

The comparison is therefore not between Israel and a form artificially manufactured for the argument. A speaker or reader of a neighboring Northwest Semitic language could encounter precisely:

«"yšʾal"
“he asks.”»

Biblical Hebrew "שאל" likewise covers more than English “ask a question.” Depending on syntax and context, it can mean:

- ask;
- inquire;
- ask for;
- request;
- demand;
- consult a deity or oracle;
- seek something from another person.

Traditional grammatical description even cites the Piel of "שאל" as an example of intensification: Qal “ask,” Piel “beg.”

The Genesis 32 forms are Qal, not Piel. The text therefore does not morphologically encode “beg” in "וַיִּשְׁאַל" or "תִּשְׁאַל". That distinction matters. Yet the pragmatic scene is already one of entreaty, and the request itself contains the particle "נָּא":

«"הַגִּידָה־נָּא שְׁמֶךָ"
“Please, tell your name.”»

Thus the text supplies the two components separately:

Component| Hebrew
ask/request| "שאל"
please| "נא"

Immediately after the name Israel is pronounced, the narrator says:

«“And Jacob asked…”»

Jacob’s first words as the man just renamed Israel are:

«“Please, tell me your name.”»

The opponent replies:

«“Why do you ask concerning my name?”»

The proposed English punchline—“Please, God!”—is not a literal dictionary gloss of Israel. It is a theatrical rendering of the scene’s pragmatic wordplay:

1. "ישאל" supplies “he asks/petitions”;
2. "נא" supplies “please”;
3. "אל" supplies “El/God”;
4. "ישראל" visually and acoustically hovers near that cluster.

A freer reconstructed echo might be:

«"יִשְׁאַל אֵל"
“He asks El.”
“He petitions God.”
“Please, God.”»

The resh prevents this from becoming an etymology. The narrative placement prevents it from being dismissed as a random dictionary game.

The joke, at maximum compression, is:

«“Your name is Please-God.”

“Say your name.”

“Jacob.”

“Now ask.”

“Please, God—tell me yours.”

“Why do you ask?”»

The scene effectively makes the newly named Israel enact his secondary name.

6. From “Bless Me” to “He "BRK"-ed Him There”

The other dominant root is "ברך".

At the beginning of the dialogue, one combatant says:

«"לֹא אֲשַׁלֵּחֲךָ כִּי אִם־בֵּרַכְתָּנִי"
“I will not release you unless you bless me.”»

At the end:

«"וַיְבָרֶךְ אֹתוֹ שָׁם"
“And he blessed him there.”»

The root therefore encloses the entire exchange:

«"בֵּרַכְתָּנִי" → name → "שאל" → "נא" → "שאל" → "וַיְבָרֶךְ"»

Both forms are Piel. Their ordinary and contextually correct meaning is bless. The final clause should not simply be translated “and he forced him to kneel there.”

Nevertheless, the consonantal complex "b-r-k" also belongs to the Hebrew vocabulary of the knee and kneeling. Under the traditional unified-root analysis, the Piel is the doubled or “intensive” D-stem of a simpler physical idea associated with bending the knee. On that traditional account, “bless” is the standard lexical meaning while “kneel intensely,” “bend,” or “bring to the knee” remains available as bodily wordplay.

The older analysis must be qualified. Contemporary semantic work argues that "ברך" “bless/praise” and the words denoting knee-bending probably derive from separate historical roots. The Semantics of Ancient Hebrew Database classifies the Piel as the declarative “bless, praise” and judges the proposed etymological connection with “bend the knee” unlikely.

This does not eliminate synchronic paronomasia. Historical unrelatedness does not prevent two identically written roots from being exploited together. English writers pun on historically unrelated homonyms constantly. The relevant question is not whether a modern etymological dictionary would merge the roots, but whether an ancient audience could hear the same three consonants while watching a contest about bodily domination, an injured hip, demanded submission, and a blessing.

Accordingly, the strongest formulation is:

«Grammatically: “and he blessed him there.”
Punningly: “and he "BRK"-ed him there.”
Bodily undertone: “and there he brought him to his knees.”»

The last line is not an alternative translation. It is the scene’s possible punchline.

Genesis immediately strengthens that possibility by doing in daylight what the night scene only suggests.

7. Seven Bows: The Body Interprets the Pun

At sunrise Jacob sees Esau approaching. Before Esau says or does anything hostile, Jacob passes ahead of his family and:

«"וַיִּשְׁתַּחוּ אַרְצָה שֶׁבַע פְּעָמִים"
“bows himself to the ground seven times.”»

The verb is not "ברך"; it is the ordinary verb of bowing or prostration. That distinction strengthens rather than weakens the argument. No strained lexical translation is required. Whatever happened in the semantic shadows of "וַיְבָרֶךְ אֹתוֹ שָׁם", the next scene shows Jacob physically lowered before Esau seven times.

The action also reverses the stolen blessing with remarkable exactness. Isaac’s blessing had declared:

«“Be lord over your brothers, and let your mother’s sons bow down to you.”»

Jacob obtained those words while pretending to be Esau.

Yet when the brothers finally meet, the mother’s son who bows is Jacob, and the brother before whom he bows is Esau.

The blessing said:

«“Your brother will bow to you.”»

The narrative answers:

«Jacob bowed to Esau—seven times.»

This is not the cancellation of Jacob’s chosen status. It is the moral correction of how he attempted to acquire it. Jacob may carry Israel’s blessing forward only after he bodily repudiates the domination clause he stole.

The movement from Genesis 32 to 33 is therefore:

1. Jacob demands a "ברכה".
2. The opponent "BRK"s him.
3. Jacob emerges limping.
4. Jacob encounters Esau.
5. Jacob lowers himself to the ground seven times.

The nocturnal “blessing” is followed by unmistakable daylight submission. The pun has grown a body.

8. The Face of Esau and the Face of God

The final verse of the wrestling episode names the location Peniel:

«"כִּי־רָאִיתִי אֱלֹהִים פָּנִים אֶל־פָּנִים"
“For I have seen God face to face.”»

Only a few lines later, Jacob tells Esau:

«"רָאִיתִי פָנֶיךָ כִּרְאֹת פְּנֵי אֱלֹהִים"
“I have seen your face as one sees the face of God.”»

The verbal correspondence is difficult to neutralize:

Night| Morning
“I have seen God face to face”| “I have seen your face as seeing the face of God”
unnamed opponent| Esau
Peniel, “face of God”| Esau’s face
survival after divine encounter| acceptance by injured brother

The second statement retrospectively interprets the first. The narrative does not necessarily assert the ontological identity “Esau is God.” It does, however, deliberately superimpose their faces. The face Jacob feared seeing becomes the face through which he understands having seen God.

This is the strongest contextual support for the proposed graphic joke "עשו אל", “Esau–El.” The visual hypothesis does not introduce an Esau/God identification into an otherwise unrelated scene. It proposes that the written name may encode, in miniature, an identification the prose immediately makes at full length:

«Seeing Esau’s face
is like seeing God’s face.»

The mysterious opponent need not be reduced to Esau alone. He can remain man, angel, God, and Esauic double simultaneously. Indeed, the refusal to provide a name protects precisely that multivalence. Jacob asks for a single name; the text declines to collapse the figure into one identity.

Jacob had falsely combined Jacob and Esau in Genesis 27:

«Jacob’s voice, Esau’s hands.»

Genesis 32–33 answers with a different composite:

«the opponent’s body, God’s presence, Esau’s face.»
