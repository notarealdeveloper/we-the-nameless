One of the more intriguing proposals in comparative Semitic philology is that חַוָּה (Ḥawwāh, Eve) and the ancient Semitic word for "serpent" ultimately descend from the same ancestral root, usually reconstructed as *ḥwy / *ḥyw (sometimes *ḥww). Hebrew itself only preserves the "life" side of the semantic field: Genesis explicitly explains Eve's name as "because she was the mother of all the living" (Gen. 3:20; cf. חָי, "living"). Other Semitic languages, however, preserve the "serpent" side: Aramaic and Syriac חויא / ܚܘܝܐ (ḥiwyā/ḥewyā), Mandaic hiwia, and probably Ugaritic 𐎈𐎅 (ḥwy) all mean "serpent," while Arabic remarkably preserves both branches in one lexical family: حيّ (ḥayy) "living," حياة (ḥayāh) "life," and حيّة (ḥayyah) "snake."

If this reconstruction is correct, an ancient Semitic audience may have heard Ḥawwāh as resonating with both life and serpent, even though biblical Hebrew itself foregrounds only the former. The etymology of חֲוִילָה (Ḥăwīlāh, Havilah) is uncertain, but if it belongs to the same root it could originally have meant something like "Land of Life" or "Land of the Serpent." Some scholars therefore see Genesis preserving an older network of related names and wordplays that has largely disappeared from Hebrew itself but remains visible through comparison with other Semitic languages.
