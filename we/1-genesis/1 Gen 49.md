I think you're putting your finger on one of the strongest tensions in the standard chronology.

The usual academic reconstruction is something like:

1. The Blessing of Jacob (Genesis 49) is very old, often dated to the early monarchy or even pre-monarchic period.

2. J is later (10th–8th century BCE depending on whom you ask).

3. The David narratives (2 Samuel) are later prose traditions.

The reason Genesis 49 is usually considered old is not because of its content but because of its form:

  

archaic poetic language,

  

tribal rather than national concerns,

  

lack of explicit Jerusalem theology,

  

apparent references to historical tribal conditions that seem earlier than the exilic period.

  

  

But the argument becomes much weaker if the poem has been edited.

The same succession logic appears again.

Your "overconstrained" analogy is actually pretty good.

  

Suppose:

  

Genesis 49 is genuinely ancient and fixed.

  

J inherited it.

  

J independently inherited stories about Reuben, Simeon, and Levi.

  

The David court history independently inherited stories about Amnon and Absalom.

  

  

Then you need three independent traditions to line up perfectly around the same succession pattern.

  

That is a lot of coincidence.

  

The standard view generally tolerates this because historians are accustomed to seeing recurring literary motifs.

  

But from a literary-compositional perspective, another possibility is much simpler:

  

A single author or redactor shaped all three layers.

  

Then the correspondences are not coincidences at all.

  

They are design.

What you're describing is close to a "Super-J" hypothesis.

  

Under such a model:

  

Genesis 49 is not an untouched archaic relic.

  

J either composed it, expanded it, or heavily revised it.

  

J deliberately creates a succession pattern:

  

Firstborn disqualified by sexual sin.

  

Second tier disqualified by violence.

  

Judah receives rule.

  

  

  

Then when J reaches the monarchy:

  

Amnon repeats Reuben.

  

Absalom repeats Simeon/Levi.

  

Solomon repeats Judah.

  

  

The entire national history becomes a recapitulation of Jacob's family history.

  

Jacob → David.

  

Judah → Solomon.

  

Israel's founding family becomes a template for Israel's royal family.

What's especially interesting is that this isn't merely a parallel of characters.

  

It's a parallel of succession mechanics.

  

The same sequence occurs:

  

1. Natural heir removed through sexual misconduct.

  

  

2. Rival removed through bloodshed.

  

  

3. Chosen heir inherits.

  

  

  

Those are much rarer than generic literary similarities.

Where I would hesitate is the claim that there are "zero solutions" besides J editing Genesis 49.

  

There is another possibility:

  

Genesis 49 could genuinely be old, but later scribes modified only a few crucial lines.

  

A very small amount of editing could create the succession logic you observe.

  

In other words, the alternatives are not just:

  

ancient untouched poem, or

  

wholly composed by J.

  

  

There is a large middle ground:

  

ancient core,

  

J-shaped recension.

  

  

Personally, if one accepts extensive literary architecture in the Pentateuch and Former Prophets, the "J-shaped recension" explanation feels substantially more economical than assuming all these succession patterns arose independently.

  

The strongest point in your argument isn't the Reuben-Bilhah connection or the Simeon/Levi-Shechem connection individually. Those are often explained as prose stories created to explain difficult poetic lines. The stronger point is the second repetition in the David narrative. Once the same succession algorithm appears in Jacob's sons and David's sons, the possibility of deliberate macro-structure becomes much harder to dismiss.

  

In that sense, your Jacob → David and Judah → Solomon correspondence is not just a character analogy; it's a proposal that the entire royal succession narrative was written as a historical-scale replay of Genesis 49's succession logic. That's exactly the sort of large-scale literary architecture that source critics have traditionally been reluctant to posit, but it is also the kind of pattern that, once seen, is difficult to unsee.
