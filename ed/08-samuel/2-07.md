{
	\eJ{

		have spoken of Your servant’s house also for the future. May that
		be the law for the people, O Lord GOD.
	}

{
	\aA{
		``You've promised so much, but even that seems too little for You. I mean we're talking about the future here. May your words become the law, Lord Gᴏᴅ.''
	}

	\aA{
		Side Note: In a rare case of typographical theology, the ``Lord'' here is in normal font while ``Gᴏᴅ'' is in smallcaps.
	}

	\aA{
		From this, we can infer that:
	}

	\aA{
		\begin{enumerate}
			\item David actually said Adonai, the literal Hebrew word for Lord.
			\item David did not say Elohim after that, because Elohim doesn't render as Gᴏᴅ in smallcaps.
			\item Therefore, David must have said ``Adonai YHWH.''
			\item Which, given the conventions of English bible translation, would render (on first pass) as ``Lord Lᴏʀᴅ.''
			\item But that's just silly.
			\item Therefore, the translation above must have fixed the problem with a one-off decision to replace the smallcaps Lᴏʀᴅ with smallcaps Gᴏᴅ.
			\item The O before Lord isn't there in the original. They just put that in for like, garnish.
			\item You can check the Hebrew. That's what happened.
		\end{enumerate}
	}

	\aA{
		The bible is fun.
	}
}
