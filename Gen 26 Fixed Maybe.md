- **Basemath:** “Perfume Girl” works in Hebrew, but *b-ś-m* fragrance vocabulary is broader Northwest Semitic. This looks much more like ordinary regional naming than a Hebrew translation of a foreign name.

- **Ishmael:** textbook West Semitic sentence-name: **“El hears.”** The important comparative point is that *El* is not an exclusively Israelite name for deity; it belongs to the common Northwest Semitic religious lexicon. So the structure could feel at home from Canaan to Transjordan.

- **Nebaioth:** this is where Arabian evidence matters. Assyrian inscriptions know a closely corresponding northern Arabian group, commonly appearing alongside **Qedar/Kedar**. - Also, equating Nebaioth automatically with the later **Nabataeans** is too easy: the consonantal correspondence is not perfectly straightforward, and older reference works already flagged the problem. citeturn13search8

| Alvah | עַלְוָה (*ʿAlwāh*) | Older derivation associates it with עלה/height, perhaps “loftiness.” BDB essentially records it as an Edomite proper name rather than confidently translating it. |
| Jetheth | יְתֵת (*Yəṯêṯ*) | Derivation explicitly uncertain in the major lexica; older glosses such as “nail” are not secure. |

\aB{These are Esau's chiefs by family and place: Chief Restraint, Chief Loftiness, Chief Who-Knows.}

%%
- **Alvah:** “Loftiness” is attractive because ʿ-l-w height vocabulary has excellent wider Semitic, especially Arabic, parallels. So the comparative reading may actually strengthen the general **“high/elevated”** semantic neighborhood while weakening any claim that it is specifically Hebrew.
- Does **“Chief Loftiness”** invite an anti-Edom pride joke, especially when later prophets hammer Edom for its height and arrogance? Yeah, it can. But that is strongest as **later intertextual reception**, not proof Genesis 36 coined the name as satire.
- **Jetheth:** this one is a hard no on false confidence. BDB/Strong call the derivation uncertain. The internet gloss “a nail” floats around, but the lexical derivation is not established. citeturn15search1turn15search7
- Source-critical footnote: older BDB notation actually labels v.40 **P**, one reason I would not premise the joke analysis on a firm “this is J” assignment. citeturn15search5

**Genesis 36:41**

> ⁴¹ chief Aholibamah, chief Elah, chief Pinon,

| Proper name | Hebrew | Possible Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aholibamah | אָהֳלִיבָמָה | “Tent of the high place.” |
| Elah | אֵלָה (*ʾÊlāh*) | “Terebinth” (a tree-name). |
| Pinon | פִּינֹן (*Pînōn*) | BDB gives no secure etymology and compares Punon. |

\aB{Chief My Tabernacle is Exalted, Chief Terebinth, Chief Who-Knows.}

%%
- **Aholibamah:** “Tent of the High Place” is one of the most overtly cultic-sounding compounds in the whole chapter. If it originated as a clan/territorial designation, that is arguably more interesting than treating it as an individual's quirky given name.
- **Elah = Terebinth:** tree/chieftain names are completely at home in old Northwest Semitic culture. Nearby *Elon* belongs to the same broad tree-name sphere.
- **Pinon:** don't fake a meaning. The best lead is its possible connection with **Punon**, a known southern toponym. That suggests a place/clan relation rather than a lost Hebrew “definition.” citeturn14search7
- Joke-wise, **Aholibamah** gives the most material. To an anti-high-place Israelite reader, “Chief Tent-of-the-High-Place” can sound almost too on-the-nose. But it may be a genuine cultic Edomite name, which makes the resonance more historically interesting, not less.

**Genesis 36:42**

> ⁴² chief Kenaz, chief Teman, chief Mibzar,

| Proper name | Hebrew | Possible Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kenaz | קְנַז | No secure Hebrew derivation. |
| Teman | תֵּימָן | “South/right-hand.” |
| Mibzar | מִבְצָר (*Miḇṣār*) | Identical in form with מִבְצָר, “fortress, fortified place, stronghold,” from בצר. |

\aB{Chief Who-Knows, Chief Southpaw, Chief Fortress.}

%%
- **Mibzar = Fortress/Stronghold** is the standout. That is transparent enough that a Hebrew listener basically hears **Chief Fortress**. citeturn14search3
- Now the anti-Edom literary angle gets genuinely interesting: later prophetic rhetoric repeatedly attacks Edom's trust in inaccessible heights and strongholds. So **“Chief Fortress”** can become beautifully ironic in the wider biblical anti-Edom conversation.
- But chronology matters: that does not prove Genesis's list was written to set up Obadiah's joke. The safer statement is that **later Israelite readers had an obvious opportunity to re-hear Mibzar ironically**.
- **Teman** likewise becomes a major prophetic symbol of Edom. The original name just means southern/right-side territory in common Semitic geography; subsequent biblical literature loads it with Edomite identity.
- **Kenaz** stays stubbornly opaque—and that is healthy. Not every Edomite chief needs a punchline.

**Genesis 36:43**

> ⁴³ chief Magdiel, chief Iram.
>
> These are the chiefs of Edom by their homes in the land of their possession. That is Esau, father of Edom.

| Proper name | Hebrew | Possible Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magdiel | מַגְדִּיאֵל (*Magdîʾēl*) | Usually understood with גדל/מגד + אל, something like “El is greatness,” “excellence of El,” or “El makes great.” |
| Iram | עִירָם (*ʿÎrām*) | Sometimes associated with עִיר, “city,” but BDB gives no secure derivation. |
| Edom | אֱדוֹם | “Red.” |
| Esau | עֵשָׂו | Possible “made/formed/rough” association; historically uncertain. |

\aB{Chief God-Makes-Great, Chief Who-Knows. Those are Redland's chiefs. That's Esau, father of Red.}

%%
- **Magdiel is one of the strongest comparative names in the finale.** BDB notes a **Sabaean parallel** to the compound. That is a big deal: it pushes the name beyond “nice Hebrew El-name” into a wider southern Semitic onomastic environment. citeturn10search10
- So something like **“El makes great / Greatness of El”** is probably the right semantic neighborhood, but calling it specifically Hebrew would miss the point.
- **Iram:** “City-man” or “their city” style derivations are possible Hebrew re-hearings, but the lexical evidence is too thin for confidence.
- As satire, the ending is surprisingly **grand rather than belittling**: *Magdiel* sounds elevated, El-theophoric, impressive. That weighs against a theory that vv.40–43 are simply a string of insulting Hebrew nicknames.
- The final **Edom / Esau** equation returns us to the chapter's master pun: after all the exotic names, the narrator closes by bringing the nation back under the familiar Israelite semantic label **“Red.”**
