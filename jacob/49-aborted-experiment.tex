% Font policy for the Blessing of Jacob:
% - the full v. 1 surface block stays as in 49-orig.tex; the dissected v. 1a
%   keeps the ordinary J hand because it is prose framing.
% - the inherited/reconstructed poem uses one readable generic Paleo-Hebrew
%   display hand throughout. It marks genre, not a particular scribe, city, or
%   century. Proto-Sinaitic would push the whole collection much too early;
%   rotating Gezer/Siloam/Tel-Zayit hands would fake line-by-line precision.
% - the prose conclusion returns to the ordinary R/P source fonts.
%
% paleo-hebrew-regular.ttf is PaleoZ in master.tex. It is the most readable of
% the new samples and looks like a coherent hand rather than a museum label.
\providecommand{\hPaleoZ}[1]{\PaleoZFont{\color{PaleoColor}\mdseries}{#1}}
\providecommand{\ePaleoZ}[1]{%
	\ApplyEnglishSourceProfile{\EnglishProtoFont\color{PaleoColor}\mdseries}{#1}%
}

% Witness strips live inside the Hebrew column. They are deliberately square
% Hebrew or Greek: the reader should be able to see when the reconstructed
% Paleo-Hebrew line is not what any surviving manuscript literally says.
\providecommand{\GenXLIXWitness}[2]{%
	\par\smallskip
	\begin{LTR}
		{\EnglishFont\scriptsize\color{Graycolor}\textsc{#1}\enspace #2\par}%
	\end{LTR}%
}
\providecommand{\GenXLIXMT}[1]{\GenXLIXWitness{MT}{\heb{#1}}}
\providecommand{\GenXLIXSP}[1]{\GenXLIXWitness{SP}{\heb{#1}}}
\providecommand{\GenXLIXLXX}[1]{\GenXLIXWitness{LXX}{#1}}

\Chapter{49}

\Verse{1}
{
	\hJ{וַיִּקְרָא יַעֲקֹב אֶל בָּנָיו וַיֹּאמֶר}
	\hJ{הֵאָסְפוּ וְאַגִּידָה לָכֶם אֵת אֲשֶׁר יִקְרָא אֶתְכֶם בְּאַחֲרִית הַיָּמִים׃}
}
{
	\eJ{And Jacob called to his sons and said:}
	\eJ{Gather, and I’ll tell you what will happen to you in the future days.}
}
{
	Strap in. Genesis has reached its final boss.

	This is not one clean poem from one clean moment. It is a pile of tribal
	sayings, family stories, regional words, royal politics, old spellings, and
	later repairs, all placed in Jacob’s mouth for his last speech.

	\aB{
		One crucial rule for the ride: the Leningrad Codex does \emph{not} have
		literal white holes in Genesis 49. Its Hebrew runs straight through. The
		blanks below mean, ``The surviving words are too mangled to reverse
		confidently,'' not, ``The manuscript left this space empty.'' \textsc{MT}
		means the old consonantal text as preserved in the medieval Masoretic
		tradition together with its later pointing and reading; \textsc{SP} the
		Samaritan Pentateuch; \textsc{LXX} the old Greek translation. The large
		Paleo-Hebrew line is the proposed reconstruction; the small square-Hebrew
		or Greek strips are surviving witnesses. Angle brackets supply letters;
		empty brackets delete them; a long blank refuses to guess. Greek may
		preserve an older Hebrew reading, or merely an ancient translator taking
		his own swing.
	}

	Only scraps of the chapter survive at Qumran: 4QGen-Exod\textit{a} reaches
	parts of vv. 1--5 and 4QGen\textit{e} parts of vv. 6--8. They are broadly
	MT-like. After v. 8, there is no Dead Sea Scroll waiting to solve the poem for
	us.

	\aC{
		The safest picture is an anthology of tribal material with more than one
		history, not one pristine poem dateable line by line from grammar alone.
		Some oddities may be archaic; others are poetic, northern/Israelian, or
		simply corrupt.
	}

	Politically, the chapter splits the firstborn package three ways: Reuben
	loses first place, Judah gets rule, and Joseph gets the double share and the
	absurd fertility blessing. 1 Chr. 5:1--2 later says nearly that in prose.
}

\Verse{1a}
{
	\hJ{וַיִּקְרָא יַעֲקֹב אֶל בָּנָיו וַיֹּאמֶר}
}
{
	\eJ{And Jacob called to his sons and said:}
}
{
	This little prose frame is healthy. MT, LXX, and the Qumran scraps give no
	reason to replace it.

	\aB{One clean line. Cherish it. The surgery starts after ``said.''}

	The frame need not be the same age as the poem. An editor can put inherited
	tribal material into Jacob’s deathbed speech without telling us when each
	saying began.
}

\Verse{1b}
{
	\hPaleoZ{האסף ואגד לכם [ ]}
	\GenXLIXMT{האספו ואגידה לכם את אשר יקרא אתכם באחרית הימים}
	\GenXLIXLXX{τί ἀπαντήσει ὑμῖν ἐπ᾽ ἐσχάτων τῶν ἡμερῶν}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{Gather, and let me tell you---}
}
{
	The empty brackets delete MT’s ``what will happen to you in future days.''
	That whole tail is also in the LXX. Nothing has been recovered from Greek;
	this is a metrical and literary guess that a shorter summons came first.

	\aB{In other words: the manuscript kept the words. We are the ones holding the knife.}

	``Future days'' need not mean the end of the world; it can mean what happens
	down the road. The full frame also makes a sound joke: Jacob \emph{calls}
	(q-r-ʾ) his sons to say what will be\emph{fall} them (q-r-ʾ).
}

\Verse{2}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		הקבצו [ ] בני יעקב

		ושמע אל ישראל אבכם
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{הקבצו ושמעו בני יעקב ושמעו אל ישראל אביכם}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Gather, sons of Jacob;

		listen to Israel, your father.
	}
}
{
	MT says ``listen'' twice. The reconstruction deletes the first one as a
	copying echo from the next line, producing three short beats: gather / sons
	of Jacob / listen to Israel. Every major ancient version also has both
	``listens,'' so the deletion is conjecture, not a variant handed to us.

	``Jacob'' and ``Israel'' let one dying father become the whole nation. From
	here on, sons are tribes and family gossip is political history. Deut. 33 and
	Judg. 5 are the closest companion tribal poems.
}

\Verse{3}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		ראובן בכר

		את כח

		[ו־] ראשית אן

		[ ] יתר עז
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{ראובן בכרי אתה כחי וראשית אוני יתר שאת ויתר עז}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Reuben, my firstborn;

		you are my strength,

		the first of my manhood,

		the best of my might.
	}
}
{
	Reuben is Leah’s firstborn. His traditional territory lies east of the Dead
	Sea, south of Gad and north of Moab.

	The verse inventories everything he should inherit before v. 4 cancels it.
	Compare Deut. 21:17, ``the first of his strength,'' and 1 Chr. 5:1--2, where
	Reuben loses the birthright, Joseph gets the double share, and Judah gets
	rule.

	\aB{
		``The first of my vigor'' is politely reproductive. Reuben is the first
		thing Jacob’s dad-making equipment successfully made.
	}

	\aC{
		The neat four-line rhyme is reconstructed: it deletes the difficult
		\heb{יתר שאת} and reads an unwritten final \emph{-i} in the last word so
		each colon ends ``my ---.'' No witness preserves exactly this cleaned text.
	}
}

\Verse{4}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		\_\_\_\_\_\_

		\_\_\_\_\_\_

		כ עלת <על> משכבי [ ]

		אז חללת יצעי <אבך>
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{פחז כמים אל תותר}
	\GenXLIXLXX{ἐξύβρισας ὡς ὕδωρ, μὴ ἐκζέσῃς}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		\ldots\ldots\ldots

		\ldots\ldots\ldots

		For you mounted my bed;

		yes, you profaned your father’s couch.
	}
}
{
	Reuben lost first place when he slept with Bilhah, Jacob’s concubine
	(Gen. 35:22; 1 Chr. 5:1).

	The first two lines are present in MT, but \heb{פחז} is a one-off word and
	the rest never settles into a secure sentence. The LXX already offers a
	different try: roughly, ``You ran wild like water; do not boil over.'' The
	blanks admit that neither witness gives us the lost wording.

	The bed half is easier: supply the normal preposition ``onto,'' read ``my
	bed'' as singular, and move ``your father'' beside ``couch.'' 1 Chr. 5:1
	helps with that rearrangement.

	\aB{
		The sex is blunt: you \emph{went up}, you \emph{mounted}, you defiled
		the bed. In royal politics, taking Dad’s or the king’s women can also claim
		his place: Absalom does it to David (2 Sam. 16:21--22); Abner and
		Adonijah are accused through the same logic (2 Sam. 3:7--8; 1 Kgs. 2).
		The water may carry a fluid/ejaculation wink, but the mounting language is
		the part we can actually defend.
	}

	\aC{The first bicolon is unintelligible as it stands; the versions were already translating a damaged text.}
}

\Verse{5}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		שמעון ולו אחים

		כל חמס מכרתם [ ]
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{שמעון ולוי אחים כלי חמס מכרתיהם}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Simeon and Levi are brothers;

		violence is their stock in trade.
	}
}
{
	Simeon and Levi are Leah’s full sons and Dinah’s full brothers. Gen. 34 is
	the backstory: they turn the Shechemites’ circumcision into a town-wide stun
	grenade and then murder the men. Simeon later survives as towns embedded
	inside Judah; Levi receives scattered cities rather than one territory.

	Everything hangs on \heb{מכרתיהם}, a word used nowhere else. It may mean
	weapons, merchandise/stock-in-trade, plans, or cutting instruments. The LXX
	does not preserve a clean equivalent.

	\aB{
		If it means ``cutting tools,'' the verse points straight at the
		circumcision knives: genital surgery was both the con and the weapon. Very
		nasty. Very possible. Still not certain.
	}
}

\Verse{6}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		בסדם אל תבא נפש

		בקהלם אל תחד כבדי

		[ ] באפם הרג אש

		[ו־] ברצנם עקר שר
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{בסדם אל תבא נפשי בקהלם אל תחד כבודי}
	\GenXLIXLXX{μὴ ἐρίσαιτο τὰ ἥπατά μου}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Into their council, do not come, my soul;

		in their assembly, do not join, my liver.

		In anger they killed a man;

		by choice they hamstrung a bull.
	}
}
{
	Jacob refuses ownership of the heroic version of Gen. 34: leave my whole
	self out of their meeting, their plan, and their song.

	MT is normally read ``my glory,'' but the LXX has ``my liver.'' In old
	body-language the liver could be the seat of emotion, so ``my soul / my
	liver'' is a perfectly normal pair. Qumran preserves only scraps here and
	does not settle it.

	Gen. 34 never says they crippled cattle. That may be another memory of the
	sack, a generic war image, or damaged wording. The David story later stages
	the same political distancing: David condemns Joab and Abishai, the violent
	``sons of Zeruiah,'' after Abner’s murder (2 Sam. 3:28--39).

	\aB{
		There may even be a broken animal joke: \heb{שור}, ``bull,'' beside
		Hamor, ``donkey,'' the name of Shechem’s father. Notice it; do not build a
		house on it.
	}
}

\Verse{7}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		ארר אפם כי עז

		ועברתם כי קשת

		אחלקם ביעקב

		אפצם בישראל
	}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Cursed is their fierce anger,

		and their hard fury.

		I’ll divide them in Jacob;

		I’ll scatter them in Israel.
	}
}
{
	This one is mostly healthy. The reconstruction chiefly removes later vowel
	letters to show how the same poem might look in older spelling; 4QGen\textit{e}
	is broadly compatible with MT.

	The curse explains two later maps at once: Simeon is divided inside Judah;
	Levi is scattered through Israel. Levi’s punishment can then be rewritten as
	a priestly privilege in Deut. 33.

	\aB{Verse 28 will call all this a ``blessing.'' Here that means a father’s binding fate, not a compliment.}
}

\Verse{8}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		יהד <הד> את

		ידך בערף איבך

		יהד ידך אחך

		ישתחו לך בני אבך
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{יהודה אתה יודוך אחיך ידך בערף איביך ישתחוו לך בני אביך}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Judah, my splendor, you;

		your hand is on your enemies’ neck.

		Judah, your brothers praise you;

		your father’s sons bow to you.
	}
}
{
	Judah is Leah’s fourth son; his traditional territory is the southern hill
	country, with Benjamin north, Philistia west, and Edom south.

	\aB{
		The three older brothers have just been disqualified. Now number four gets
		praise, victory, brothers bowing, and soon a permanent staff between his
		legs. Come on: this section reads like a Davidic succession brief.
	}

	The name pun is explicit: \emph{Yehudah}, your brothers will
	\emph{yodukha}, ``praise you'' (Gen. 29:35). More politically, Joseph’s
	dream image---the brothers bowing---is transferred to Judah. Gen. 43--44
	already turns Judah into spokesman and substitute; Judg. 1:1--2 and 2 Sam.
	5:1--3 give the tribal and royal payoff.

	\aC{
		``My splendor'' (\heb{הודי}) is a conjectural restoration based on short,
		similar-looking old spellings and meter. The versions help rearrange the
		lines, but no surviving manuscript actually contains that restored word.
	}
}

\Verse{9}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		גר ארי יהד

		מטרף בני עלת

		כרע רבץ כארי

		[ו־] כלבא מי יקמנו
	}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Judah is a lion cub;

		from the prey, my son, you rose.

		He crouches and lies like a lion,

		like a lioness---who dares wake him?
	}
}
{
	There is little textual surgery here. Judah has already eaten and can lie
	down because nobody dares disturb him: conquest followed by secure rule,
	not merely generic animal violence. Num. 24:9 repeats the crouching lion and
	``who will rouse him?''

	``From the prey, my son, you rose'' may glance back at Judah pulling Joseph
	away from immediate murder in Gen. 37. It is attractive, not demonstrable.
}

\Verse{10}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		לא יסר שפט מיהד

		ומחקק מבין דגלו

		\_\_\_\_\_\_

		\_\_\_\_\_\_
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{לא יסור שבט מיהודה ומחקק מבין רגליו עד כי יבא שילה ולו יקהת עמים}
	\GenXLIXSP{לא יסור שבט מיהודה ומחקק מבין דגליו}
	\GenXLIXLXX{οὐκ ἐκλείψει ἄρχων ἐξ Ιουδα καὶ ἡγούμενος ἐκ τῶν μηρῶν αὐτοῦ \ldots{}}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		A judge will not fail from Judah,

		nor a commander from among his standards.

		\ldots\ldots\ldots

		\ldots\ldots\ldots
	}
}
{
	MT gives the famous ``scepter from Judah / ruler’s staff from between his
	legs.'' This reconstruction changes \heb{שבט}, ``staff/scepter,'' to
	\heb{שפט}, ``judge,'' and follows SP’s ``his banners'' instead of MT’s
	``his legs.'' Old \emph{bet} and \emph{pe} were easy to confuse, and the LXX
	has ``ruler,'' so the first change is plausible. SP supplies the second.

	\aB{
		But MT’s version absolutely leaves the staff in Judah’s crotch. A seated
		ruler can hold a scepter there; ``feet'' can also cover the genitals; and
		the thing between his legs produces the ruling line. Political staff first,
		dynastic dick second. SP’s banners are precisely the reading that makes the
		penis disappear. The LXX, however, still has the ruler coming ``from his
		thighs,'' so the body reading is ancient, not a trick created by medieval
		vowel points.
	}

	Genesis turns up the voltage: in Gen. 38 Judah gives Tamar a different
	word for ``staff'' during the sex that produces Perez, ancestor of David.
	Staff + sex + descendants + Judahite rule is therefore a real cluster even
	without an exact lexical pun.

	The final two lines are worse. ``Shiloh'' may be a place, a title, ``until he
	comes,'' ``until what belongs to him comes,'' or part of a tribute formula;
	\heb{יקהת} may be obedience, gathering, or allegiance. Ezek. 21:32 already
	looks like one ancient interpretation: ``until the one whose right it is
	comes.''

	\aC{No satisfactory solution of the problems in this bicolon has ever been presented.}
}

\Verse{11}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		אסר לגפנו ערו

		[ו־] לשרק בני אתנו

		כבס בין לבש

		[ו־] בדם ענבים סות
	}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		He ties his ass to a vine,

		his she-ass’s colt to a choice vine;

		he washes his clothes in wine,

		his robe in grape-blood.
	}
}
{
	The joke is economic insanity. A donkey tied to a good vine can eat or ruin
	it; Judah has so many vines that they are hitching posts. Wine is so cheap
	that it replaces wash water. Compare Gen. 27:28 and Amos 9:13.

	\aB{Grapes are weeds. Wine is laundry water. That is the whole ridiculous blessing.}

	\aC{
		An ingenious Ugaritic re-reading makes the first pair ``bridle'' and
		``trappings,'' but no Hebrew witness supplies it and the LXX plainly reads
		vineyard language. The rare \heb{סותה}, ``robe,'' does have good
		Phoenician support.
	}
}

\Verse{12}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		חכלל ענם מיין

		לבן שנם מחלב
	}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		His eyes are darker than wine;

		his teeth are whiter than milk.
	}
}
{
	\heb{חכלילי} occurs nowhere else. It may mean dark, red, gleaming, or
	bloodshot, and the comparison can be ``darker than wine'' or ``dark from
	wine.''

	\aB{So Judah is rich, hot, healthy, and possibly hammered.}

	The wine-red / milk-white face is unusually sensual, but there is no secure
	genital joke hiding in this one.
}

		at its inlets he camps.

		\ldots\ldots\ldots

		\ldots\ldots\ldots
	}

{
	Zebulun is Leah’s son. Joshua places him inland in Lower Galilee, between

	The map problem may be trade access or a road into Phoenician commercial
	space rather than a Joshua-style coastline. Deut. 33 again gives Zebulun the
	wealth of the seas.

\Verse{14}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		יששכר חמר \_\_\_

		רבץ בין [־] משפתם
	}
	\GenXLIXMT{יששכר חמר גרם רבץ בין המשפתים}
	\GenXLIXLXX{Ἰσσαχαρ τὸ καλὸν ἐπεθύμησεν}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Issachar is an ass \ldots

		crouching between the \ldots
	}
}
{
	Issachar is Leah’s son; his traditional land is the fertile Jezreel Valley,
	between Manasseh, Zebulun, Naphtali, and the Jordan.

	``Ass'' is clear and need not insult him: donkeys are strong, valuable work
	animals. The next words are the wreck. \heb{גרם} may mean bony,
	strong-boned, sinewy, or something else; \heb{משפתים} has been rendered
	saddlebags, sheepfolds, boundary ridges, hearths, or rubbish heaps. The LXX
	appears to have given up on the donkey too.

	\aB{Strong ass? Bony ass? Sinewy ass? Castrated ass? We have a donkey and vibes.}

	\heb{גרם} is a useful northern/Israelian word, not automatic proof that the
	line predates J. Issachar’s name already comes from Leah hiring Jacob for a
	night in Gen. 30; v. 15 finishes the joke.
}

\Verse{15}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		ירא מנחה כי טוב

		יבט ארצו כי נעם

		יט שכמו לסבל

		ויה למס עבד
	}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		He sees a good resting place;

		he looks on a pleasant land.

		He bends his shoulder to the burden

		and becomes a corvée slave.
	}
}
{
	This is not ``Issachar has a great work ethic.'' \heb{מס עבד} is levy labor,
	corvée: good farmland bought at the price of political subjection. Compare
	the subjected populations in Judg. 1 and the labor state in 1 Kgs.

	The extra ``he looks'' is a conjectural restoration to balance ``he sees'';
	the possessive readings ``his rest / his land'' receive some versional
	support. The basic land--shoulder--forced-labor sequence is secure.

	\aB{Mom rented Dad for the night; the child becomes rented labor. Genesis spent fifteen chapters setting up that joke.}

	The word for ``pleasant'' is another likely northern/Israelian feature.
}

\Verse{16}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		דן ידן <דין> עם

		כאחד שבט ישראל
	}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Dan pleads his people’s case,

		as one of Israel’s tribes.
	}
}
{
	Dan is Bilhah’s son and Naphtali’s full brother. His first allotment lies
	west of Benjamin and Judah under Philistine pressure; Judg. 18 moves the
	tribe to Laish in the far north.

	The pun flashes in neon: \emph{Dan yadin}, ``Dan will judge.'' Gen. 30:6
	already explains his name by judgment, and the famous Danite Samson becomes
	a judge.

	The inserted matching noun \heb{דין}, ``case,'' gives an old Semitic
	``judge the case'' idiom and better meter, but no manuscript has it. MT’s
	shorter ``Dan will judge his people'' already makes sense.

	``As one of Israel’s tribes'' may defensively insist that displaced,
	shrine-haunted Dan still counts. That is history inferred from the line, not
	grammar proving it.
}

\Verse{17}
{
	\hPaleoZ{

		שפפן על ארח

		הנשך עקבי סס

		ויפל רכב אחר
	}

{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Dan is a snake on the road,

		a venomous snake on the path,

		biting a horse’s heels

		so its rider falls backward.
	}
}
{
	The animal is a military tactic. Tiny Dan does not beat horse and rider
	head-on; he hits the vulnerable heel and lets the larger system crash.
	Samson’s sabotage-and-ambush war against the Philistines fits perfectly.

	Snake + heel recalls Gen. 3:15, while ``heel'' also echoes Jacob the
	heel-grabber. The family has produced a heel-biting tribe.

	The opening blank deletes MT’s \heb{יהי}, ``let Dan be.'' The LXX retains
	it. Again, this is a meter-based deletion, not lost text recovered elsewhere.

\Verse{18}
{
	\hPaleoZ{לישעתך קויתי יהוה}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{For your rescue I wait, YHWH.}
}
{
	\aB{Mid-snake poem, Jacob looks up and says: God, please save us.}

	This is the only explicit YHWH-line inside the tribal poem. Its abrupt
	first-person prayer may be a liturgical seam or midpoint insertion.

	But all the major ancient witnesses contain it. The previous draft deleted
	v. 18 as literary history; no manuscript actually supplies a Blessing of
	Jacob without it. So it stays, visibly separate and honestly labeled.

\Verse{19}
{

		והא יגד עקבם
	}

	\GenXLIXWitness{RESEGMENT}{\heb{והוא יגד עקבם | אשר שמן לחמו}}

{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Gad is a raiding band;

		he raids from the rear.
	}
}
{
	Gad is Zilpah’s son and Asher’s full brother. His traditional territory is
	Gilead east of the Jordan, exposed between Reuben, Manasseh, Ammon, and

	The Hebrew is a tongue-twister: \emph{Gad / gedud / yagud}---Gad, gang,

	tribe, ``\emph{from} Asher.'' LXX, Vulgate, and Peshitta begin simply
	``Asher,'' supporting movement of that one letter backward: ``at their
	heel/from the rear.'' Nothing huge vanished; one consonant probably crossed
	a verse boundary.

		his land is fertile;

\Verse{25}
{
	\hPaleoZ{
		מאל אבך ויעזרך

		ואל־שדי ויברכך

		ברכת שמם מעל

		ברכת תהם [רבצת] מתחת

	}
	\GenXLIXMT{ואת שדי}
	\GenXLIXSP{ואל שדי}
	\GenXLIXLXX{ὁ θεὸς ὁ ἐμός}
}
{
	\ePaleoZ{
		From your father’s God, who helps you,

		and El-Shaddai, who blesses you:

		blessings of heaven above,

		blessings of the Deep beneath,

		blessings of breasts and womb.
	}
}
{
	The first repair has actual witness support. MT’s awkward ``and Shaddai'' is
	``and El-Shaddai'' in three Hebrew manuscripts, SP, and Peshitta; LXX instead

	The Deep line also moved in transmission. MT has ``the Deep crouching
	beneath''; this reconstruction drops ``crouching'' as an overlong
	harmonization toward Deut. 33, while the LXX gives ``earth containing
	everything.'' Genesis 49 and Deut. 33 preserve related Joseph blessings, not
	two photocopies of one fixed master.

	The blessing runs vertically through the fertility machine: rain from heaven,
	groundwater from the Deep, milk from breasts, children from womb.

	\aB{

		``breasts,'' and then womb. Shaddai gives shadayim. Boob-God gives boob
		blessings. That does not prove the divine name’s etymology; it does prove
		that Hebrew ears could hear the rhyme.

\Verse{26}
{

	}

	\GenXLIXLXX{εὐλογίας πατρός σου καὶ μητρός σου}

{
	\ePaleoZ{
		Blessings of father and mother,

		man and child;

		on the brow of the brother set apart.
	}

{
	MT’s opening is mashed together. SP and LXX preserve the clear pair
	``father and mother''; the reconstruction suspects that MT fused it with

	MT’s ``my progenitors/parents'' is then read ``mountains,'' helped by Hab.
	3:6 and the almost parallel Joseph blessing in Deut. 33:15--16. LXX also

	crown-on-Joseph’s-head image is much safer.

	separated from the others who becomes distinguished above them. Gen. 48

	both become Jacob’s tribal sons.
