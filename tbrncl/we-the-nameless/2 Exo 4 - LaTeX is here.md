Dingir (𒀭) is a Sumerian cuneiform sign representing "god," "goddess," "sky," or "heaven". It served as a divine determinative in Mesopotamian writing to indicate a deity's name, often transliterated as a superscript '\({}^{d}\)'. The symbol originated as an ideogram for "sky" (\(an\)) and is often associated with the god of heaven, Any.

The Hebrew name:

\[
\texthebrew{סִינַי}
\qquad

\[
\texthebrew{סיני}

\[
\textit{Sîn}

\boxed{
\textit{Sinai}

\text{Mountain of the Moon-God}

\[
\textit{Sîn}

\begin{itemize}
    \item the moon,
    \item cyclical time,

    \item illumination at night,

    \item divine revelation.
\end{itemize}

Major centers of his worship included:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ur,
    \item Harran.
\end{itemize}

## Definition of Jerusalem

\[
\textit{Šaḥar}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{Foundation of Shalim}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{City of Shalim}
}

\[

    \item lay a foundation.
\end{itemize}

\approx
\text{Foundation of Shalim}
\]

\[
\text{Settlement dedicated to Shalim}
\]

\bigskip

\section*{Connection with \textit{Šalim}}

In Ugaritic mythology:

\[
\textit{Šalim}
\]

is one of the twin ``Gentle Gods'':

\begin{itemize}
    \item Šaḥar = Dawn
    \item Šalim = Dusk
\end{itemize}

born through the sexual and generative acts of Ilu.

Šalim is associated with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item evening,
    \item completion,
    \item fullness,
    \item wholeness,
    \item transition into night.
\end{itemize}

This overlaps strongly with the broader Semitic root:

\[
\texthebrew{שלם}
\]

meaning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item complete,
    \item whole,
    \item intact,
    \item at peace,
    \item fulfilled.
\end{itemize}

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Šalim}
\leftrightarrow
\text{completion}
\leftrightarrow
\text{peace}
\leftrightarrow
\text{wholeness}
}
\]

\bigskip

\section*{Jerusalem and Peace}

Later Hebrew speakers naturally associated:

\[
\texthebrew{ירושלים}
\]

with:

\[
\texthebrew{שלום}
\qquad
(\textit{shalom})
\]

Thus Jerusalem became interpreted popularly as:

\begin{quote}
``City of Peace.''
\end{quote}

Historically, however,
the older meaning was likely connected not to abstract ``peace''
but specifically to the deity:

\[
\textit{Šalim}
\]

whose name later blended semantically into:
\[
\texthebrew{שלום}
\]

\bigskip

\section*{Conceptual Structure}

\[
\boxed{
\textit{Šalim}
=
\text{dusk, completion, wholeness}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\textit{Yerushalayim}
=
\text{foundation/city of Šalim}
}
\]

\bigskip

\section*{Connection with Dawn and Dusk}

In the Ugaritic myth:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Šaḥar (Dawn)
    \item Šalim (Dusk)
\end{itemize}

form cosmic boundary-markers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item beginning and ending,
    \item opening and closing,
    \item birth and completion.
\end{itemize}

Thus a ``city of Šalim'' may originally have implied:

\begin{quote}
\emph{the founded place of completion, evening, fullness, or cosmic boundary-order.}
\end{quote}

\bigskip

\section*{Conclusion}

The name:

\[
\texthebrew{ירושלים}
\]

likely predates Israelite religion itself
and probably preserves an older Northwest Semitic divine name:

\[
\textit{Šalim}
\]

Thus ``Jerusalem'' may originally have meant:

\begin{quote}
\Large
\emph{Foundation of Šalim}
\end{quote}

or:

\begin{quote}
\Large
\emph{City of the god of dusk, completion, and wholeness.}
\end{quote}

Only later did the name become reinterpreted through Hebrew:

\[
\texthebrew{שלום}
\]

yielding the familiar theological association:

\begin{quote}
\Large
``City of Peace.''
\end{quote}
```

Hansen's disease.
\end{quote}

Biblical \texthebrew{צרעת} is clearly much broader.

Reasons include:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item It affects houses and clothing.
    \item It can disappear rapidly.
    \item Priests, not physicians, diagnose it.
    \item The concern is ritual and symbolic, not merely medical.
    \item Whiteness and surface spread are central features.
\end{enumerate}

Thus translating \texthebrew{צרעת} simply as:
\begin{quote}
``leprosy''
\end{quote}

can be seriously misleading.

\bigskip

\section*{Other Semitic Languages}

\subsection*{Aramaic / Syriac}

Related forms preserve meanings involving:
\begin{itemize}
    \item leprous affliction,
    \item ritual impurity,
    \item visible bodily corruption.
\end{itemize}

The emphasis remains:
\begin{quote}
\emph{publicly visible affliction producing exclusion.}
\end{quote}

\bigskip

\subsection*{Arabic Comparisons}

Direct cognates are debated,
but several nearby roots are suggestive.

\subsubsection*{\textarabic{صَرَعَ} (\textit{ṣaraʿa})}

Meanings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item strike down,
    \item throw down,
    \item overpower.
\end{itemize}

The overlap is conceptual:
\begin{quote}
being visibly struck or overwhelmed.
\end{quote}

\bigskip

\subsubsection*{\textarabic{جُذَام} (\textit{judhām})}

Arabic word for leprosy.

Its root involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item cutting off,
    \item mutilation,
    \item fragmentation.
\end{itemize}

Again the semantic field centers on:
\[
\text{loss of wholeness}
\]

\bigskip

\section*{Conceptual Structure}

The underlying Semitic imagery may be summarized as:

\[
\boxed{
\text{visible disruptive corruption spreading outward}
}
\]

This produces several interconnected meanings:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Physical} & \textbf{Abstract} \\
\hline
Whitening skin & Visible disorder \\
\hline
Spreading stain & Boundary disruption \\
\hline
Surface corruption & Ritual impurity \\
\hline
Isolation from camp & Social exclusion \\
\hline
Cleansing & Reintegration \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

\section*{Conclusion}

Biblical \texthebrew{צרעת} is best understood not as:
\begin{quote}
``a specific infectious disease''
\end{quote}

but rather as:

\begin{quote}
\Large
\emph{a visibly spreading condition of impurity, corruption, or disorder appearing upon a bounded surface.}
\end{quote}

The central imagery involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item whiteness,
    \item visibility,
    \item impurity,
    \item exclusion,
    \item disrupted wholeness,
    \item outward manifestation of disorder.
\end{itemize}
```

\hspace*{2em}the vine-tier ties it;\\
\hspace*{2em}like a vine, he drops it on his death-field.\footnote{
This difficult section may metaphorically describe winter: a season of sterility in which vines are cut back and fields cleared. Through destruction comes eventual fertility.
