
## Paleo vs Square Script

The short version is:

The ancient Israelites gradually switched from the old local Canaanite/Phoenician-style script (“Paleo-Hebrew”) to the Imperial Aramaic script because:

1. the Assyrian and then Babylonian empires made Aramaic the dominant administrative language,

2. the Persian Empire standardized Imperial Aramaic across a huge territory,

3. Jewish scribes in exile and administration increasingly wrote in Aramaic script,

4. over time that script evolved into the Jewish “square” Hebrew script still used today.


It was not originally “switching languages.” Hebrew continued to exist. The writing system changed.


---

The Scripts

Paleo-Hebrew

The older script used in ancient Israel and Judah looked very close to Phoenician.

Example:

Paleo Hebrew aleph looked like an ox-head shape

letters were more angular and pictographic-looking


This is the script seen in:

the First Temple period

the Siloam Inscription

the Lachish Letters



---

Square Hebrew Script

What we now call “Hebrew script” is actually descended from Imperial Aramaic.

It developed during and after the exile period.

This is the script used in:

Torah scrolls today

Rabbinic literature

most surviving Second Temple Jewish texts


It is sometimes called:

Ashuri script (כתב אשורי)

Assyrian script

square script



---

The Historical Process

1. Assyrian Imperial Dominance

Beginning around the 8th century BCE, the Neo-Assyrian Empire expansion made Aramaic increasingly important.

Aramaic became the practical international language for:

trade

administration

diplomacy


Even before the Babylonian exile, Judah was already exposed heavily to Aramaic bureaucracy.


---

2. Babylonian Exile

The big turning point was the Babylonian captivity.

Judean elites:

scribes

priests

administrators


were deported into a world where Aramaic dominated written administration.

This matters because scripts are usually preserved by scribal institutions, not peasants.

The literate class became bilingual or trilingual:

Hebrew

Aramaic

sometimes Akkadian


Over generations, scribes naturally started writing Hebrew texts using Aramaic letterforms.


---

3. Persian Standardization

The decisive step came under the Achaemenid Empire.

The Persians used Imperial Aramaic as a gigantic bureaucratic standard language across the empire from Egypt to Central Asia.

That meant:

official documents,

contracts,

correspondence,

tax records,

administration


were all flowing through Aramaic scribal conventions.

Jewish scribes integrated into that system.

So the “square script” was:

more internationally legible,

administratively useful,

associated with elite scribal culture.



---

Did Jews View This as Foreign?

At first: probably yes.

Later Jewish tradition actually remembers the transition.

The Talmud discusses whether:

the Torah was originally given in Paleo-Hebrew,

later “restored” in Ashuri script,

or always intended to be in Ashuri.


That debate itself preserves memory of a script transition.

The Samaritans are especially important here.

They preserved a direct descendant of Paleo-Hebrew:

The Samaritan script is basically a living fossil of the older Hebrew writing tradition.


---

Why Did Paleo-Hebrew Survive at All?

Interestingly, some Jews continued using Paleo-Hebrew for symbolic or ideological reasons.

Examples:

Hasmonean coins

Dead Sea Scrolls divine names

nationalist or archaising contexts


At Qumran community, scribes sometimes wrote the divine name יהוה in Paleo-Hebrew inside otherwise square-script manuscripts.

That strongly suggests:

Paleo script had sacred/ancient prestige,

while square script had become the practical standard.


This is very similar to:

Gothic fonts in Europe,

“classical” Chinese forms,

pseudo-runic nationalist aesthetics,

or writing Latin mottos in Roman capitals.



---

The Deep Irony

Modern people often think:

> “Square script = original Hebrew.”



Historically, that’s backwards.

The square script is:

historically Aramaic,

adopted through imperial administration,

then thoroughly Judaized.


Meanwhile Paleo-Hebrew is actually much closer to the original Israelite writing tradition.

So your intuition about:

priestly centralization,

imperial bureaucracy,

exile,

scribal authority,


is very much connected to the actual history of the script transition.
