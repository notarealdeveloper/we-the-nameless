
The original Hebrew alphabet consisted only of consonants, but gradually the letters א, ה, ו, י also became used to indicate vowels, known as matres lectionis (“mothers of reading”). This likely developed through phonetic shifts; for example, bayt (“house”) shifted pronunciation in construct forms while retaining older spelling.

Although no extremely early Hebrew orthography survives directly, Phoenician and Moabite inscriptions give a picture of how First Temple Hebrew was probably written. Early inscriptions generally lack matres lectionis in the middle or end of words. Phoenician inscriptions from the 10th century BCE write forms like לפן instead of לפני and ז instead of זה. The Hebrew Gezer Calendar similarly writes ירח instead of ירחו and שערמ instead of שעורים.

Later, vowel letters began appearing at the ends of words. The Mesha inscription, for example, writes בללה (modern בלילה) and בנתי (modern בניתי). At this stage, final “o” sounds were often written with ה instead of the later ו, so the Mesha inscription writes ארצה for ארצו (“his land”) and בנה for בנו (“his son”). This older usage survives occasionally in the Bible, such as אהלה instead of אהלו (“his tent”), and became fossilized in words like פה (“here”), איפה (“where”), כה (“thus”), and names like שלמה, שילה, and פרעה.

At this stage, medial vowel letters were still uncommon. Some biblical names preserve these older spellings down to the present day: דוד instead of דויד, משה instead of מושה, אהרן instead of אהרון, and יהושע instead of יהושוע.

The terms defective and full/plene spelling refer to writing systems with fewer or more matres lectionis, respectively.

The Hebrew Bible was probably originally written in an even more defective orthography than any surviving manuscript tradition. Among surviving textual traditions, the Masoretic Text is generally the most conservative in its use of vowel letters, while the Samaritan Pentateuch is fuller, and the Qumran texts use vowel letters most freely.

The Masoretic tradition usually marks long vowels with vowel letters, though with many inconsistencies. In the Qumran tradition, ו is commonly used for o/u-type vowels, while י is often used for long i and even tsere vowels. Final long i may appear as יא by analogy with forms like היא and הביא. Qumran texts also use final ה in forms like חוטה and קורה, and sometimes א to indicate vowel quality in final or medial positions.

Samaritan texts likewise tend toward fuller spelling traditions compared to the Masoretic Text.
