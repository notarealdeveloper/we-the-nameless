Yes — with some important nuance, but overall your claim is basically mainstream.

The key distinction is:

“Jacob” as an ancestral individual vs.

“Jacob/Israel” as an ethnonym or collective self-designation

The second is absolutely solidly attested in biblical poetry and early Hebrew tradition.

The oldest poetic strata of the Hebrew Bible routinely use:

Jacob

Israel

Jeshurun

as parallel collective names for the people.

And many scholars think some of those poems preserve genuinely early Iron I or even late Bronze/Iron transition traditions.

Especially:

Song of Deborah (Judges 5)

Blessing of Jacob (Genesis 49)

Song of the Sea (Exodus 15)

parts of Balaam material (Numbers 23–24)

These are widely regarded as among the oldest linguistic layers in the Hebrew Bible.

---

The important thing is this:

In those poems, “Jacob” often behaves less like:

> “a remembered biological founder”

and more like:

> “the poetic-personified collective”

Exactly like:

Israel

Edom

Moab

Ammon

often do.

That is very normal ethnogenesis language in the ancient Near East.

---

So when you say:

> “the tribes called themselves that word”

Yes, broadly speaking.

At minimum:

the tradition-bearing population behind early Israelite poetry clearly used “Jacob” as a self-referential collective designation.

That is not controversial.

---

And yes, the chronology broadly works.

If the Gezer sherd is roughly:

Middle Bronze / Late Bronze transition-ish

ca. 17th–15th century BCE depending on dating arguments,

then that is earlier than the composition of the preserved biblical poems, but not absurdly earlier than the likely formation of the ethnolinguistic substrate that later became highland Israel.

The crucial point:

The name Yaʿqob / Yaʿqub / ʿQB is already ancient West Semitic independently of the Bible.

It's not a uniquely Israelite invention.

We have related forms in broader Semitic onomastics.

So reading ʿQB on an early inscription does not require:

biblical patriarch historicity,

monarchy,

Torah,

or even “Israel” yet.

It only requires:

an already-existing Northwest Semitic root/name cluster.

Which is entirely plausible.

---

Where mainstream scholars would become cautious is this step:

> “Therefore this inscription refers to proto-Israelites.”

That is much harder.

Because:

Gezer is Canaanite broadly,

many names/roots are shared across West Semitic populations,

and early alphabetic inscriptions are too short to securely identify ethnicity.

So:

“possible ʿQB reading” = plausible

“this proves Israelites” = not plausible

“this may reflect an already-circulating Northwest Semitic name later central in Israelite identity” = very defensible wording

That last formulation is probably the strongest academically sustainable version of your idea.
