Best guess:

𐤓𐤁?𐤍𐤌 𐤍𐤈𐤍𐤋𐤌 𐤄 𐤀𐤏𐤌 𐤇𐤓

RB?NM NṬNLM H AʿM ḤR

Reading:

“The great ones dedicated (this) to the mountain people.”

Why:

- 𐤍𐤈𐤍 = a very plausible archaic/spelling-loose form of נתן “gave, dedicated”
- 𐤋 = the strongest grammatical filler
- 𐤌 = plural/collective marker
- “to/for the mountain people” is exactly the kind of terse dedicatory formula expected on a prestige object
- avoids forcing obscure vocabulary
- works with unstable proto-alphabetic spelling conventions
- semantically coherent from start to finish

RB LYM YHY PM H ʿM ḤR

Possible readings:

- “The chief of the companions: let this be the command of the mountain people.”
- “The lord of the joined people: this shall be the decree of the hill tribe.”
- “The master of the Levite-like associates: may the mountain people proclaim this.”
- “The great one of the attached clan: let the utterance of the highland people stand.”
- “Chief of the companions, may this be the word of the mountain people.”

## Background 

The Wadi el-Hol inscriptions are usually dated to roughly:

ca. 1900–1800 BCE

sometimes narrowed to:

late 12th Dynasty,

early 13th Dynasty,

around the reigns of:

Amenemhat III,

Amenemhat IV,

or slightly later.




The most commonly cited scholarly range today is approximately:

1850–1800 BCE

They are therefore:

probably older than the Lachish Dagger inscription,

and among the earliest known proto-alphabetic inscriptions.


The inscriptions were found along a desert road in Upper Egypt used by:

soldiers,

miners,

expeditions,

Semitic laborers,

and Canaanite-speaking groups interacting with Egypt.


What makes them especially important is that:

they already show signs of a true consonantal alphabet,

likely derived acrophonically from Egyptian hieroglyphs,

but adapted for a Northwest Semitic language.


They are often treated as the “missing link” between:

Egyptian hieroglyphic signs,

Proto-Sinaitic,

and later Proto-Canaanite/Phoenician.


Some scholars even think the Wadi el-Hol inscriptions are earlier than most Sinai inscriptions traditionally called Proto-Sinaitic.

One reason your ideas about:

unstable spelling,

mixed directionality,

nonstandard phonetics,

and Egyptian-influenced redundant writing


are actually pretty reasonable is precisely because Wadi el-Hol exists in this transitional environment where:

Egyptian scribal influence is still very strong,

but alphabetic reduction is already happening.
