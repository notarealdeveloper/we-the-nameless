Here's the existing view of what it says:

The so-called “Lachish Dagger” is a bronze dagger from Middle Bronze Age Tel Lachish with a tiny scratched inscription that many scholars think may be one of the earliest alphabetic inscriptions in the southern Levant. The inscription is extremely short and ambiguous — basically just a handful of marks — so there is no consensus reading. 

The most common scholarly positions are roughly:

1. It really is an early alphabetic inscription

This is now the majority view.

Usually interpreted as 4 Proto-Canaanite / Proto-Sinaitic letters scratched onto the blade.

Scholars like Benjamin Sass and Gordon Hamilton generally accept it as alphabetic. 



2. The signs are too ambiguous to decipher confidently

Even scholars who accept them as letters usually admit the “reading” is highly uncertain.

The signs are weathered and primitive enough that several letters can fit each mark.

3. The marks may not be writing at all

Minority position.

Some earlier archaeologists thought they could be decorative scratches, ownership marks, or pseudo-writing.

But later discoveries at Lachish made alphabetic interpretation more plausible. 

The actual proposed readings vary wildly because the signs are so ambiguous. Common identifications include letters equivalent to:

l

k

b

ʿ

n

t

m


depending on orientation and direction of reading.

One often-mentioned tentative sequence is something like:

trnz

lbʿ

mqt

or similar fragmentary combinations


—but none are widely accepted as meaningful words. Most modern discussions are more cautious and simply say “four probable alphabetic signs.”

Visually, the signs are usually interpreted as simplified pictographic Proto-Canaanite characters resembling:

eye (ʿayin)

palm (kaph)

ox head (ʾaleph)

cross/mark (taw)

etc.


The important thing is less the content than the existence of alphabetic signs that early. The dagger matters historically because it may bridge the chronological gap between the Sinai inscriptions and later clear Canaanite alphabetic inscriptions. 

As for whether it was a real dagger:

Yes — almost certainly a genuine functional bronze dagger, not merely decorative.

Reasons:

It came from a tomb context typical for real weapons in the Middle Bronze Age.

Bronze daggers were common prestige sidearms at the time.

The object’s proportions and metallurgy fit functional weapons.

The inscription is tiny and secondary; the dagger was not primarily a writing tablet or ceremonial plaque.

That said, “functional” in Bronze Age elite contexts often also meant:

status object

burial prestige item

symbolic warrior identity

So it may not have been used constantly in combat. Think:

elite sidearm,

ritual/status weapon,

or high-status personal possession.


But not “merely decorative” in the modern fake-replica sense.

The inscription itself was probably scratched in after manufacture, possibly by:

an owner,

a donor,

a craftsman,

or someone marking possession or identity.

One major reason scholars think the marks are intentional writing rather than decoration is that they appear arranged linearly in a way resembling other early alphabetic inscriptions rather than ornamental motifs.
