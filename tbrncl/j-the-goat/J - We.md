## When is J

### J likely wrote after the kingdom fractured (930 BC)

J mentions Peni-El, a northern city founded by Jeroboam I.
Jeroboam I was the first king of the northern Israelite kingdom.
This places J after c. 930 BC.

J appears to include many puns on Rehoboam's name.
Rehoboam's reign was between c. 931-913 BC.
This places J after 931 BC.

J only includes the sons of Jacob that correspond to southern (Judah) tribes.

Genesis 49 (Blessing of Judah)
The scepter shall not depart from Judah.
Appears to be foreshadowing Davidic monarchy.

Southern geography is elevated in J.
Abraham settles in Hebron.
Burial cave at Machpelah (Gen 23).
Hebron was an early Davidic capital. (check this)
Sacred history anchored in Judah territory.

### J likely wrote before Assyria destroyed Israel (722 BC)

J mostly references southern kingdom of Judah.
E mostly references northern kingdom of Israel.
Israel was defeated by Assyria in 722 B.C. and ceased to exist.
Neither J nor E shows any awareness of Israel ceasing to exist.
So both J and E almost certainly must have been written before 722 B.C.

## What is J

### J as Satire

J's Yahweh character appears to be irony, satire, polemic, or joke.

### Satire of the Priestly Class

J's book is in part a satire of the outsider Levite priest class who are the elites of society with no indigenous land who don't understand the local culture but tax it.

And J performs that satire by writing a book about _their god_ Yahweh, using jokes that only native Canaanites will get.

Yahweh claims to be El, Asherah, he's acting like Baal, and they get it. Because that's how the Levites act.

The Torah catches on among the priests because it appears to elevate them, while actually being a series of dogwhistles that everyone except the Priests gets.

In other words, it's the New Testament. It's Jesus vs the Pharisees. And then that story gets co-opted by the Pharisees and they spread it.

### Abracadabra

J is much harder to characterize than any other source.

J is punny, brilliant, irreverent, ironic, and hilarious.

The closest genre would be something like "playful word magic."

## Who is J?

Probably someone in the royal court.

### Anti Canaanite themes

J is described as: Earthy, ironic, the opposite of a Priest.

Everyone says J "Emphasizes a close relationship between humans and the soil."

Who has a relationship with the soil in this region?

The native Canaanites.

J is said to contain "Anti-Canaanite themes" that sound a lot like irony.
- For example, in Genesis 28 below.
- For context: Genesis 27 is where Isaac says Jacob's goat skin Esau costume "smells like the aroma of a field blessed by Yahweh." (Seriously, tell me THAT's not a joke.)
- In Genesis 27:36, after the deception, Esau says "Isn't he rightly named Jacob?" The NIV contains a footnote saying: Jacob means he grasps the heel, a Hebrew idiom for "he takes advantage of" or "he deceives."
- This suggests that the pun of "Jacob as deceiver" would have been clear to readers at the time.
- Also note: The passage below can be read as extremely obvious irony:
- "Do not marry a Canaanite woman... May El Shaddai bless you..."
- This is like saying: "In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful, do not marry a Muslim woman and do not pray to their gods."
- The following line adds to the irony: "So that you may steal this land from its rightful inhabitants, where we now reside as foreigners."
- Right after that, when Esau realizes how much his father hates the Canaanites, he's like "Oh, ok." and grabs a non-Canaanite wife.
- He doesn't divorce his Canaanite wives, he just marries another one!
- Translation:
	- You have: X, X
	- Father: "Never have X my son."
	- You: "Oh. Ok."
	- You now have: X, X, Y
- How do people not see this is fucking funny?

Gen 28:1-9

> 2. Go at once to Paddan Aram, to the house of your mother’s father Bethuel. Take a wife for yourself there, from among the daughters of Laban, your mother’s brother.
> 3. May El Shaddai bless you and make you fruitful and increase your numbers until you become a community of peoples.

> 6. Now Esau learned that Isaac had blessed Jacob and had sent him to Paddan Aram to take a wife from there, and that when he blessed him he commanded him, "Do not marry a Canaanite woman,"
> 7. and that Jacob had obeyed his father and mother and had gone to Paddan Aram.
> 8. Esau then realized how displeasing the Canaanite women were to his father Isaac;
> 9. so he went to Ishmael and married Mahalath, the sister of Nebaioth and daughter of Ishmael son of Abraham, in addition to the wives he already had.

Interpretation:
- Jacob here is doing to Esau what the Levites later did to Canaan, by assimilating their pantheon into YHWH, and by taxing them despite not owning any land in the region.
- The Levites are the ones who say "You must treat outsiders equally."
- The Levites are the ones whose name seems to mean something like "resident alien."
- The Levites are the ones that J describes as "Brutal and violent" in the Shechem story and later again in the blessing of Jacob.
