	Here are some comments scholars have made over the years on this topic:

		\begin{quote}
		\aC{
			\textsl{Er's wickedness was like Onan's wickedness: he deliberately
			prevented Tamar from becoming pregnant so that pregnancy would not
			spoil her beauty.}\\
			\hfill ---Babylonian Talmud, \emph{Yevamot} 34b (ca. 500 CE); cf. Rashi (ca. 1040--1105)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Scripture does not tell us Er's sin. His death nevertheless
			shows that he died for his own wickedness, rather than as punishment
			upon Judah for the sale of Joseph.}\\
			\hfill ---Nachmanides (1194--1270)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Since Er was evil specifically ``in YHWH's eyes,'' his crime
			was apparently hidden from other people but known to God.}\\
			\hfill ---Sforno (1475--1550)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Perhaps Er committed the same sexual offense as Onan;
			others proposed still other ``unnatural'' sexual acts.}\\
			\hfill ---Christian commentators, ancient--early modern (ca. 400--1700)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Er was notoriously wicked; whatever his unnamed crime was,
			it must have been sufficiently grave to provoke extraordinary
			divine judgment.}\\
			\hfill ---common Protestant interpretation (ca. 1600--1900)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Er was ``a Sodomite, say the Hebrews''---though this
			is ``hard to say.'' Still, he was ``too wicked to live,'' and
			``you may know him to be the son of a Canaanitess.''}\\
			\hfill ---John Trapp (1650s)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Er was ``notoriously wicked,'' and YHWH slew him
			``in some extraordinary and remarkable manner.''}\\
			\hfill ---Matthew Poole (1680s)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Er was guilty of some ``very heinous sin.''
			Perhaps, as one tradition held, he had intercourse
			with Tamar in the ``sodomitical'' manner.\fB{You know the one.}
			YHWH either struck him dead immediately or sent a disease which
			quickly carried him off.}\\
			\hfill ---John Gill (1748--1763)

			\medskip

			Finally, after several thousand years, there appeared the voice
			of one crying in the wilderness, Adam, as if the man, and he
			spoke to the people, saying:

			\medskip

			\textsl{The Hebrew word expressing Er's wickedness is his own name
			with the letters reversed: \heb{ער}, Er; \heb{רע}, bad.}\\
			\hfill ---Adam Clarke (1762--1832; commentary 1810s)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Er lived ``in defiance of God, and his law.'' His untimely
			and childless death was accordingly a divine punishment.}\\
			\hfill ---Joseph Benson (ca. 1811--1818)

			\medskip

			But the people heeded not the voice of Adam, and they continued
			their search for the ancient lost secret of Er's wickedness.
			And their exegesis continued, as they spoke to us, saying.

			\medskip

			\textsl{In what particular forms he showed his wickedness we
			are not told; but being the son of a Canaanitish woman, he
			probably imbibed, in his earliest years, the spirit of
			Canaanitish idolatry and vice.}\\
			\hfill ---Daniel D. Whedon (ca. 1870s)

			\medskip

			\textsl{Judah had married into a Canaanite family, ``casting
			off the restraints of religion,'' so it is ``not surprising''
			that the family produced ``bold and unblushing wickedness.''
			Er was, ``in all likelihood,'' addicted to the ``abominable
			vices of Canaan.''}\\
			\hfill ---Jamieson, Fausset \& Brown (1871)

			\medskip

			Two thousand years of commentators thus attempted to discover
			what terrible thing Er had done. The narrator supplies no crime.
			Even commentators who eventually noticed something curious
			about the spelling often went right on looking for the crime.

			The search continues to the present day.

			\medskip

			\textsl{And Er, Judah’s firstborn, was evil in the eyes of the LORD.
			The nature of his moral failing remains unspecified, 
			but given the insistent pattern of reversal of 
			primogeniture in all these stories, it seems almost 
			sufficient merely to be firstborn in order to incur God’s 
			displeasure: though the firstborn is not necessarily evil, 
			he usually turns out to be obtuse, rash, wild, or otherwise 
			disqualified from carrying on the heritage. It is 
			noteworthy that Judah, who invented the lie that triggered 
			his own father’s mourning for a dead son, is bereaved of 
			two sons in rapid sequence. In contrast to Jacob’s 
			extravagant grief, nothing is said about Judah’s emotional 
			response to the losses.}\\
			\hfill ---A comment on AR from someone named R.A. (1997).
		}
		\end{quote}
