## Q

Mark 6:41. παρατιθῶσιν.

Does the grammar imply that he DID set it before them?

Does it clearly specify that THEM is the 5,000 and not the disciples?

I know the standard reading.

What does the grammar say?

## A

> Worked for 40ד and 40ㄴ

The grammar is more precise than most English translations make it look. Mark 6:41 has:

> καὶ ἐδίδου τοῖς μαθηταῖς [αὐτοῦ] ἵνα παρατιθῶσιν αὐτοῖς
“and he was giving [them] to the disciples so that they might set [them] before them.” 

The crucial point is that παρατιθῶσιν does not grammatically assert that the setting-before actually happened. It is present active subjunctive, 3rd-person plural, governed by ἵνα. So its force is purpose:

> ἵνα παρατιθῶσιν
“so that they may/might set [it] before…”
“in order that they serve…”

not:

> “and they did set it before…”

The indicative ἐδίδου, by contrast, does assert an event:

> ἐδίδου τοῖς μαθηταῖς
“he was giving / kept giving [the loaves] to the disciples.”

So grammatically Mark asserts Jesus → bread → disciples. He then states the intended next step with a purpose clause. 

Who is who?

The Greek gives you:

Greek	Grammar	Referent

ἐδίδου	3sg imperfect	Jesus was giving
τοῖς μαθηταῖς	dative plural	to the disciples
παρατιθῶσιν	3pl present subjunctive	they should set/serve
αὐτοῖς	3pl dative pronoun	to them

The subject of παρατιθῶσιν is not explicitly named. Greek just says “they.” But the overwhelmingly natural antecedent is τοῖς μαθηταῖς:

> he kept giving to the disciples,
so that they might serve…

That connection is not encoded by some special grammatical agreement—the dative μαθηταῖς cannot grammatically be the subject of παρατιθῶσιν—but ordinary discourse makes the disciples the obvious supplied subject.

Does αὐτοῖς grammatically force “the 5,000”?

No—not by morphology alone.

αὐτοῖς simply means:

> “to them / for them”

masculine/neuter dative plural. It does not contain “crowd,” “people,” or “5,000.”

So if your question is extremely strict—

> Could I determine from the isolated morphology of αὐτοῖς that it means the crowd?

No.

But in the sentence as actual Greek discourse, the crowd is by far the natural referent.

The alternative reading would produce something like:

> Jesus kept giving the bread to the disciples
so that they [the disciples] might set it before them [the disciples].


That would make αὐτοῖς effectively coreferential with the subject of παρατιθῶσιν. Greek has a reflexive pronoun ἑαυτοῖς, “for/before themselves,” which would be the natural explicit way to express that idea. More importantly, it makes poor sense in this context.

The preceding sentence has just supplied the other plural participant:

> ἀνακλῖναι πάντας
“to make everyone recline”

and immediately after the παρατιθῶσιν clause Mark writes:

> καὶ τοὺς δύο ἰχθύας ἐμέρισεν πᾶσιν
“and the two fish he divided among all.”


Then:

> καὶ ἔφαγον πάντες
“and all ate.” 


So the discourse structure is essentially:

> Jesus → disciples → them/everyone
fish → everyone
everyone ate.



That makes αὐτοῖς = the assembled crowd exceptionally strong even though the pronoun itself doesn't lexically say so.

But here's the distinction I think you're getting at

The Greek does not quite say:

> “The disciples distributed the bread to the crowd.”

It says:

> Jesus was giving the bread to the disciples for them to serve it to the others.

The first is asserted by an indicative.

The second is expressed as purpose/intention by ἵνα + subjunctive.

Verse 42 subsequently tells us:

> καὶ ἔφαγον πάντες
> “and they all ate.”


So the narrative as a whole makes clear that the feeding was accomplished, but παρατιθῶσιν itself does not assert completion.

And there's one further interesting detail: because Mark has the present subjunctive παρατιθῶσιν, rather than an aorist like παραθῶσιν found in some later textual traditions, its aspect presents the serving as an ongoing/processual activity:

> “so that they might be setting/serving [it] before them.”

That pairs very naturally with the imperfect:

> ἐδίδου = “he kept giving”
> ἵνα παρατιθῶσιν = “so that they could keep serving”



So the grammar gives a sort of conveyor belt:

Jesus kept giving → disciples were to keep serving → everybody ate.

But the middle arrow is grammatically purpose, not an independently asserted historical action.
