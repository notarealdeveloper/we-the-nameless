## Q

Is the phrase "of the loaves" missing in Mark 6:44 in early Markan manuscripts? If so, show me.

## A

Worked for 1m 31s

Yes. And this is a real early textual variant, not merely a translation issue.

The Greek words in question are τοὺς ἄρτους, literally “the loaves” (accusative, not grammatically “of the loaves”; English versions sometimes supply “of”). The longer text reads:

> καὶ ἦσαν οἱ φαγόντες τοὺς ἄρτους πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
> “And the ones who ate the loaves were five thousand men.”

But 𝔓45, our earliest surviving manuscript containing Mark 6:44 (3rd century), omits τοὺς ἄρτους. CSNTM dates 𝔓45 to the third century and identifies folio 5r as containing Mark 6:36–50.  A published transcription of the relevant part of 𝔓45 reads:

> … φαγοντες πεντακισχειλιοι ανδ[ρες …
“… the ones who ate — five thousand men …”

There is nothing between φαγόντες and πεντακισχίλιοι. 

So schematically:

Witness	Mark 6:44

𝔓45, 3rd c.	οἱ φαγόντες ——— πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
Sinaiticus א, 4th c.	οἱ φαγόντες ——— … πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
Vaticanus B, 4th c.	οἱ φαγόντες τοὺς ἄρτους πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
Alexandrinus A, 5th c.	οἱ φαγόντες τοὺς ἄρτους πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες

The Sinaiticus transcription is particularly interesting. It actually displays:

> Και ησαν οι φαγοντες <το> ως πεντακισχιλιοι ανδρες

with a deleted το, while the apparatus classifies Sinaiticus as omitting τοὺς ἄρτους. Vaticanus and Alexandrinus explicitly have τους αρτους. 

And this isn't just 𝔓45 doing one of its notorious abbreviations. The omission has a substantial independent manuscript tradition. The NET textual note gives the omission as:

> 𝔓45 א D W Θ f¹ f¹³ 28 565 700 2542 lat sa

while the inclusion is supported by weighty witnesses including:

> A B L 33 𝔐

The NET editors therefore call the decision difficult. 

So there are genuinely two ancient versions of the sentence:

Long:

> καὶ ἦσαν οἱ φαγόντες τοὺς ἄρτους πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
> “The ones who ate the loaves were five thousand men.”

Short:

> καὶ ἦσαν οἱ φαγόντες πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
> “The ones who ate were five thousand men.”

And given what we were just discussing, the short form is actually more interesting stylistically. It leaves οἱ φαγόντες standing naked as a substantive:

> “the eaters” / “the ones who ate”

The narrative begins in 6:31 with the apostles unable even to eat, and—in the earliest surviving Mark manuscript—the episode ends simply by counting οἱ φαγόντες, “the eaters”: 5,000 men.

That makes me substantially less inclined than I was before to build any literary argument around “the loaves” in v.44 itself. There is a very serious possibility that Mark's sentence originally just ended:

“And the eaters were five thousand men.”

Which is, frankly, more Mark-like.

## Q

Is it the way you'd generally say "men"?

Does Mark EVER use that word for "men" anywhere else in the book?

## A

No.

Mark 6:44 is the only place in the entire Gospel where Mark uses ἀνήρ in the plural, ἄνδρες, “men.” But he does use the same lemma three other times in the singular.

I counted the lemmatized SBLGNT text of Mark directly using MorphGNT’s lemma column, then checked the relevant passages against NA28. MorphGNT identifies its final column as the lemma, so inflected forms such as ἄνδρα, ἄνδρες, and ἀνδρί all count under ἀνήρ. 

Lemma	Basic meaning	Occurrences in Mark	Where / what Mark does with it

ἄνθρωπος	human being, person; traditionally “man”	56	Mark's overwhelmingly common human-being word. Includes the 14 occurrences in the title “Son of Man.”
ἀνήρ	man, adult male; husband	4	6:20 “a righteous and holy man”; 6:44 “5,000 men”; 10:2 “a man/husband [divorcing] a woman”; 10:12 “her husband”
ἄρσην	male, as a sex/category	1	10:6: ἄρσεν καὶ θῆλυ, “male and female”
νεανίσκος	young man, youth	2	14:51, the fleeing young man; 16:5, the young man at the tomb

The four ἀνήρ occurrences can be checked directly in the lemmatized text: Mark 6:20 has ἄνδρα … ἀνήρ, 6:44 has ἄνδρες … ἀνήρ, 10:2 has ἀνδρὶ … ἀνήρ, and 10:12 has ἄνδρα … ἀνήρ. 

So, more specifically:

Mark 6:20: ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον = “a righteous and holy man.” This is unequivocally an adult human male—John the Baptist. NA28 has exactly this wording. 

Mark 6:44: πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες = “five thousand men.” The only plural ἄνδρες in Mark. 

Mark 10:2: ἀνδρὶ γυναῖκα ἀπολῦσαι = “for a man/husband to divorce a woman/wife.” 

Mark 10:12: τὸν ἄνδρα αὐτῆς = explicitly “her husband.” 

And Mark gives us an exceptionally useful control four verses after ἀνδρί in 10:2:

> ἄρσεν καὶ θῆλυ ἐποίησεν αὐτούς
> “male and female he made them.”

That's ἄρσεν, from ἄρσην, when Mark actually wants the category “male.” 

So I would sharpen the lexical distinction considerably:

ἀνήρ : ἄρσην ≈ man : male

rather than treating ἀνήρ as roughly equivalent to English male.

And there's a striking frequency contrast:

> ἄνθρωπος — 56×
> ἀνήρ — 4×
> ἄρσην — 1×

Thus πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες isn't weird Greek for “5,000 men.” It's perfectly normal.

What's unusual for Mark is his lexical choice: he uses ἀνήρ extremely sparingly, and 6:44 is the one and only occasion in his Gospel where he deploys it as a plural count of “men.”

That makes the wording worth noticing, but it does not make “5,000 males [of some species]” a natural first reading. Mark himself demonstrates that ἄρσεν is his available word for male in precisely that sense.

## Q

Give me the verses where he uses the singular.

## A

The three singular uses of ἀνήρ in Mark are:

Verse	Greek form	Phrase	Meaning

Mark 6:20	ἄνδρα	ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον	“a righteous and holy man” — John the Baptist
Mark 10:2	ἀνδρί	εἰ ἔξεστιν ἀνδρὶ γυναῖκα ἀπολῦσαι	“whether it is lawful for a man to divorce a woman/wife”
Mark 10:12	ἄνδρα	ἐὰν αὐτὴ ἀπολύσασα τὸν ἄνδρα αὐτῆς	“if she divorces her husband”


So the lemma ἀνήρ occurs:

3× singular: Mark 6:20; 10:2; 10:12

1× plural: Mark 6:44, πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες, “five thousand men”


The clearest pure “adult male human” comparison with 6:44 is 6:20, since 10:2 and 10:12 are in a marriage context where ἀνήρ naturally shades into “husband.”

## Q

So it's husband twice, man once, then the people lying on the green grass in the sheep without a shepherd scene, then all other Marcan references to men are 56 uses of anthropos?

## A

Basically yes, with one important correction: the 56 uses of ἄνθρωπος are not all specifically references to men in the male sense. They mean human/person/humanity, and include things like “the Son of Man.”

Mark’s pattern is:

Word	Mark’s usage

ἀνήρ	4× total
→ Mark 6:20	“a righteous and holy man”
→ Mark 10:2	man/husband divorcing a woman
→ Mark 10:12	her husband
→ Mark 6:44	5,000 ἄνδρες reclining on the green grass
ἄνθρωπος	56× — human/person/man in the generic human sense
ἄρσην	1× — specifically male, in “male and female”
νεανίσκος	2× — young man/youth


So the really striking fact is:

> Outside the marriage passages, Mark uses ἀνήρ only twice in the entire Gospel.



One is:

> John the Baptist was ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον
“a righteous and holy man.” — 6:20



The other is only 24 verses later:

> πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες
“five thousand men.” — 6:44



And yes, those are the ἄνδρες sitting/reclining on χλωρὸς χόρτος, “green grass/fodder,” immediately after Mark says the crowd resembles πρόβατα, “sheep,” without a shepherd.

So it is fair to say that Mark does not habitually call male humans ἀνήρ. His ordinary human-being vocabulary is overwhelmingly ἄνθρωπος. When ἀνήρ appears, two of four occurrences are essentially forced by marriage/husband terminology, leaving only John in 6:20 and the 5,000 in 6:44 as non-marital uses.

That doesn't prove a sheep/male-animal double entendre—but it makes the lexical choice in 6:44 substantially more conspicuous than if Mark were throwing ἄνδρες around routinely.
