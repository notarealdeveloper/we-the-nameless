An editorial proof of concept: a small recurring character vocabulary used to segment the Primary History into meaningful reading units.

The Chinese glosses are lexical; the English microbook titles and the mappings to biblical passages are editorial. Repeated signs are intentional. Most units span multiple chapters; a few close a story mid-chapter or preserve a short but independent closing movement. These are navigation divisions, not claims to recover original source documents.

References use common English/KJV-style versification. Map them explicitly to the Hebrew verse anchors before implementing them in the source. `1 Sam 4:1a` denotes the initial statement about Samuel’s word reaching Israel; `4:1b` begins the account of Israel going out to battle.

The supplied master.tex chapter summaries are the coverage checklist. The full chapter includes were not supplied. The original On the Lamb interlude and Genesis Apocrypha have therefore not been given invented subdivisions.

## Why these divisions belong together

### A repeated vocabulary, not a collection of logos

A sign keeps a recognizable meaning when it returns. 冊 gathers people into a written order: the first wilderness census, the replacement generation, the reckoning at the end of Samuel, and the reconstructed community in Nehemiah. 分 divides nations, classifies bodies, apportions spoils, splits kingdoms, and excludes members. Those changes of setting are part of the reading, not reasons to invent unrelated signs.

### 衣: identity that can be put on, taken off, or passed on

Genesis 37–41 binds Joseph and Tamar through clothing: the distinctive robe, the veil, the garment retained as evidence, and the dress accompanying elevation at court. The unit does not treat Genesis 38 as disposable interruption. Later, 衣 returns for the mantle inherited by Elisha. The lexical meaning is clothing; the recurring editorial meaning is authority and identity made transferable through clothing. Sources: Genesis 37–38, Genesis 39, Genesis 41, and 2 Kings 2.

### 工 … 工: work changes its owner

Exodus begins with imposed work and ends with contributed materials and skilled work. Repeating 工 makes the contrast visible without falsely suggesting that departure from Egypt means the end of work. The intervening questions are whom one serves, who commands, and what people make. Sources: Exodus 1 and 35–40.

### 切: the covenant is cut

Genesis 15–17 joins a covenant made amid divided animals to circumcision, with disputed offspring between them. The Hebrew idiom in Genesis 15:18 literally includes “cut a covenant.” 切 is therefore a cross-language semantic match, not a fabricated similarity of pronunciation. Source: NET Bible, Genesis 15:18, translation note: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+15&version=NET .

### Mouth, eye, staff

Numbers 10:11–19 moves from hungry and challenging mouths, to eyes that see the land and reports that prevent entry, to the staff that authenticates disputed priestly authority. The sequence 口 目 杖 condenses different failures of collective action without assigning the entire book a single vague “desert” symbol. Sources: Numbers 11–19, with the chapter inventory in master.tex.

### The house becomes the sanctuary

The modern graphic composition 宀 + 示 gives 宗. CUHK identifies the early 宗 graph with an ancestral sacred house. This is not an invented etymology. The biblical connection is our editorial analogy: David’s “house” moves among palace, household, and dynasty in 2 Samuel 7, then becomes the problem of building the sacred house in Kings. Sources: CUHK 宗 and 宀; 2 Samuel 7: https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Samuel+7&version=NRSVUE .

### The container is not a boat

匚 is used as the two-stroke container radical for the Ark narrative, not as a ship character. CUHK also documents ritual uses of the depicted vessel for ancestral tablets. This supplies a historical Chinese parallel to the general idea of a sacred container; it is not a claim of cultural borrowing between China and Israel. Source: CUHK 匚.

### Boundaries that carry narrative meaning

Creation closes at Genesis 2:3. Departure’s sea narrative closes after its song at Exodus 15:21. Deuteronomy changes from instruction to Moses’s final limit at 32:48. Joshua changes from arrival and its rites to the armed encounter at 5:13. Samuel’s emerging public word closes at 1 Samuel 4:1a before the battle narrative begins. Saul’s fall is not complete until David’s lament in 2 Samuel 1. The final Kings movement crosses a modern chapter boundary at 23:31 and then stops before the distinct release notice in 25:27–30. These are proposed reading boundaries, not claims that the underlying text came from a single author at each boundary.

### 亡 is not the final word

Kings’ last four verses are given 存: Jehoiachin is released, changes prison clothes, and receives continuing food at a foreign royal table. That is a different ending from the preceding destroyed city, assassinated governor, and flight. 存 means survival/existence, and Chinese sources explicitly contrast it with 亡. The chosen title does not promise restored sovereignty. Sources: CUHK 存; 2 Kings 25:27–30.

### Sound echoes that survive the semantic test

合 hé, “join,” and 禾 hé, “grain,” are modern Mandarin homophones. In the final Genesis sequence the family’s reunion becomes the management of food and the settlement of Egypt. The lexical sound echo supports an already chosen boundary; it is not a historical connection between the two Chinese words.

Japanese supplies two secondary echoes. 衣, clothing, and 位, rank or position, both have the on-reading i. 冊, books or written documents, and 殺, killing, both have the on-reading satsu. The first suits clothing that changes social identity; the second is a grim optional echo for the register/census that ends Samuel in plague. These are shared dictionary-listed on-readings, not assertions that the bare characters always receive those readings in Japanese prose, and not biblical authorial puns.

### A disciplined compromise on simplicity

Eighty of the 115 section signs use at most five strokes; 101 use at most six. The average is about 4.8 strokes per section occurrence. The larger forms earn specific meanings: 客 for the mirrored hospitality crises; 易 for the Jacob–Laban economy of substitutions; 骨 for descendants, burial, and the remains whose story is not finished. Preserve distinctions such as 口 versus 囗 and 土 versus 士 rather than making similar-looking characters indistinguishable.

# Radicalized Index: Additional Data

**I would make this a recurring language, not a collection of one-off icons.** A character should become familiar, disappear for a while, then return when the same human problem returns in another form.

That leads to **104 microbooks for Genesis–Kings**, plus **11 for the Ezra–Nehemiah–Esther coda in your manuscript**. The lengths vary deliberately: Joshua needs only five; Genesis needs seventeen. A four-verse ending can deserve its own sign, while nine chapters of territorial allocation can belong together.

# 元 — Genesis

Genesis needs the finest grain: the creation of a world, the formation of families, and the transmission of property, identity, and memory are different kinds of beginnings. Your summaries provide the incidents; I would regroup them like this. 

### 天　人　血　水　分　行　切　客　子　代　兄　易　回　衣　合　禾　骨

|  **人** rén  | **Humanity**         | 2:4–3:24    | Earth becomes a person; companionship, desire, knowledge, work, and expulsion follow.               |
|  **血** xuè  | **Bloodlines**       | 4:1–6:8     | Brothers, murder, descendants, and multiplying violence: blood as kinship and bloodshed.            |

**衣 is the strongest discovery here.** Genesis 38 no longer looks like an inconvenient interruption to “the Joseph story.” Joseph’s robe is used to misidentify his fate; Tamar’s veil changes how she is identified; Joseph’s garment becomes false evidence; changed clothing accompanies his emergence from prison and elevation at court. The recurring object is a means of changing what another person believes someone to be. ([Bible Gateway][1])

There is even a useful Japanese echo: **衣**, clothing, and **位**, rank or position, share the on-reading **i**. The clothing motif earns the choice independently; the sound adds another layer. ([Kanjipedia][2])

**切** is comparably economical. Genesis 15:18 literally uses the Hebrew idiom “cut a covenant”; the divided animals and the later circumcision make *The Cut* more than a modern editorial joke. ([Bible Gateway][3])

And the near-ending has a small sound joint:

**合 hé → 禾 hé**
**Joining → grain.**

The family is reunited, then its reunion becomes entangled with the food system that reorganizes Egypt. The Chinese words provide “joining” and “grain”; the connection between them is our designed reading. ([Humanum][4])

# 出 — Exodus

The decisive framing is **工 … 工**: work at both ends, but a changed relationship between the workers, the commanding power, and what gets built. The intervening units preserve the movement from enslavement through departure to sanctuary.

I would **not** put all ten plagues under 災. The final plague belongs with the marked doorway, meal, departure, and subsequent observance. **門 is where calamity becomes a threshold.** This also gives the firstborn material in chapter 13 a home rather than treating it as an appendix to the escape. The Exodus introduction’s major divisions similarly recognize the joins at 7:8, 13:17, and 15:22. ([USCCB][5])

The repeated **工** should remain exactly the same sign. Exodus 35 explicitly emphasizes willing contributions and the work of skilled women and men. The contrast is not *work versus no work*, but different relationships to work and service. ([Oremus Bible Browser][6])

# 申 — Deuteronomy

The book’s movement is not simply “Moses gives laws.” I would articulate it as **memory → allegiance → institutions → responsibility → choice → transmission → limit**. Your chapter summaries supply each of those changes.

**比** uses the literary reading **bì**, “adjacent/close,” as in **比鄰**, neighbors—not merely the familiar **bǐ**, “compare.” Its old graphic pairing of people is an unusually economical emblem for the difficult business of living alongside others. ([Revised Mandarin Dictionary][8])

**土** deserves its short section. The firstfruits declaration turns the long history into something a person can say while holding the land’s produce. At 26:16, the text changes into the declaration of obligation that opens the next movement. ([ESV Bible][9])

And **止** begins at **32:48**, where Moses is told to ascend and see the land he will not enter. That lets the next book begin with the answer:

**止 → 入**
**The limit → entry.** ([Bible Gateway][10])

**內** then gives the book an ending more revealing than “Benjamin.” The violence has come inside. The final sign changes how one reads the earlier victories.

# 王 — Samuel

Samuel should remain one book for this purpose. I would not let the division between 1 and 2 Samuel cut Saul’s death away from its lament, or obscure the long movement from hearing a voice to establishing—and damaging—a royal house.

For **4:1a/b**, the cut is after the sentence about Samuel’s word reaching Israel and before the account of Israel going out to battle. That makes the end of 耳 an outward movement: the child who learned to hear now has a word that others hear. ([Bible Gateway][12])

**匚 is an especially good radical here.** It is a two-stroke container sign, not a boat sign. CUHK also documents the depicted vessel’s use for ancestral tablets in a ritual setting. That gives it a real Chinese sacred-container association without claiming any historical relationship between the Chinese vessel and the Israelite Ark. ([Humanum][13])

**冊** has an optional, rather dark Japanese pun: **冊**, books or written documents, and **殺**, killing, share the on-reading **satsu**. A book of records ending in a lethal census can carry that secondary echo without changing the sign’s primary meaning. ([Kanjipedia][14])

# 主 — Kings

Kings needs room for the competing sequences within it: royal succession, sacred construction, accumulating wealth, prophetic authority, dynastic killing, reform, and the destruction of both kingdoms. But destruction is not quite its last movement.

There are three particularly important connections here.

**First, 耳 → 心.** Samuel begins with hearing; Solomon asks for a *listening heart*. 心 is therefore not generic “wisdom” decoration. The language of hearing has moved into the king’s capacity to judge. ([USCCB][15])

**Second, 宀 + 示 → 宗.** This is not merely a modern visual trick. 宗 is historically associated with an ancestral sacred house, composed of the roof/house element and 示. ([Humanum][16])

That joins the section emblems to an actual biblical wordplay: in 2 Samuel 7, **house** moves among dwelling, sacred building, and dynasty. Samuel gives us **宀, The House**; Kings gives us **宗, The House Built**. The same problem has become architecture. ([Bible Gateway][17])

**Third, 亡 → 存.** I would insist on preserving this tiny final microbook.

The last four verses are not another description of Jerusalem’s destruction. A person leaves prison, changes clothes, and receives continuing food. **存** means surviving or remaining, and Chinese sources explicitly contrast it with 亡. ([Humanum][18])

This is **survival, not restored sovereignty**. Ending with only 亡 would erase a real change in the text’s final posture. ([esv.org][19])

# What makes this a single system

The strongest improvement is not the reduction in the number of headings. It is that **the contents now make arguments through recurrence**.

**分** first divides peoples and languages. It later distinguishes bodies, apportions spoils, splits a kingdom, and excludes members of a recovered community. The sign remains “division”; what changes is whether division creates order, distributes goods, or destroys belonging.

**冊** repeatedly turns people into a written order: the first census, the replacement generation, the reckoning at the end of Samuel, and the registered community in Nehemiah. A list is not treated as empty space between stories. It is one of the ways the history constructs a people.

**衣** lets authority and identity travel between bodies. **田** distinguishes land organized as an inheritance from **土**, land as earth and possession. **工** returns with a changed relationship to service. **文** moves from a witness entrusted to the future to a recovered text that judges the present.

Those are readings proposed by the table, not extra dictionary meanings smuggled into the characters.

The meaningful boundaries do similar work. **Creation keeps its rest. The sea keeps its song. Deborah’s prose keeps its poem. Saul’s death keeps its lament. Moses’s limit answers Joshua’s entrance. Judah’s destruction does not swallow Jehoiachin’s survival.**

I would keep the occasional larger character where the meaning pays for it. **客** earns its nine strokes through the mirrored hospitality crises; **易** earns eight through Jacob’s economy of substitutions; **骨** earns ten because *remains whose journey is unfinished* is more precise than simply “death.” Across the whole proposal, **80 of the 115 section signs use five strokes or fewer; 101 use six or fewer**.

For navigation, the old chapter and verse numbers should remain underneath this new layer. The JSON gives each microbook a stable ID, since recurring signs must not be forced into unique identifiers. Your current `master.tex` defines chapter destinations; the intra-chapter divisions will need their own targets rather than links to nonexistent verse anchors.  

**The whole history begins by making a world and ends by keeping someone alive.**

**天 → 人 … 王 → 亡 → 存**

That is the ending I would preserve.
