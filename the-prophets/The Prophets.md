
Yes. I found a much better core bibliography than the piecemeal recommendations I gave before. There is actually a classic monograph devoted to exactly this subject that should be your starting point:

Thomas Jemielity, Satire and the Hebrew Prophets (1992).

This is probably the single book you want most. Jemielity approaches Hebrew prophecy explicitly as satire: ridicule, parody, grotesque imagery, obscenity, humiliation, reversal, attacks on institutions, attacks on rival prophets, and the prophet's strange relationship to his own persona. Hosea, Amos, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Micah and Jonah all figure prominently. He compares prophetic rhetoric with actual literary satirists rather than treating the funny bits as incidental ornament. 

Even better, Jemielity later condensed and refined the argument into “Ancient Biblical Satire,” pp. 13–30 of A Companion to Satire. That chapter is freely readable in the publisher excerpt and is an excellent 18-page test drive. He calls Hosea perhaps the most thoroughly and searingly ironic biblical text, talks about Jeremiah making himself the victim of a divine con/seduction, and treats prophetic obscenity and humiliation as structural features of satire.

For Amos, Domeris is particularly worth opening immediately:

[Read Domeris, “Shades of Irony in the Anti-Language of Amos”](https://hts.org.za/index.php/HTS/article/view/3292/7794?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

His thesis is more interesting than “Amos occasionally uses sarcasm.” He says Amos creates an alternative linguistic reality for an opposition group. The rich are not neutrally described; they're caricatured as grotesque cows reclining on ivory beds, guzzling bowls of wine. Rival religion isn't impartially reported; its language is mangled, mocked and renamed. And when opponents are allowed to speak, they frequently convict themselves with their own dialogue. 

For Ezekiel, I'd go straight here:

[Read Arthur, “Satire and Subversion in the Oracles of Ezekiel”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/375897176_Satire_and_Subversion_in_the_Oracles_of_Ezekiel?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Arthur explicitly says Ezekiel takes conventional wisdom forms—which normally enforce prudence and conformity—and weaponizes them to destroy “debased institutions, delusional cult ideology, and aggrandizing historical narrative.” That's remarkably close to the phenomenon we've been circling: the grotesqueness isn't a regrettable wrapper around the theological content; the grotesqueness is the weapon. 

For Joel, Kynes is especially useful methodologically:

[Kynes, “Beat Your Parodies into Swords…”](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A00fa3f8d-226f-4a23-af40-ea8bbd0819a2?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

He makes an important distinction I think will help our project: parody needn't mean making fun of the source text. A later author can quote an earlier text, reverse it, and use the resulting distorted quotation as a weapon against somebody else. Joel:

> swords → plowshares



becomes

> plowshares → swords



The Isaiah/Micah text isn't necessarily the butt of the joke; its recognizable language supplies the ammunition.

Nahum is worth moving much higher on your list than I initially suggested. A substantial portion of the book is essentially victory trash-talk against Assyria: fake mourning, sarcastic instructions to prepare defenses that won't help, formerly terrifying imperial lions suddenly missing, the invincible metropolis exposed and humiliated. Scholarship explicitly identifies multiple sections as taunt-songs functioning as satire. 

The five I'd read first

For what you're specifically chasing, I'd order them:

Jemielity → Arthur on Ezekiel → Domeris on Amos → Marcus → Weisman.

Then Kynes because he gives us an extremely useful tool for detecting something beyond ordinary sarcasm: look for a famous traditional formulation that has been minutely altered or reversed.

That last technique may turn out to be huge in the prophets. It gives us something analogous to what we've been doing with textual parallelism in Job: instead of assuming a strange formulation is merely awkward, ask whether the author has made it strange because the audience is supposed to hear another text underneath it.
