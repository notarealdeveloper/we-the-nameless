------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:2–3

------------------------------------------------------------------------

How do you offer help without power?

How do you save with an arm lacking strength?

How do you give counsel without wisdom,

Imparting so much sage knowledge?

---

Footnote 26:2–3. The singular address must belong to a companion, not to Job, who addresses his companions in the plural.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:4

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Whose words are you revealing?

Whose breath comes forth from you:

---

Footnote 26:4. An introduction to direct discourse, in this case a parody of the words of the spirit, quoted by Job in 4:17–19. Eliphaz had done the same in 15:14–16.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:5–6

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The shades of the dead writhe

Under the sea and its dwellers.

Sheol is naked before him,

Abaddon has no garment to cover it.

---

Footnote 26:5–6. The netherworld (see the next line) is located here beneath the sea. Compare Isaiah 26:19, where the “shades” “dwell” in the dust.

Footnote 26:5–6. The realm of oblivion, another name for the netherworld; see also Job 28:22.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:7–8

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He stretches Mount Zaphon o’er the Chaos,

He suspends the earth over nothing at all.

He bundles up water in his clouds,

And none bursts under its weight.

---

Footnote 26:7–8. The Mount Olympus of Canaanite mythology; see Psalm 48:3, where Jerusalem, seat of Israel’s God, is identified with the seat of the Canaanite pantheon. The verb “stretch” is conventionally used of the sky (as in Job 9:8), but it is the land, not the sky, that is set over the watery chaos, and the context here makes a reference to land fairly clear.

Footnote 26:7–8. The watery chaos of Genesis 1:2.

Footnote 26:7–8. Compare Proverbs 30:4.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:9–10

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He encloses the face of the new moon,

He spreads over it his clouds.

He draws a border round the face of the water,

At the limit where light meets darkness.

---

Footnote 26:9–10. Compare later Aramaic usage in the sense of “to shut.”

Footnote 26:9–10. The surface; compare the surface of the water in the following couplet.

Footnote 26:9–10. Reading *keseh* with no change in spelling; see Psalm 81:4. Ancient translations and most moderns read “throne,” but that word is properly spelled *ks’* (not *ksh*), and the sense of “throne” is inapt in context; compare the image in Job 9:7. Traditional commentators interpret “throne” figuratively as the heavens, the throne of the deity.

Footnote 26:9–10. The well-known Hebrew verb *paras* (compare Psalm 105:39) has a superfluous *z* at the end. Perhaps a stray letter, like a *w* from a spelling *pwrs*, was mistakenly inserted here; or there is contamination from the *z* at the end of *m’ḥz* in the preceding line.

Footnote 26:9–10. Compare Job 9:7.

Footnote 26:9–10. An act from the time of creation; see Proverbs 8:27.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:11

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The pillars supporting the sky shudder,

Stunned by the blast of his rebuke.

---

Footnote 26:11. An Aramaism; the verb *r-p-p* translates the “trembling” of the “pillars” in the Aramaic translation to Job 9:6.

Footnote 26:11. The noun “rebuke” is used of the deity’s primordial suppression of the sea and issues from a “blast of his nostrils”; see Psalm 18:16.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:12–13

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Using his power he aroused and stilled the sea,

And using his skill he smote Rahab;

Using his wind he put Yamm in his net;

His hand pierced the Elusive Serpent.

---

Footnote 26:12–13. The verb is artfully ambiguous. Contrast *raga‘* in Isaiah 51:15 and Jeremiah 31:34 (“arouse”) with *heraga‘* in Jeremiah 47:6 (“be stilled”).

Footnote 26:12–13. An Israelite name for the primeval sea monster; see Job 9:13.

Footnote 26:12–13. The Canaanite sea god and sea monster of Israelite mythology; see Job 3:8; 7:12; 38:8–9.

Footnote 26:12–13. The Hebrew is unintelligible as written. Following Tur-Sinai, many now divide the word *shamayim*, “sky,” in the middle and read: *sam yam sipro*, “he put Yamm into his net,” which has a precise parallel in the scene of combat between the Babylonian storm-god Marduk and the old sea goddess Ti’awat in the epic myth *Enuma Elish*. The Hebrew *sipro* “his net” reflects the Babylonian word for “net,” *saparru*, which is cognate to Hebrew *siprah* “bag” in Psalm 56:9 and perhaps the word *shaprir* “net(?)” in Jeremiah 43:10.

Footnote 26:12–13. Literally, “fleeing”; it is an archaic epithet of the primordial sea monster in Canaanite (Ugaritic) mythology and in Isaiah 27:1.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 26:14

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Now these are only the outskirts of his ways.

Yet what hint of a word do we hear of them?

Who can apprehend his mighty thunder?

---

Footnote 26:14. Compare Job 28:24.

Footnote 26:14. Like the tip of the iceberg.

Footnote 26:14. More literally, “But what hint of a word is heard of it?”

Footnote 26:14. Thunder is often understood as the voice of the deity; for example Psalm 18:14.
