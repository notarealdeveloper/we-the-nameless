

# יאמר A

Yes. If the goal is **“take the worst wreck in Biblical Hebrew and attack it as though the Masoretes had never existed”**, I would start with **Job 24**.

There is no objective metric for “hardest chapter in the Bible,” but my shortlist is:

|Rank|Chapter|Why it is a monster|
|---|---|---|
|**1**|**Job 24**|Vocabulary, syntax, unidentified subjects, apparent speaker/theological discontinuities, uncertain word division, and a final third so obscure that NJPS famously warns that 24:18–25 is largely conjectural. Gordis called the chapter “extremely difficult”; Duhm called its text essentially hopeless; surveys of scholarship record a remarkable number of proposed relocations and deletions just for vv.5–12. ([SciELO](https://www.scielo.org.za/scielo.php?pid=S1010-99192014000300012&script=sci_arttext "The fate of undesirables (Job 24:5-12)"))|
|**2**|**Job 30**|A concentrated minefield of hapaxes, rare roots, unusual imagery, and uncertain textual details. It may actually beat 24 lexically, but its _overall discourse_ is easier to recover: Job is describing his present humiliation. ([Free Bible Commentary](https://www.freebiblecommentary.org/old_testament_studies/VOL09AOT/VOL09AOT_30.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com "JOB 30"))|
|**3**|**Job 41**|Leviathan produces a spectacular pile of rare zoological/body-part vocabulary, hapaxes, and verses whose ancient versions already disagree. But we know what the poem is broadly doing, which gives us more error-correcting redundancy than Job 24. ([NET Bible](https://classic.net.bible.org/bible.php/d/xml/bible.php?book=Job&chapter=41&utm_source=chatgpt.com "NETBible: Job 41"))|

Job 19:25–27 is arguably competitive for **hardest three consecutive verses**, but not hardest entire chapter. ([SBL Site](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/SPPJob.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Job Book 1.indb"))

So: **Job 24 wins.**

And something interesting happens when we apply your algorithm. I think the chapter becomes **considerably more coherent than the standard reconstruction suggests**, especially at the supposedly impossible end.

# 0. Rules of the experiment

I'm deliberately separating two things.

The **archaeological stream** gets no vowels, accents, punctuation, or spaces. For Ketiv/Qere differences I privilege the **written consonants**, because our imaginary excavators don't have the Masoretic reading tradition.

For the lossy ASCII layer:

ש/ס → s  
ח/ה → h  
ע → 3  
א → '  
צ → S  
ט → T  
ק → q

I am _not_ collapsing ṣ/q/ṭ into s/k/t because that would destroy extremely valuable comparative-Semitic information. We can forget those distinctions mentally when testing a wild segmentation, but restore them when asking whether an Akkadian/Ugaritic cognate exists.

Also, when a perfectly ordinary Hebrew root has no useful external cognate for our purposes, I write — rather than manufacture one.

The transmitted Hebrew text itself is WLC/MT. ([Mechon Mamre](https://mechon-mamre.org/p/pt/pt2724.htm "Job 24 / Hebrew - English Bible / Mechon-Mamre"))

The question launching the chapter is therefore: **Where the hell are God's scheduled interventions?**

# 6. Job 24:6 — first major payoff

**Raw:** בשדהבלילויקצירווכרםרשעילקשו  
**Lossy:** bsdhblylwyqSyrwwkrmrs3ylqsw

MT segments:

בשדה | בלילו | יקצורו

But our excavator sees:

BSDHBLYLWYQṢRW

And one extremely natural segmentation is:

**בשדה | בלי לו | יקצרו**

|Hebrew original / candidate|Lossy|Comparative evidence|Best English|
|---|---|---|---|
|בַּשָּׂדֶה|bsdh|ŠD “field” (NW Sem.)|field|
|בְּלִילוֹ → בְּלִי לוֹ|blylw|BL(Y) “without/not” (NW Sem.)|another's|
|יִקְצוֹרוּ|yqSwrw|QṢR “harvest” (Uga/Arm)|reap|
|כֶרֶם|krm|KRM “vineyard” (Uga/Phn)|vineyard|
|רָשָׁע|rs3|RŠʿ (Arm/NW Sem.)|wicked|
|יְלַקֵּשׁוּ|ylqsw|LQŠ, late gathering/late crop|glean|

### Reconstruction

**“In a field not their own they reap; a wicked man's vineyard they glean.”**

This is better than **“they reap his fodder”**, to my ear and structurally.

And we didn't emend a consonant. We merely ignored the traditional boundary/vocalization.

Ancient versions were already reading something equivalent to **“a field that is not theirs.”** Modern notes acknowledge that בלילו is problematic and that the early versions effectively parse it as בלי לו. ([NET Bible](https://classic.net.bible.org/bible.php?book=Job&chapter=24&theme=wiki "NETBible: Job 24"))

**First real win for the algorithm.**

# 12. Job 24:12 — second major payoff

**Raw:** מעירמתיםינאקונפשחלליםתשועואלהלאישיםתפלה  
**Lossy:** m3yrmtymyn'qwnpshllymtsw3w'lhl'ysymtplh

|Hebrew original|Lossy|Comparative evidence|Best English|
|---|---|---|---|
|מֵעִיר|m3yr|ʿYR “city” (Uga/Phn)|city|
|מְתִים|mtym|MWT “die” (Uga); _mātu_ (Akk)|dying|
|יִנְאָקוּ|yn'qw|NʾQ, groan|groan|
|נֶפֶשׁ|nps|NPŠ (Uga/Phn); _napištu_ (Akk)|throat|
|חֲלָלִים|hllym|ḤLL “slain/wounded”|wounded|
|תְּשַׁוֵּעַ|tsw3|ŠWʿ, cry for help|screams|
|אֱלוֹהַּ|'lwh|ʾL (Uga/Phn)|God|
|יָשִׂים|ysym|ŠYM (Uga/Arm); cf. Akk _šāmu_|registers|
|תִּפְלָה|tplh|TPL, tasteless/absurd/wrong|wrong|

### Reconstruction

**“From the city the dying groan; the throat of the wounded screams for help—yet God does not register it as wrong.”**

I strongly prefer this to the popular emendation:

> “God does not hear the prayer.”

That emendation requires turning both **ישים → ישמע** and **תפלה → תפילה**.

The consonantal text already makes excellent sense.

And there is a fantastic Job-internal reason to keep it: in **Job 1:22**, Job did not assign תפלה to God. Here **God does not assign תפלה to the atrocity in front of him**.

The same strange word becomes a possible bookend:

**Job didn't charge God with WRONG.  
Now Job observes that God won't charge the world with WRONG.**

Modern notes acknowledge both possibilities but concede that the MT construction “impute/charge wrongdoing” is workable. ([NET Bible](https://classic.net.bible.org/bible.php?book=Job&chapter=24&theme=wiki "NETBible: Job 24"))

This may be intentional rather than corruption.

# 18. Job 24:18 — the hinge

Here we enter the passage where translations start flying apart.

**Raw:** קלהואעלפנימיםתקללחלקתםבארץלאיפנהדרךכרמים  
**Lossy:** qlhw'3lpnymymtqllhlqtmb'rSl'ypnhdrkkrmym

MT traditionally produces:

> “He is swift/light on the face of the waters; their portion is cursed in the land; he does not turn toward the vineyards.”

But look at the consonantal repetition:

**QL … TQLL**

The first word unquestionably means **light/swift/slight**.

Why immediately decide that the second occurrence of **QLL** suddenly means **curse**?

**“Light as something on the water, he passes; his holding on earth dwindles; he never returns by the vineyard road.”**

This is speculative—but **interesting speculative**, because it immediately solves the alleged theological discontinuity.

> **“Even this guy eventually becomes weightless and disappears from his estate.”**

The image anticipates vv.19–20: **death erases him**.

Traditional scholarship has struggled enormously here; Le Clerc reportedly regarded 24:18 as among the most obscure passages in Scripture, and modern translations oscillate among “swift,” “foam,” curses and conjectural restructuring. I would give this re-reading **medium confidence**, but it has a very high explanatory payoff.

# 21. Job 24:21 — third major payoff

**Raw:** רעהעקרהלאתלדואלמנהלאייטיב

**רֹעֶה עֲקָרָה**

literally something like:

But strip the vowels and we have:

**RʿH**

The parallel colon says:

> widow — **he does not do GOOD**

So the opposite member should almost certainly be:

> barren woman — **he does BAD**

# The recovered chapter

Now concatenate the semantic output and ignore traditional chapter headings:

> **Why doesn't Shaddai reserve times? Why don't those who know him see his days?**
> 
> People shift boundaries. They steal flocks and openly pasture them. They drive away the orphan's donkey and seize the widow's ox. They force the needy off the road until the poor hide.
> 
> Look at them—human onagers in the wilderness, going out at dawn hunting food. The wasteland has become bread for their children. They reap fields that aren't theirs and glean the wicked man's vineyard. They sleep naked and have no blanket against the cold. Mountain runoff soaks them; without shelter they hug rocks.
> 
> An orphan is torn from the breast; collateral is seized from the poor. Naked and hungry people carry other people's sheaves. They press oil and tread winepresses—and remain thirsty.
> 
> From the city the dying groan. The throats of the wounded scream.
> 
> **And God doesn't register it as wrong.**
> 
> Then there are the people who hate daylight. Murderers, adulterers and burglars organize their lives around darkness. Morning is darkness to them because daylight exposes them.
> 
> Yet even these people are light things floating away on water. Their earthly holding dwindles and they never return to their vineyards. As heat steals snow-water, Sheol eventually steals sinners. Mothers forget them; maggots enjoy them; memory of them disappears. Their injustice eventually snaps.
> 
> But look what happens **before** that. They abuse barren women and never help widows. They overpower even strong men. They rise in power. **Security is given them, and they rest in it, while God's eyes are on everything they do.**
> 
> Eventually they rise, briefly, and disappear. They collapse **like everybody else**. They fold up and wither like a head of grain.
> 
> **If that's not what happens, prove me wrong.**

That is, to me, **substantially more coherent than the usual chapter.**

# What I think actually happened to Job 24

My provisional reconstruction is that **the text is less corrupt than its reputation**.

The real problem is a combination of three things.

First, **Masoretic vocalization occasionally chooses the wrong member of a polysemous consonantal family**. The standout candidates are:

| MT-style reading             | Bottom-up reading                | Why                                                       |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| בלילו = “his fodder”         | **בלי לו** = “not his”           | word-boundary change; ancient versions; excellent context |
| משֹד = “from violence”       | **משַד** = “from the breast”     | orphan + snatching + known meaning                        |
| תפלה → emended “prayer”      | **תפלה = wrongdoing**            | requires **zero** consonantal change and echoes 1:22      |
| רֹעה = “pastures/preys upon” | **רע = harms**                   | parallel is “does not do good”                            |
| תקֻלל = “is cursed”          | **Q-L-L = becomes light/slight** | immediately echoes preceding קל; restores mortality image |

Second, the poem relies heavily on **parallelism to supply deleted semantic material**. Job 24:19 is the cleanest demonstration:

**drought : snow-water :: Sheol : sinners**

The verb **GZL / steal** need only be written once.

Third—and this is the big one—the traditional interpretation confuses:

**“wicked people eventually die”**

with

**“wicked people receive divine punishment.”**

Job's entire argument depends on those being different statements.

Verse 24 practically screams the distinction:

> **ככל — LIKE EVERYONE ELSE.**

Death is not evidence that cosmic justice worked. It's just death.

That interpretation also means we **do not need** one of the most drastic standard rescue operations: moving 24:18–24 into Zophar's missing speech or treating it as Job quoting the friends. Scholars have resorted to such rearrangements precisely because the passage seems incompatible with Job's position; NET notes this explicitly, while the detailed study of vv.5–12 records roughly fifteen proposed shifts/deletions just within that smaller unit. ([NET Bible](https://classic.net.bible.org/bible.php?book=Job&chapter=24&theme=wiki "NETBible: Job 24"))

The ancient Greek is useful as a witness to how early the trouble began: it already diverges dramatically in this chapter, particularly around vv.5–13 and 18–24. ([Bible Hub](https://biblehub.com/sepd/job/24.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Job 24 Swete's Septuagint"))

## My confidence ranking

**Very high:** vv.1–4, 7–8, 10, 13–17, 19, 25.

**High:** משד = breast in v.9; oil-making in v.11; תפלה = wrongdoing in v.12; **harm** rather than “pasture” in v.21.

**Medium-high:** בלי לו in v.6.

**Medium:** vv.22–24 as a continuous statement about the tyrant's power/security followed by completely ordinary mortality.

**Low-to-medium but extremely interesting:** reading the QLL complex of v.18 as **light/slight/fading** rather than suddenly switching from קל = light to תקלל = cursed.

And that last one is exactly where I would dig next, because **24:18 is probably the single best verse in the chapter for your algorithm**. We can take

throw away **every inherited word boundary and every Masoretic assumption**, generate all linguistically plausible segmentations against Ugaritic/Akkadian/Aramaic/Phoenician lexica, score them using the parallel structure of **24:18–20**, and see whether something substantially cleaner falls out.
