# Genesis 38:7 and the Unnamed Wickedness of Er

## What the Hebrew actually says

Genesis 38:7 reads:

> וַיְהִי עֵר בְּכוֹר יְהוּדָה רַע בְּעֵינֵי יְהֹוָה וַיְמִתֵהוּ יְהֹוָה׃

Your rendering,

> “And Er, Judah’s firstborn, was bad in YHWH’s eyes, and YHWH killed him,”

is quite deliberately literal. בְּעֵינֵי means “in the eyes of”; רַע is “bad/evil/wicked”; and וַיְמִתֵהוּ is the Hiphil form of מות, literally causative, “and he caused him to die,” idiomatically “and [he] killed/put him to death.” The following יְהֹוָה supplies the subject, so there is no ambiguity about the narrator's claim that YHWH is the agent of Er's death. An interlinear accordingly parses וַיְמִתֵהוּ as Hiphil and glosses it simply “and killed him.” citeturn15view0

“Bad” is lexically defensible for רַע, although “evil” or “wicked” is more idiomatic English when the word predicates moral character. Likewise, your “and YHWH killed him” mirrors the Hebrew conjunction more closely than translations that render the second waw as “so.” The causal connection is obvious in context, but the Hebrew itself gives a sequence: Er was רַע בעיני יהוה, **and** YHWH killed him. English versions consequently divide between “and” and the interpretive “so.” citeturn15view0turn17view5

There is one important qualification to the line “no one knows why.” **Genesis does tell us why at the most general level:** Er was רַע בְּעֵינֵי יהוה, “bad/evil in YHWH's eyes.” What Genesis never says is **what Er did that made him bad**. A translation commentary puts the distinction bluntly: “There is no way of knowing what Er did” that displeased YHWH. citeturn15view1

That textual silence is conspicuous because the very next case is different. Genesis 38:9 tells the reader what Onan does and why he does it; 38:10 then says the deed was evil in YHWH's eyes. Er receives no corresponding description. The later biblical genealogy in 1 Chronicles 2:3 merely repeats that Er was evil/displeasing to YHWH and was killed; it supplies no missing offense. Numbers 26:19 is terser still, simply recording the deaths of Er and Onan in Canaan. citeturn17view0

So I would tighten your emphatic formulation to something like:

> **No one knows from the biblical text what Er actually did.**

That is stronger historically than “no one knows why,” because later interpreters certainly *claimed* to know or infer what he had done.

### The ער / רע reversal

Your visual observation is correct: the consonantal spelling of **Er**, עֵר / ער, consists of ayin–resh, while **bad/evil**, רַע / רע, consists of resh–ayin. They are the same two consonants in reverse order. This is a striking piece of wordplay in precisely the sentence where Er is characterized as רע. citeturn15view0turn15view6

One small terminological point: a footnote such as “Er, lit. ער” is not really a *literal translation*; ער is simply the Hebrew spelling of the proper name. Likewise רע is the spelling of the adjective translated “bad.” “Heb. ער” and “Heb. רע” would be more exact labels.

The reversal also turns out to matter a great deal for the historical survey below, because **Adam Clarke was nowhere near the first commentator to notice it**.

## The Jewish interpretive tradition

The rabbinic explanation in **Babylonian Talmud, Yevamot 34b**, followed explicitly by Rashi, is substantially as you describe it. The Gemara reasons backward from Genesis 38:10, where God kills Onan “also”: the shared death suggests a shared transgression. The talmudic discussion characterizes Er and Onan's sexual conduct as intercourse “in an atypical manner” and asks why Er would have acted that way when Onan's stated motive did not apply to him. Its answer is that Er wished to prevent Tamar's pregnancy so pregnancy would not diminish her beauty. citeturn16search20turn16search26

**Rashi on Genesis 38:7** makes the tradition especially explicit. He identifies Er's wrongdoing with Onan's and says Er acted so that Tamar would not bear children and “her beauty thereby become impaired.” Your paraphrase—

> Er's wickedness was like Onan's wickedness; he deliberately prevented Tamar from becoming pregnant so pregnancy would not spoil her beauty

—is therefore a fair representation of Rashi and of the rabbinic tradition Rashi cites. citeturn17view0

There is a useful nuance, however. Your wording makes the rabbinic theory sound like a straightforward report of a fact omitted from Genesis. In **Yevamot 34b**, it is actually an exegetical deduction: Onan was killed “also,” therefore his offense is inferred to resemble Er's; Er's motive is then supplied to explain the inferred conduct. The chain of reasoning is later interpretation, not information stated in Genesis 38:7 itself. citeturn16search20turn16search26

**Nachmanides/Ramban on Genesis 38:7** is an excellent citation for your second item. He expressly says, “Scripture does not specify the nature of his wickedness,” in contrast to the account of Onan. He then argues that Scripture's point in mentioning Er's guilt is to establish that Er “died for his own sin,” rather than that his death was divine punishment inflicted upon Judah for Judah's part in selling Joseph. That is essentially exactly what your paraphrase says. citeturn17view0

Your **Sforno** attribution needs correction. Sforno comments on the phrase “in the eyes of God” by saying Er “was not evil to his fellow human beings.” That can support an inference that his offense was of a private or God-directed sort, but Sforno does **not**, in the text located, explicitly say that the act was hidden from everybody and known only to God. citeturn15view2

That stronger formulation belongs much more nearly to **Radak (David Kimhi)** on the same verse. Radak explains “in the eyes of YHWH” on the ground that nobody else knew of Er's offense, which occurred in the privacy of his relations with Tamar; he then cites the rabbinic explanation that Er avoided impregnating her in order to preserve her beauty. So this sentence in your draft—

> “his crime was apparently hidden from other people but known to God”

should preferably be attributed to **Radak**, not Sforno. Sforno can be retained separately with the narrower claim that Er was not wicked toward other human beings. citeturn15view2

**Chizkuni** gives another version of the sexual explanation, stating that Er did not have intercourse with Tamar in a way that could result in conception. This illustrates how a gap consisting of only a few uninformative Hebrew words generated quite detailed reconstructions in medieval exegesis. citeturn15view2

The Jewish part of your passage can therefore be made considerably more precise:

> **Bavli Yevamot 34b / Rashi:** Er committed essentially the same reproductive/sexual offense as Onan, motivated by a desire to preserve Tamar's beauty.  
> **Ramban:** Scripture never identifies Er's particular offense; the point is that Er died for his own sin, not for Judah's treatment of Joseph.  
> **Sforno:** “in YHWH's eyes” indicates that Er was not wicked toward other people.  
> **Radak:** the phrase indicates an offense unknown to other people and committed privately with Tamar. citeturn17view0turn15view2

## Ancient and early-modern Christian interpretations

Your broad heading “Christian commentators, ancient–early modern” needs some qualification, because the tradition was not uniform.

A particularly valuable counterexample is **Augustine, *Contra Faustum* 22.84**. Augustine does not identify Er's offense with Onan's sexual act. In his allegorical reading of the Judah-Tamar narrative, he describes one son—Er—as “evil or cruel before the Lord,” while distinguishing Onan as the son who spilled seed on the ground. Augustine's immediate interest is typological: the two sons symbolize two sorts of bad rulers, those who injure and those who withhold good. citeturn20view2turn20view3

That matters because **Cornelius à Lapide**, writing in the early seventeenth century, explicitly knows the disagreement. In his commentary on Genesis 38:7 he says that “Both Jews and Christians agree that Er as well as Onan sinned” sexually, but then specifically rejects Augustine's position that Er's wrong was cruelty and argues instead for lust and withdrawal during intercourse. Lapide explains Er's motive in essentially the rabbinic manner: preserving Tamar's beauty and therefore his sexual enjoyment. citeturn17view3

Lapide therefore gives you an excellent source for the early-modern form of your “same sexual offense as Onan” claim, but his own discussion proves that it would be too strong to call that an undifferentiated **ancient Christian** consensus. Augustine represents a visibly different line. A safer sentence would be:

> **Some early-modern Christian exegetes identified Er's offense with Onan's sexual conduct, while other Christian interpretations differed; Augustine, for example, construed Er as injurious or cruel.** citeturn20view3turn17view3

Lapide is also devastating to the comic chronology in your Adam Clarke passage. Long before Clarke, Lapide remarks that “Er in Hebrew by metathesis becomes *ra*”—that is, ער becomes רע by transposition. citeturn17view3

And Lapide was not alone. **John Trapp**, likewise well before Clarke, explicitly calls the Hebrew feature an *inversio nominis*, an “inversion of the name.” citeturn17view5

So the dramatic passage about “finally, after several thousand years” Adam Clarke discovering the spelling trick works as comedy, but **not as intellectual history**. The observation is demonstrably attested at least in early-seventeenth-century Christian commentary, roughly two centuries before Clarke wrote.

## The Protestant commentary trail

The later English Protestant material in your draft is unusually verifiable. Most of the quotations are very close to the sources, although several attributions or dates need adjustment.

### John Trapp

Your Trapp material is essentially exact. On Genesis 38:7 he writes:

> “A Sodomite, say the Hebrews; but this is hard to say.”

He nevertheless treats Er as extraordinarily wicked and later says he was “Too wicked … to live,” connecting Er's character disparagingly with his Canaanite mother. In the same note Trapp explicitly observes the Hebrew “inversion” of Er's name. citeturn17view5

The date label “1650s” is a little late if intended as the date of the commentary's first appearance. Modern bibliographic descriptions date Trapp's commentary collection to **1649**, though seventeenth-century volumes and editions appeared over a span of years. “John Trapp (commentary first published 1649)” would therefore be safer than “1650s.” citeturn18search16

This source also means that Clarke cannot be portrayed as the first English Protestant to notice ער / רע: Trapp had already done so in the mid-seventeenth century. citeturn17view5

### Matthew Poole

Your Poole entry is almost verbatim. His note on Genesis 38:7 calls Er “notoriously wicked” and says that “The Lord slew him” in “some extraordinary and remarkable manner.” citeturn15view4

The dating “1680s” works as a publication date, with one caveat: Poole died in 1679, and his English *Annotations upon the Holy Bible* was completed by associates and published posthumously in **1685**. An original-edition catalog record confirms the 1685 publication date. citeturn18search1turn18search5

So a polished attribution would be:

> **Matthew Poole, *Annotations upon the Holy Bible* (published posthumously, 1685), on Gen 38:7.** citeturn18search5

### John Gill

Your Gill summary is also very accurate. Gill begins by saying Er “was guilty of some very heinous sin, but what is not mentioned.” He reports the Targum/Jarchi tradition identifying it with Onan's conduct and the motive of preserving Tamar's beauty, but importantly **does not endorse that identification without reservation**: Gill argues that if the offenses were identical, one might expect Scripture to have said so as explicitly as it does for Onan. citeturn17view4

Gill next cites an “Arabic writer”—his note identifies the source as **Abulpharagius**—who said Er had relations with Tamar in what Gill calls the “sodomitical” manner. Gill then proposes that YHWH may have killed Er either immediately or by a rapidly fatal illness sent as divine judgment. citeturn17view4

So your wording is good, but it can be more source-specific:

> Gill says the actual offense is **not mentioned**; he reports, rather than simply asserts, both the Jewish Onan-parallel and an Arabic tradition of “sodomitical” intercourse. citeturn17view4

Your date range **1748–1763** is correct for Gill's six-volume *Exposition of the Old Testament*. citeturn18search10turn18search30

### Adam Clarke

Clarke really does say:

> “What this wickedness consisted in we are not told”

and then remarks that the Hebrew expression for Er's wickedness is “his own name, the letters reversed”: ער / רע. citeturn15view6

Thus your quotation of Clarke is substantively accurate. His Bible commentary appeared over **1810–1826**, so “commentary 1810s” is reasonable shorthand, though “1810–1826” is bibliographically safer. citeturn18search3turn18search11

But the surrounding joke that Adam Clarke finally discovered the lost secret after millennia should be marked unmistakably as a joke. **Lapide had already made the same Er/ra metathesis observation in the early seventeenth century, and Trapp explicitly discussed the name's inversion in 1649-era commentary.** citeturn17view3turn17view5

Indeed, Lapide's version is almost startlingly close to Clarke's: Er, he says, becomes *ra* by metathesis, “evil/perverse.” So the historical trajectory is not “centuries of crime-hunting, then Clarke notices the letters”; the wordplay and crime-hunting were already coexisting long before Clarke. citeturn17view3

### Joseph Benson

Your Benson paraphrase is solid. Benson glosses Er's wickedness as living “in defiance of God, and his law” and interprets the premature, childless death as punishment. He explicitly contrasts long life as a divine blessing with untimely death as punishment. citeturn15view7

The bibliographic dating **1811–1818** is particularly well supported: Benson's five-volume *Holy Bible … with Critical, Explanatory, and Practical Notes* appeared in that period in its second edition. citeturn21search3turn21search5

### Daniel D. Whedon

The Whedon quotation is also genuine. On Genesis 38:7 he says:

> “In what particular forms he showed his wickedness we are not told”

and then speculates that as the son of a Canaanite woman Er probably absorbed “Canaanitish idolatry and vice” in childhood. He construes Er's death as a sudden or frightening death recognized as divine judgment. citeturn21search0

So your substantive paraphrase is accurate. I would be more cautious with the parenthetical “ca. 1870s” unless you decide exactly which edition of Whedon's commentary you intend to cite; the text itself is readily verifiable, but digitized bibliographic records for the multivolume commentary circulate in different nineteenth-century editions. The safest citation in the present draft is simply **Daniel D. Whedon, commentary on Genesis 38:7**. citeturn21search0

### Jamieson, Fausset & Brown

Here there is a genuine attribution problem.

JFB does indeed say that Judah, by marrying into a Canaanite family, was “casting off the restraints of religion,” and that it is unsurprising that the resulting family should become “infamous for bold and unblushing wickedness.” citeturn15view9

But that comment belongs to **their note on Genesis 38:2**, the account of Judah's Canaanite marriage. The JFB text located has **no separate commentary on Genesis 38:7** beyond printing the verse itself. Another indexed presentation explicitly says, “No JFB commentary on this verse.” citeturn15view9turn17view5

More importantly, I did **not** find your specific sentence that Er was “in all likelihood” addicted to the “abominable vices of Canaan” in JFB. That sounds like a conflation of JFB's comment on the family with the much closer Whedon speculation about Er personally imbibing “Canaanitish idolatry and vice.” citeturn21search0turn15view9

I would therefore revise this entry to:

> **Judah's Canaanite marriage involved, for JFB, “casting off the restraints of religion”; they regard the resulting family's “bold and unblushing wickedness” as unsurprising.**  
> —Robert Jamieson, A. R. Fausset & David Brown, *Commentary Critical and Explanatory on the Whole Bible* (1871), **on Gen 38:2**, not specifically 38:7. citeturn15view9turn21search2

The work's first publication in **1871** is independently confirmed by CCEL and other bibliographic descriptions. citeturn21search12turn21search2

### The “common Protestant interpretation”

As a synthesis, your characterization is fair with a caveat. A sequence of major English-language Protestant commentaries really does exhibit a repeated pattern: the text does not identify Er's concrete offense; the commentator nevertheless assumes that it was exceptionally serious because of the severity of the divine judgment, and sometimes supplements the gap with Jewish, sexual, or Canaanite speculation. Poole calls Er notoriously wicked; Gill calls the unknown offense heinous; Clarke says the offense is not told but must have been a great evil; Whedon says its particular form is unknown and then speculates about Canaanite influence. citeturn15view4turn17view4turn15view6turn21search0

What I would avoid is presenting

> “common Protestant interpretation (ca. 1600–1900)”

as though it were itself the title of a documented scholarly consensus. Better to identify it explicitly as **your synthesis of a recurrent interpretive pattern**, illustrated by Trapp, Poole, Gill, Clarke, Benson, and Whedon. citeturn17view5turn15view4turn17view4turn15view6turn15view7turn21search0

## The Robert Alter passage

The final passage is not an anonymous “comment on AR from someone named R.A.” It is **Robert Alter**.

The wording beginning,

> “The nature of [Er’s] moral failing remains unspecified”

is from Alter's commentary on Genesis 38:7. He then connects Er with Genesis's recurrent displacement or disqualification of firstborn sons and observes the narrative irony that Judah, after helping cause Jacob to mourn a supposedly dead son, now loses two sons himself. citeturn15view10turn15view11

The bibliographic identification is secure: **Robert Alter, *Genesis: Translation and Commentary*, W. W. Norton, 1997**. Google Books catalogs the 1997 Norton edition under precisely that title, with ISBN 039331670X; a contemporary reading guide likewise dates the Norton paperback to September 17, 1997. citeturn16search7turn16search16

Pagination appears to differ between editions or citations: one secondary transcription places the comment on p. 215, while other citations to the Norton edition use different pagination. The most robust citation for a text that may circulate in multiple editions is therefore:

> **Robert Alter, *Genesis: Translation and Commentary* (New York: W. W. Norton, 1997), comment on Genesis 38:7.** citeturn15view10turn16search7

Alter is especially valuable for your larger point because, unlike many earlier interpreters, he does not fill the gap with a conjectured sexual crime, idolatry, or private vice. He treats the unspecified character of Er's offense as an actual literary datum and shifts attention to the narrative pattern of firstborn displacement and to the ironic parallel between Jacob's bereavement and Judah's. citeturn15view10turn15view11

## Citation-ready assessment of the draft

The central claim of your passage survives the research very well, but I would formulate it carefully:

**Genesis itself never identifies Er's particular offense.** The narrator says only that Er was רַע בְּעֵינֵי יהוה and that YHWH killed him. Ramban explicitly observes the omission; Clarke says the wickedness is not told; Whedon says its particular forms are not told; Alter says the moral failing remains unspecified; a modern translation commentary says there is no way to know from the verse what Er did. citeturn15view0turn17view0turn15view6turn21search0turn15view10turn15view1

The history of interpretation is then, in effect, a history of attempts to fill that blank. The Bavli and Rashi infer that Er behaved like Onan so Tamar would not become pregnant and lose her beauty; Radak develops the idea of a private bedroom offense; Augustine reads Er's wickedness as injurious or cruel; Lapide argues for a sexual offense like Onan's; Trapp reports the “Sodomite” tradition while expressing doubt; Gill reviews several competing traditions; Whedon turns toward Canaanite moral influence. None of those reconstructions comes from an explicit statement of Er's act in Genesis 38:7. citeturn16search26turn17view0turn20view3turn17view3turn17view5turn17view4turn21search0

Three alterations to your draft are especially important.

First, change the **Sforno** item. Sforno says Er was not evil toward his fellow human beings; the claim that nobody else knew his private offense is much more precisely **Radak**. citeturn15view2

Second, qualify the **ancient Christian** generalization. Augustine is a concrete ancient counterexample to the idea that Christians simply identified Er's sin with Onan's sexual conduct; the explicit same-sexual-offense argument is securely documented in early-modern Lapide. citeturn20view3turn17view3

Third, the **Adam Clarke “discovery” is not historically original**. Clarke does indeed notice ער / רע, but Lapide had already written that Er becomes *ra* by metathesis, and Trapp had already described the Hebrew as an “inversion of the name.” The comic claim can stay as satire, but a factual footnote should acknowledge the earlier notices. citeturn17view3turn17view5turn15view6

The **JFB** paragraph also needs a smaller but significant repair: their genuine Canaanite-family comment is on **Genesis 38:2**, not a commentary on 38:7, and the phrase about Er personally being addicted to the “abominable vices of Canaan” was not found in their text. Whedon is the much closer source for that specific idea. citeturn15view9turn17view5turn21search0

> Genesis says that Er was evil in YHWH's eyes and that YHWH killed him, but **never says what Er did**.
