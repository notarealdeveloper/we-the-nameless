
Is there a plausible pun here where Esau's saying "Your name is 'Please god!' Say your name" and Jacob finally either says it or is forced to kneel (ברכ)

---

Yes. As a deliberate literary-pun reading, rather than historical etymology, I think you can construct something remarkably close to that. But one piece is much stronger than the others.

The sequence in the Hebrew is worth putting almost mechanically:

> “Release me.”
“I won't release you unless ברכתני.”
“What's your name?”
“Jacob.”
“Your name won't be Jacob anymore, but ישראל.”
...
וישאל Jacob — “and Jacob šʾl-ed...”
“הגידה־נא שמך — tell, please, your name.”
“Why תשאל concerning my name?”
ויברך אתו שם — and he brk-ed him there. 



There are two roots being conspicuously repeated around the naming:

שׁאל — ask / request / petition

and

ברך — bless / kneel

The latter ambiguity is real, not something we have to manufacture: the Qal of BRK means “kneel,” while the Piel is the normal “bless” verb. 

“Your name is Please-God”

Here's the attractive part.

As we just noticed, Northwest Semitic gives us:

*yišʾal ʾil
≈ “he asks El / he requests of El / he petitions El”

and Ugaritic actually has yšʾal.

Phonetically compare:

yišʾal ʾil
“he petitions El”

yiśrāʾēl
“Israel”

Not an etymology. But potentially excellent paronomasia.

If the pragmatic force of šʾl is supplication, then colloquial English “Please, God!” captures the joke beautifully, though it's deliberately freer than the literal “he petitions El.”

Now look at what the character renamed Israel immediately does:

> וַיִּשְׁאַל יַעֲקֹב



Literally, “And Jacob asked/requested...” 

That's striking because the narrator doesn't call him Israel there. Immediately after announcing “Jacob will no longer be your name; rather Israel,” the next sentence goes right back to:

> Jacob šʾl-ed.



If Israel is being made to echo yišʾal-El, that's almost a punchline:

> “You're not Jacob anymore. You're Yišʾal-El — Asks-God.”

And Jacob asked...



And what does Jacob say?

Even better:

הַגִּידָה־נָּא שְׁמֶךָ

The particle נא gives the request its polite/entreating force:

> “Please, tell your name.”



So the progression can be heard:

> “Your name is Please-God.”

Jacob: “Please, tell me your name.”



That's substantially better evidence for the proposed joke than merely deriving something vaguely Israel-ish from ŠʾL. The very next speech act actually is a request, explicitly marked with nāʾ.

Then comes BRK

And this is where your proposed punchline gets wonderfully nasty, although grammatically we need to distinguish the forms.

The final clause is:

וַיְבָרֶךְ אֹתוֹ שָׁם

wayḇāreḵ ʾōṯô šām.

Morphologically וַיְבָרֶךְ is Piel, so synchronically its straightforward lexical meaning is:

> “and he blessed him there.”



It does not grammatically mean “and he knelt him there.”

The actual Qal ברך is the one meaning “kneel”; compare “he knelt upon his knees” in 2 Chr 6:13. 

But that does not kill wordplay. Quite the opposite: the audience hears the same BRK consonantal root whose semantic complex contains knees/kneeling/blessing, and this story has just been obsessively concerned with someone's thigh/hip, physical submission, and demanding a BRK.

So I would not translate:

> “he forced him to kneel there.”



That's grammatically too aggressive.

But I absolutely would investigate whether the author is exploiting:

> BRK = blessing ↔ kneeling



while narrating a wrestling match whose outcome is being negotiated through a demanded ברכה.

That produces a surprisingly coherent alternative joke

Your hypothesis can be sharpened into something like:

> “What's your name?”
“Jacob.”

“Not anymore. You're ‘Petitions-El.’”

And Jacob petitions him:

“Please—tell me your name.”

“Why are you petitioning about my name?”

And there he BRK's him.



In deliberately joke-preserving English:

> “Your name isn't Jacob anymore. It's Please-God.”

“Please! Tell me your name.”

“Why are you pleading with me about my name?”

And there he blessed/bent-the-knee him.



There is also a potentially important structural irony here. Jacob originally demanded the blessing:

> “I won't let you go unless you BRK me.”



At the end he gets precisely that:

> “and he BRK'd him there.”



So the whole passage is bracketed by ברכתני → ויברך אתו: BRK me! → he BRK'd him. 

Meanwhile the name in the middle can potentially be heard against שאל, and immediately afterward we get וישאל ... נא ... תשאל.

So the lexical architecture is almost:

BRK → NAME → ISRAEL → ŠʾL → PLEASE → ŠʾL → BRK

That pattern is real regardless of whether the Israel ≈ yišʾal-El pun is intentional. And that's what makes your proposed reading much more interesting than a random phonetic coincidence: the proposed two pun-roots actually form the frame around the naming scene.

The weakest claim is “Israel etymologically means Please God.” I wouldn't say that.

The much stronger claim worth testing is: the narrator may be deliberately surrounding ישראל with ŠʾL language so that the newly coined/explained name acquires a secondary echo of “one who petitions El,” while simultaneously exploiting the BRK blessing/kneeling complex. That is philologically defensible as a hypothesis, and the placement of וישאל immediately after the renaming is the detail I'd put a giant red circle around.
