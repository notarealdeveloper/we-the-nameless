
## Q

Who has done for Proverbs what Greenstein did for Job? I'm convinced something is going on in that book and we are missing it. Solomon, the guy with A THOUSAND GIRLS, is like "Step one of wisdom: Get wisdom. By the way, wisdom is bitches." It's obviously some kind of parody lol.

## A

Worked for 44s

YES. There are two different answers, and one of them is hilariously close to exactly what you just said.

If by “what Greenstein did for Job” you mean take the Hebrew seriously enough to stop smoothing over weirdness, re-examine textual problems, cognates, syntax, and inherited translations, then the Proverbs scholar you want is Michael V. Fox. But if you mean “has anyone noticed that the alleged wisdom of Solomon may actually be making Solomon himself the punchline?”, then you want Johnny E. Miles, Wise King, Royal Fool: Semiotics, Satire and Proverbs 1–9.

Miles's thesis is almost absurdly on target. He argues that Proverbs 1–9 should be read as satire of Solomon, aimed specifically at two things: Solomon's exploitative royal economics and his sexual excess. The publisher's summary literally says that Solomon's “numerous sexual (in)discretions” reveal his need for Wisdom, who is presented through erotic personification, and that the supposed wise king emerges as a “royal fool.” 

So your:

> Solomon: “You want to become wise? First, acquire Wisdom. Wisdom is a hot woman. Don't chase women, though.”



is, remarkably, an existing scholarly interpretation.

And once you read Proverbs 1–9 that way, the sequence starts looking really suspicious:

Proverbs 1:1: “Proverbs of Solomon…”

Then immediately the governing criterion becomes:

1:7: fear of YHWH = beginning of knowledge.

Okay. Now remember the literary Solomon of Kings: supposedly wisest man alive, but ultimately fails precisely because of wealth, oppressive royal policy, and women/foreign cults.

Then Proverbs starts hammering:

4:5: Acquire Wisdom.
4:7: The beginning of wisdom is: acquire Wisdom.

Which sounds almost comically circular in English because Wisdom is simultaneously an abstraction and increasingly a woman.

Then:

4:8: cherish/exalt her; embrace her and she will honor you.

And the sexual metaphor keeps getting harder to miss.

Then chapter 5 suddenly gives you an extended lecture about not screwing other women, using intensely erotic language about one's own wife—cistern/well imagery and “let her breasts intoxicate you.”

Then chapters 6–7: MORE warnings about the dangerous woman.

And my favorite transition for this reading is:

7:4: Say to Wisdom, “You are my sister.”

…followed immediately by the purpose:

so that she will protect you from the strange/foreign woman.

Then chapter 8: Wisdom herself appears as a woman standing at the intersections and city gates calling men to herself:

“I love those who love me…”

Then chapter 9 is almost impossible to regard as accidental compositionally. You get two women running rival establishments:

Woman Wisdom: builds a house, slaughters animals, mixes wine, sets a table, sends women out calling naive young men inside.

versus

Woman Folly: sits at her doorway and calls naive young men inside, enticing them with illicit food/water and leading them to death.

That is not merely “wisdom happens to have feminine grammatical gender.” The book has turned moral/intellectual formation into an extended which woman are you going home with? drama.

The four scholars I'd put on your desk

1. Johnny E. Miles — Wise King, Royal Fool
Start here because this is your hypothesis. It is only about Proverbs 1–9 and it's short, roughly 134–144 pages depending on edition. Miles explicitly reads the work as satire targeting Solomon's sexual misconduct and economic injustice. 

I would not tell you this is the scholarly consensus. It isn't. One reader reviewing it explicitly says Miles didn't convince him Proverbs 1–9 is a Solomon satire, although he found the argument interesting.  But the hypothesis is absolutely academically respectable enough to have received a JSOT Supplement/LHBOTS monograph from T&T Clark.

2. Michael V. Fox — Proverbs 1–9 and Proverbs 10–31
This is the closest thing to Greenstein-for-Proverbs in the conventional philological sense. Fox translates from Hebrew, gets deeply into lexical and textual problems, compares Egyptian wisdom, ancient versions, medieval Hebrew commentators, and separates readable commentary from technical philological notes. 

But there's an even more Greenstein-ish Fox book:

Proverbs: An Eclectic Edition with Introduction and Textual Commentary.

This is nuts in exactly the direction we've been talking about with Job: Fox does not simply print the MT and discuss variants underneath it. He actually constructs an eclectic critical Hebrew text, choosing among witnesses and emending where appropriate. It was the first volume of the Hebrew Bible: A Critical Edition project. 

For Proverbs specifically, Fox emphasizes that the Hebrew manuscript evidence is frustratingly sparse and that the Septuagint is by far the most important textual witness besides MT, despite its often extraordinarily free relationship to the Hebrew. 

So if we decide to do to Proverbs what we've been doing to Job, Fox's critical text is where I'd start rather than BHS/BHQ alone.

3. Knut Martin Heim — Poetic Imagination in Proverbs
This one may actually be the most useful for the kind of experiment we've been doing.

Heim noticed that a gigantic amount of Proverbs is made of variant repetitions. His count: 223 verses—more than 24% of the book—occur elsewhere two, three, or four times in identical or slightly altered form. He systematically compares the Hebrew, textual variants, grammar, syntax, and parallelism of those repetitions. 

That is screamingly relevant to your current method because it gives us something like an internal manuscript tradition.

Instead of merely:

> MT says X; maybe X is corrupt.



we can ask:

> This proverb occurs four times. What remained constant? What changed? Did an editor deliberately vary it? Can one reading recover the other? Are the variants jokes? Does the altered word produce a second meaning?



Heim explicitly argues that ambiguity and wordplay are positive features of biblical poetry, and that scholarship has often “normalized” unusual features instead of recognizing deliberate poetic invention. 

His earlier Like Grapes of Gold Set in Silver is also important because he argues that Proverbs 10–22 isn't simply a random sack of fortune-cookie sayings: editors created clusters, and neighboring proverbs alter one another's meanings. 

That could be huge.

4. Claudia V. Camp — Wisdom and the Feminine in the Book of Proverbs
If the question becomes “what the hell is going on with all these women?”, Camp is foundational. She treats female Wisdom not as a decorative grammatical accident but as a major literary/religious symbol, including the relationship among personified Wisdom, wives, foreign/strange women, and the woman of Proverbs 31. 

And there is a potentially enormous structural observation

Proverbs doesn't simply begin:

> Here are Solomon's wise sayings.



It begins:

> THE PROVERBS OF SOLOMON…



and then gives you nine chapters instructing a “son” on how not to be an idiot, with astonishing emphasis on money, kingship/justice, and women.

Only at 10:1 do we get another heading:

> “The Proverbs of Solomon.”



So Proverbs 1–9 can plausibly function as an editorial frame telling you how to read the allegedly Solomonic material that follows. Stuart Weeks independently argues that Proverbs 1–9 is a coherent composition rather than just disconnected wisdom poems, and Arthur Keefer has made the explicit argument that 1–9 functions as an interpretive introduction to the later collections. 

And then there's Proverbs 25:1:

> “These too are proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied.”



Meaning the final book itself tells us we are looking at later editors arranging alleged Solomonic material.

That drastically changes the joke possibility.

It needn't be:

Historical Solomon: “Guys, stop chasing women.”

It could be:

Later Judean scribes:
“Here are the Wise Sayings™ of Solomon, history's wisest man.”

“Before we begin, children, the first requirement for being wise is fearing YHWH.”

“Also control your money.”

“Also don't exploit people.”

“Also, for the love of God, pick the right woman.”

“Okay, now here are Solomon's sayings.”

That is a very different book.

So my reading order for you

I'd actually go:

Miles → Fox 1–9 → Heim → Camp → Fox HBCE

because Miles will give you the big provocative hypothesis first; Fox will stop us from hallucinating Hebrew that isn't there; Heim will show us the book's hidden repetition/parallelism machinery; Camp will make the female imagery legible; and then Fox's HBCE critical Hebrew text gives us the raw material for trying a Greenstein-style reconstruction ourselves.

The really exciting possibility is that Miles has identified the macro-joke, while Heim and Fox give us the tools to look for micro-jokes that nobody has systematically connected to it.

In other words: don't begin with “Can we prove Proverbs is parody?” Begin with “What happens if we read every ambiguity, duplicated proverb, erotic double meaning, and editorial juxtaposition while keeping the literary Solomon of Kings in the room?”

I suspect that experiment is going to pay off spectacularly.
