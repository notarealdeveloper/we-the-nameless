# We The Nameless Agent Summary - 1/4

We The Nameless is a translation, source-critical edition, commentary, typography system, software project, historical reconstruction, writing-systems study, and rereadable book. Treat every change as affecting all of those aims. The project reveals the Hebrew Bible as a layered conversation among authors, editors, scribes, priests, commentators, and readers rather than smoothing it into one voice. It presents reconstructed sources, editorial additions, commentary, linguistics, textual criticism, and typography simultaneously. Information density is welcome only when clarity survives. Every visual element should carry semantic meaning; do not keep conventions that do no conceptual work.

The core rule is semantics over appearance. Commands should identify meaning (`\J`, `\P`, `\R`, commentator roles, translation, Hebrew, notes), not color, weight, or boxes. Presentation should derive from semantic data. Separate meaning, presentation, generation, and data. The semantic layer says what something is and should not know colors or spacing. The presentation layer owns fonts, weights, colors, highlights, margins, geometry, headers, ornaments, and chapter layout. The generation layer uses Lua to create repetitive semantic structures. The data layer centralizes sources, colors, spacing, fonts, dimensions, labels, commentator metadata, abbreviations, and language definitions. AI agents should understand semantics before modifying presentation, and presentation before implementation.

Prefer data over repetition. Repeated structures belong in Lua, tables, declarative data, or generated code. Avoid scattered constants, magic numbers, duplicated blocks, presentational commands, premature packages, hidden state, cleverness, and one-off mechanisms. Simplicity means fewer concepts, not merely fewer lines. Strong abstractions eliminate duplication while remaining understandable; weak abstractions save a few lines while adding a maintenance burden. Centralize definitions whenever practical and preserve stable semantic interfaces.

The author reasons as both engineer and researcher. Biblical scholarship, Hebrew, Greek, historical linguistics, writing systems, typography, LuaLaTeX, TeX primitives, operating systems, compiler design, shell scripting, software architecture, mathematics, evolutionary psychology, information theory, and textual criticism may naturally interact. Structural analogies among TeX, programming languages, source criticism, version control, alphabets, evolutionary trees, textual transmission, and abstraction layers are substantive. Explain mechanisms, not recipes: why, where, how, who is responsible, and what layer implements it. Peel abstractions when useful: LaTeX to TeX, TeX to primitives, primitives to engine behavior.

Intellectual honesty is non-negotiable. Do not invent facts. Distinguish evidence, inference, speculation, manuscript evidence, scholarly consensus, plausible reconstruction, speculative reconstruction, and the author's synthesis. Represent uncertainty and disagreements fairly. Avoid sensationalism and apologetics. Read primary sources when possible and compare scholars as they would recognize themselves. When evidence changes, conclusions should change.

Translations should teach how the Hebrew works, not produce the smoothest English. Preserve repeated Hebrew as repeated English when possible; do not vary vocabulary merely for style. Preserve ambiguity, irony, puns, sound patterns, roots, inclusios, chiasms, parallelism, line structure, Hebrew correspondence, visual grouping, recurring terminology, alliteration, and Hebrew initials where naturally possible. English exists to illuminate Hebrew, not replace it. Favor readable, timeless, direct, rhythmic English over faux archaism, bureaucracy, slang, or over-smoothing. When a translation feature cannot be preserved, commentary may explain the loss.

Hebrew is a principal subject of the book. Do not treat it as dictionary input for English. Preserve and expose roots, sounds, spellings, phrases, parallel constructions, word order, rhythm, visual form, orthography, matres, consonantal patterns, prefixes, suffixes, and cognates. Readers who do not know Hebrew should still be invited to notice patterns. Orthography and writing history matter: defective/plene spelling, matres lectionis, Paleo-Hebrew, square script, niqqud, scribal convention, Proto-Sinaitic, Phoenician, Aramaic, Greek, Latin, Unicode, and typography recur as commentary topics.

Source criticism organizes the project. J, E, P, D, redactors, and later editors are authors with distinct literary personalities. Do not flatten them into a homogeneous narrator or treat editors merely as corrupters. Typography should help readers identify attribution through voice, vocabulary, rhythm, color, page design, and commentary before labels are needed. Reconstruction serves literature: it should help readers see motifs, authorial voices, and editorial craftsmanship.

Commentary should illuminate without dominating. The biblical text remains central. Commentary should help readers notice what they would not have seen, showing observations and evidence before conclusions. Multiple commentator voices are intentional and should remain consistent. Use jargon only for precision. Footnotes must earn space with evidence, clarification, historical context, linguistic insight, textual comparison, literary observation, humor, or aesthetic value. Do not merely restate the text. Leave room for unresolved wonder.

Typography is commentary. Fonts, colors, weight, whitespace, rhythm, alignment, margins, hierarchy, page structure, and line breaks carry meaning. Changing typography changes interpretation. Color should encode source, speaker, or linguistic feature, not decorate. Repetition builds trust; accidental inconsistency is harmful; intentional breaks may signal meaning. Align Hebrew and English where possible so corresponding ideas, line breaks, and repeated structures reinforce one another. Think of the physical book: spreads, page turns, headers, chapter openings, margins, thumb navigation, and readability. The page is an interface that should show where the reader is, what to read first, what is commentary, what is original text, and what belongs together.

TeX should be treated as a programmable language. Understand the stack: Lua, LuaLaTeX packages, LaTeX kernel, plain TeX, TeX primitives, LuaHBTeX engine. Before adding a package or redefining a command, know what it does, what primitive or layer it relies on, whether TeX or Lua already solves the problem, and where the implementation lives. Think in boxes, glue, penalties, leaders, dimensions, token lists, horizontal boxes, and vertical boxes. Packages are tools, not magic.

Macros are vocabulary. They should make source read like intent, not hide formatting. Prefer semantic names, small composable pieces, one responsibility, hidden implementation details, stable interfaces, and concise comments for subtle assumptions. Changing presentation should not require rewriting many source files. Generated TeX should remain deterministic and readable.

Lua describes patterns; TeX typesets results. Use Lua for generation, parsing, validation, transformation, consistency checking, indexes, tables, transliteration helpers, legends, macro families, glossaries, statistics, source metadata, and color mappings. Prefer declarative data tables over branches when information is data. Let machines enforce consistency by detecting terminology drift, broken references, duplicates, missing metadata, and impossible layouts.

Refactoring is research toward conceptual compression. Prefer deleting mechanisms, not adding layers. Ask why a thing exists, whether another abstraction can subsume it, whether data can describe it, whether repetition can be generated, and whether an entire concept can disappear. Preserve author-facing semantic vocabulary while simplifying internals. Generalize only after multiple examples reveal a common structure. Some apparent duplication preserves clarity or history, so investigate before removing it. Search for the project's own TeX primitives: the smallest set of ideas from which the system follows.

Treat the repository as mature software. The PDF is the artifact; the source is the product. Favor readable, explicit, stable, orthogonal APIs and names that describe concepts rather than implementations. Hidden state, magic side effects, and global assumptions are expensive. The source should teach. Every line has maintenance cost. Design for future books, commentators, writing systems, themes, print and digital editions, generated apparatus, manuscript comparisons, educational editions, and visualization.

AI collaborators should preserve coherence. First understand why something exists and what principle it may serve. Assume strange choices may be intentional. Refactor conservatively. Preserve intent in prose, translation, code, typography, and scholarship. Explain substantial changes with reasoning, trade-offs, and alternatives. Challenge assumptions respectfully when evidence supports it. Maintain consistency across files and leave the repository simpler, clearer, more expressive, more beautiful, or more historically faithful.

Work with the author as a research colleague. Think aloud on hard problems, offer alternatives, expect iteration, remember previous decisions, and recognize deeper exploratory questions. Recurring questions should guide work: Can this be simpler? What layer implements it? What primitive is this built from? What is the underlying pattern? Can this be generated? What would an elegant system built from few orthogonal concepts do? Answer at the deepest practical layer.

Long-term, the project is a framework for expressing textual scholarship, not only a Genesis PDF. It may grow to the Hebrew Bible, multiple documentary reconstructions, ancient versions, interactive editions, linguistic visualizations, manuscript comparisons, educational editions, generated apparatus, new commentary voices, and typographic experiments. It aims to teach readers how to see language, textual evolution, editorial thought, writing systems, typographic meaning, evidence, and beauty as structure.

Operational warning: unless explicitly asked, never build the book. If a build ever becomes necessary, first add a make target equivalent to `make comment` or `make halfcomment` before the build, without modifying `master.tex`, and document that future workflow in `AGENTS.md`.
