
## Q

What about ע and י looking very similar in certain stages of Hebrew, e.g., Aramaic, and ר and ו looking extremely similar at different times too.

ישראל is almost exactly עשואל as a visual pun if you look across various variants of the Hebrew alphabet.

Do we know anything about Edomite epigraphy?

It's plausible and suspicious that there's a joke here.

## A

Worked for 2m 39s

Yes — this is substantially more interesting than the phonetic שאל → ישראל idea, because the proposed alternate reading has an exact structural property:

ישראל = y–ś–r–ʾ–l
עשו אל = ʿ–ś–w–ʾ–l

So three of the five consonantal positions are literally identical, and the other two are exactly the pairs you're pointing to:

י ↔ ע and ר ↔ ו.

And עשו אל is not a made-up collection of radicals: it is simply Esau + El. The spelling ʿśw for Esau is also independently epigraphically attested in Hebrew, with a related ʿsw attested in Nabataean. 

The right way to test this, though, is stricter than “can I find some historical alphabet where each pair looks similar?” We need to ask whether there is a plausible single scribal tradition/hand in which both substitutions could be visually activated.

On ר ↔ ו, the evidence is genuinely encouraging. An Israel Antiquities Authority epigraphic publication describing an inscription actually says of one sign that it is a waw with a rounded head but that “the waw might be read as resh or dalet.” In other words, this isn't theoretical: professional epigraphers encounter actual Northwest Semitic handwriting in which waw and resh are alternative readings of the same ink trace.  Another IAA corpus has similarly uncertain readings such as waw/yod, and even individual letters that can be read as ayin, resh, or bet. 

That matters especially because Edomite writing itself is not simply Paleo-Hebrew. The surviving Iron Age Edomite corpus is tiny, but its script is a West Semitic tradition strongly affected by contemporary Aramaic; the major Edomite inscriptions belong mainly to the late eighth through early sixth centuries BCE. Tell el-Kheleifeh, Horvat ʿUza, Tel ʿAroer and related Negev sites furnish the important material.  A 2023 IAA study goes further: one Tel Malḥata ostracon apparently contains both Edomite and Hebrew scribal dialects, interpreted as evidence for cooperation among scribes belonging to different traditions. 

So an author/scribe living in that border world could absolutely have been conscious of multiple graphical realizations of the same alphabet.

The י ↔ ע leg is presently weaker. In normalized Phoenician/Paleo-Hebrew glyphs, the difference is obvious: yod 𐤉 versus ayin 𐤏. Likewise normalized resh 𐤓 and waw 𐤅 aren't terribly alike. So I would not say “ישראל and עשואל naturally look nearly identical in Paleo-Hebrew.”

But normalized fonts are deceptive here. Actual cursive writing gets much messier. Emanuel Tov notes that in some Judean Desert hands yod and waw are almost indistinguishable, and in 11QPsᵃ the ligatures ayin+waw, ayin+zayin, and ayin+yod can become mutually indistinguishable, even resembling shin.  That's later evidence, so it cannot simply be projected backward into seventh-century Edomite writing. But it establishes the important palaeographic fact that ayin/yod-containing forms can collapse visually in real Hebrew-Aramaic handwriting, not merely in someone's imaginative alphabet chart.

And there's a much bigger reason I now take your proposal seriously.

Hosea basically proves that ישראל was susceptible to this kind of visual resegmentation

Hosea 12:5 describes this exact wrestling episode with:

וישר אל־מלאך

wayyāśar ʾel-malʾāḵ — “and he strove with an angel.” 

Now eliminate the word boundary:

וישר אל → וישראל

That's exactly the consonantal spelling “and Israel.”

Not approximately.

Exactly.

So an ancient Hebrew author discussing the Jacob wrestling/name story constructs:

ו + ישר + אל
“and he strove + with/to El…”

which visually collapses into:

וישראל
“and Israel.”

That's extremely important methodological precedent. It shows that resegmenting the Israel string into meaningful components belonging to the wrestling story is not something we're imposing from modern typography. Hosea is already exploiting precisely that consonantal material in his ancient interpretation of the story. 

And immediately afterward Hosea says:

בכה ויתחנן־לו — someone “wept and supplicated him.” 

So the ancient interpretive constellation is already:

Israel-name → wrestling → El → prevailing → begging/supplication.

That is remarkably close to what we've been finding independently.

Now put your visual proposal into that environment

Suppose the eye is invited to oscillate between something like:

ישראל

and

עשו אל

Then the second string is simply:

Esau | El

and in a suitable syntactic construal could even suggest a verbless “Esau is El/God”, although I would not claim that meaning is forced. Merely seeing ESAU + EL is sufficient for the visual joke.

Now look at what Genesis does narratively.

The mysterious man appears the night immediately before Esau arrives. He demands Jacob's name. Jacob finally identifies himself correctly—unlike the earlier blessing scene where he answered his father's identity interrogation with “I am Esau.” He receives the Israel name and a blessing. The following morning Jacob encounters Esau and tells him:

“I have seen your face as seeing the face of God.” 

That sentence is almost embarrassingly accommodating to an Esau ↔ God visual joke.

The night scene ends with:

> I saw God face to face.



Then the next scene says to Esau:

> Seeing your face was like seeing God's face. 



So if some scribal joke made ישראל visually flicker into עשו אל, the surrounding narrative supplies exactly the semantic instruction needed to decode it:

ESAU ↔ EL.

That's the part that makes this suspicious rather than merely clever.

Edomite epigraphy makes the historical setting better, not worse

We actually know much more Edomite writing than “none,” although it's still a painfully small corpus. The principal evidence consists of ostraca, seals and seal impressions, especially from the seventh–early sixth centuries. The largest early Edomite text was traditionally the Tell el-Kheleifeh name list; Horvat ʿUza produced a much more informative letter. Edomite script reflects substantial contemporary Aramaic influence. 

And the Horvat ʿUza Edomite letter contains this wonderful formula:

והברכתך לקוס

“and I bless you by Qos.” 

So the actual surviving language of Edom gives us BRK + Edomite god in precisely the sort of epistolary/religious environment we'd expect. That isn't evidence for the Genesis joke by itself, but given how obsessively Genesis 27–33 revolves around Esau, deity, and ברכה, it's an amusingly appropriate external datum.

There is even good evidence that biblical writers could make graphemic jokes specifically involving Edom's god Qos. Scholars have proposed that Lamentations 4:21 exploits כוס “cup” / קוס Qos while addressing Edom, and another proposal reconstructs Qos in Proverbs through a graphically easy scribal confusion. These remain scholarly proposals rather than settled readings, but the kind of device—an Edomite reference concealed by tiny graphical/phonetic differences—is absolutely represented in serious scholarship. 

Where I land now

I would assign the individual claims roughly like this:

ישראל historically derives from עשו אל: essentially no.

The spoken words Israel and Esau-El are a clean phonetic pun: no.

Ancient scribes could exploit ambiguous letterforms to make words visually echo other words: unquestionably yes. 

Edomite scribes inhabited exactly the Hebrew/Aramaic graphical borderland that could facilitate such play: yes. 

Israel itself is demonstrably subjected to word-boundary/resegmentation play in the ancient interpretation of this very scene: yes, spectacularly so in Hosea's וישר אל / וישראל. 

Genesis 32–33 deliberately makes Esau and God overlap: at minimum, the text itself explicitly compares seeing Esau's face with seeing God's face. 

Therefore ישראל ↔ עשו אל is an intentional additional visual joke: plausible and now genuinely worth testing, but not established.


The one thing I would want before pushing it from “holy shit, suspicious” to a serious textual argument is a palaeographic specimen from one plausible Iron Age Edomite/Aramaic/Judahite hand in which both substitutions simultaneously work:

י ≈ ע
and
ר ≈ ו.

We already have excellent direct evidence for the second kind of ambiguity.  I have not yet found comparably good Iron Age Edomite evidence for standalone י ≈ ע; the strongest thing I found is later cursive evidence involving ayin-yod ligatures. 

So that's the bottleneck.

But the Hosea result massively raises my prior. The ancient tradition is demonstrably already playing the game:

ישראל ↔ וישר־אל

If Genesis additionally contains:

ישראל ↔ עשו־אל

then the two readings would be almost perversely appropriate:

Israel = “he strives with El”
but visually, Israel = “Esau–El.”

And the very next morning Israel looks at Esau and says, essentially:

“Your face looks like God's.”

That is sufficiently overdetermined that I would not dismiss it as accidental without doing the palaeographic test.
