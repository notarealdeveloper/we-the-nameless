------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:1

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Up spoke Job and he said:


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:2

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I in fact know this is true:

“How can a mortal be righteous before God?”

---

Footnote 9:2. A paraphrase of the spirit in 4:17.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:3

------------------------------------------------------------------------

If one wanted to press charges against him,

Not once in a thousand would he respond.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:4

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The wise of heart and stern of strength—

Who has ever coerced him and come out whole?

---

Footnote 9:4. Using this phrase, which ordinarily denotes stubbornness, in reference to the deity is parodic; see Deuteronomy 2:30.

Footnote 9:4. See Deuteronomy 2:30, where the deity “hardens the spirit” (will) of Israel’s opponent.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:5–6

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Him who moves mountains without their knowing;

Who overturns them in his anger.

Who shakes the land from its place,

So that its pillars tremble.

---

Footnote 9:5–6. The earth was imagined as resting on pillars; for example Psalm 75:4.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:7–9

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Who commands the sun so it does not shine;

Who seals the celestial lights.

Who spreads out the sky all alone;

And who steps on the back of Sea.

Who has made the Hyades, Orion,

The Pleiades and the South-Wind Chambers.

---

Footnote 9:7–9. Borrowed from Isaiah 44:24, where God’s being the sole creator is more to the point.

Footnote 9:7–9. Stepping on the back of an enemy is a gesture of triumph; see Deuteronomy 33:29. Sea is both the Canaanite deity Yamm (see Job 7:12) and the sea.

Footnote 9:7–9. These are all constellations. In the light of classical mythology, the constellations are, like Sea (Yamm), resistant powers that had to be subdued at the time of creation.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:10

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Who performs great things too deep to probe,

And wondrous things, beyond number.

---

Footnote 9:10. A parody of Eliphaz in 5:9.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:11

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Since he can cross without my seeing him,

Pass by without my discerning him,


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:12

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He can snatch—and who can restrain him?

Who will say to him: “What are you doing?”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:13

------------------------------------------------------------------------

(Since) Eloah will not restrain his anger—

Even the soldiers of Rahab cower beneath him—

---

Footnote 9:13. A biblical name for the primeval sea monster, like Sea / Yamm and Leviathan (Psalm 89:10–11).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:14

------------------------------------------------------------------------

How could I call him to account,

Choose my charges against him?

---

Footnote 9:14. The term “word(s)” often denotes legal matters.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:15–16

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Even in the right, I would get no response;

Even if I implored my opponent in court.

But if I would summon and he would respond,

I do not trust he would hear my complaint.

---

Footnote 9:15–16. Literally, “he would give ear to my voice.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:17–18

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He would push me on the hair-line,

And multiply my wounds for no cause.

He would not let me return breath,

For he saturates me with poison.

---

Footnote 9:17–18. A gesture indicating the rejection of a legal claim, attested in Alalakh (north Syria) in the seventeenth century BCE (Wiseman, 38). Others understand “hair” (as in 4:15) as its homonym “storm,” but in Job that word is always spelled with *samekh* not *sin*.

Footnote 9:17–18. “For nothing” in Job 1:9. Job does not realize how right he is.

Footnote 9:17–18. That is, make any statement; compare 15:13. There may be a secondary sense of not allowing Job even to “restore his breath” (compare Psalm 23:3).

Footnote 9:17–18. Reading *bimrorim* (Lamentations 3:15). Compare the image in Job 6:4.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:19

------------------------------------------------------------------------

If it’s a matter of strength, then he is the strong;

And if a legal proceeding, who can convene us?

---

Footnote 9:19. Reading final—*nu* “us” for—*ni* “me.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:20

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Even were I in the right, his mouth would condemn me.

(Even) were I innocent, he would wrong me.

---

Footnote 9:20. Read *piw* for *pi* “my mouth.” The virtually synonymous parallel line makes this certain. That Job incriminates himself is Eliphaz’s later contention (15:5–6), not Job’s. An ancient scribe surely sanitized the reading here; but the point is elaborated by Job in verses 30–31 below.

Footnote 9:20. “Whole (of heart)” in Job 1:1, 8.

Footnote 9:20. Reading *ya‘ashqeni* (with metathesis).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:21

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I am innocent—I care not for my self;

I’m fed up with my life.

---

Footnote 9:21. For this sense of *yada‘*, see for example Exodus 2:25; Psalm 1:6.

Footnote 9:21. Compare 7:16.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:22

------------------------------------------------------------------------

It is all the same.

And so I declare:

The innocent and the guilty he brings to (the same) end.

---

Footnote 9:22. This radical claim finds a parallel in Ecclesiastes (7:15; 8:14). It is exceeded only by Ezekiel 21:8, where the deity himself threatens to annihilate both the righteous and the wicked.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:23

------------------------------------------------------------------------

While (his) scourge brings death to fools,

He laughs at the trials of the spotless.

---

Footnote 9:23. Reading *peta’im* for *pit’om* “suddenly,” as I did in 5:3; compare the ancient Greek translation.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:24

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The earth is handed over to the wicked;

He covers the eyes of its judges.

If it is not he, then who?

---

Footnote 9:24. Job overturns the conventional wisdom according to which the reverent “inherit the earth” (for example Psalm 25:13; 37:9).

Footnote 9:24. So that they cannot render justice.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:25–26

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Meanwhile my days go faster than a runner;

They flee having seen nothing good.

They pass on like boats made of reeds,

Like an eagle swooping down on food.

---

Footnote 9:25–26. A racing messenger; see for example 2 Samuel 18:19–32. Job returns to the theme of 7:6.

Footnote 9:25–26. Compare the images in Habakkuk 1:8.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:27–28

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Were I to say, “I’ll ignore my complaint,

I’ll forgo my sullen face and be happy,”

I’d dread all my afflictions;

I know you will not find me spotless.

---

Footnote 9:27–28. For “face” alone in the sense of a sullen mien, see for example 1 Samuel 1:18.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:29

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I will be found guilty.

So why should I strive in vain?

---

Footnote 9:29. The term for “in vain” is *hebel* “mere breath” (as in Qohelet 1:2).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:30–31

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Were I to wash myself with soap-plant,

And cleanse my palms with lye,

You would plunge me into the pit,

So even my clothing would abhor me.

---

Footnote 9:30–31. Reading with the traditional *Ketib* (written form) rather than the Masoretic reading “with snow water,” which is a much less apt parallel.

Footnote 9:30–31. The word *bor*, employed instead of conventional *borit*, usually means “pit” and therefore produces a pun with the term for pit in the following couplet. The verse parodies Jeremiah 2:22, where God says to the people of Judah: “Even if you clean (yourself) with soap and apply a lot of lye, your sin will be a stain in my sight.” Job boldly accuses the deity of falsely incriminating him.

Footnote 9:30–31. “Pit” also connotes the grave (for example 33:18).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:32

------------------------------------------------------------------------

For he is not like me a man I could call to account;

(With whom) we could join in a lawsuit.

---

Footnote 9:32. Compare the usage in verse 14 above.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 9:33–35

------------------------------------------------------------------------

If only were between us a prosecutor,

Who would lay his hand on us both;

Who would remove his rod from upon me,

So his awe would not terrify me,

Then could I speak and not fear him—

For I am not being right toward myself.

*Job begins to overcome his intimidation and to build a case against the deity. Assuming that he is being held accountable for minor misdeeds (see 7:16ff.), Job makes a classic argument, found already in second millennium BCE Hittite prayers from Asia Minor: If I am sinful, it is your fault for having made me this way. You should be more tolerant of your creature’s infractions. Returning to earlier themes (his opening discourse), Job asks why he had to be born at all and why he should be so closely watched, when he will soon be gone. Job does not yet sue God formally; but he lays out a hypothetical case.*

---

Footnote 9:33–35. Reading *lu’* for *lo’* with some ancient translations and many commentators. For *lu’* spelled with *aleph*, see 2 Samuel 19:7; compare also 1 Samuel 14:30; Isaiah 48:18; 63:19.

Footnote 9:33–35. The deity’s.

Footnote 9:33–35. For the force of the expression “not right,” see 2 Kings 17:9.
