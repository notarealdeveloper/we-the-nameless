------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:1

------------------------------------------------------------------------

It really makes my heart flutter,

Makes it leap out of its place!

---

Footnote 37:1. Literally, “At it (the lightning) my heart trembles.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:2–5

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Hear oh hear the shudder of his thunder,

The sound coming out of his mouth!

He flashes from beneath the whole sky,

His lightning o’er the span of the land.

Then he roars his thunder;

Booming in his surging voice.

He does not hold back bolts,

So his voice can be heard.

El’s voice thunders wondrously;

He performs great things beyond our ken.

---

Footnote 37:2–5. In the ancient Near East and the Bible thunder is figured, and perhaps understood, as the voice of the storm god.

Footnote 37:2–5. Reading *yishrah;* the verb *sharah* is found in this context in ancient Canaanite (Ugaritic).

Footnote 37:2–5. Literally, “the wings, edges.”

Footnote 37:2–5. To complete the short line I read (with Budde, Dhorme) *ye‘aqqeb beraqim* “he restrains lightning-bolts,” assuming the omission of three repeated letters.

Footnote 37:2–5. Literally, “El thunders with his voice wondrously.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:6

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He orders the snow, “Fall to earth!”

And the rain, the staves of his strength.

---

Footnote 37:6. An Arabism and not the Hebrew or Aramaic verb “to be.”

Footnote 37:6. Reading *mattot ‘uzzo;* see Ezekiel 19:11. The scribe began writing *mattot* but wrote *matar* “rain” by mistake; he then continued copying correctly. Unfortunately, a later scribe added a *resh* to *mattot*, producing an awkward text.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:7–8

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He seals all humanity in,

Letting them know of his works.

Wild animals enter their dens,

Settling down in their lairs.

---

Footnote 37:7–8. Reading *we-tabo’* for *wa-tabo’* “entered.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:9–10

------------------------------------------------------------------------

The storm winds come out of their chamber,

The chill (comes) out of the storerooms.

By the breath of El ice is made;

Expanses of water turn solid.

---

Footnote 37:9–10. Reading *mezawim* (Psalm 144:13) for the unique term *mezarim*. For the image, see Job 38:22.

Footnote 37:9–10. Literally, “he/it gives (ice).” The subject is unclear—possibly the celestial “chamber” (verse 9); many read a passive form of the verb (“the ice is given/made”).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:11–13

------------------------------------------------------------------------

He weighs down with moisture the clouds;

The nimbus scatters his lightning;

And it whirls around on all sides—

They behave in accord with his plan,

However he directs them to cover the earth—

Whether to punish the land,

Or to favor it he bestows them.

---

Footnote 37:11–13. Reading the noun as an unbound form (not “cloud of …”).

Footnote 37:11–13. The lightning.

Footnote 37:11–13. The final phrase is, more literally, “over the face of the land earthward.”

Footnote 37:11–13. The second instance of *’im* “whether” preceding “the land” is a scribal error.

## Job 37:14

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Give ear to this, Job!

Stand and contemplate

The wonders of El!


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:15–17

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Do you know how he enjoins them—

And lightning gleams out of his clouds?

Do you know how he spreads the clouds,

A wonder of the Wholly Knowing?

(Do you know) why your garments are hot,

When the south wind is calm in the land?

---

Footnote 37:15–17. Here an epithet of the deity. The same phrase as “(man) of honest intent” in 36:4.

Footnote 37:15–17. Literally, “when the land is calm without the south wind.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:18

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Can you beat out the sky-dome as he can—

Solid as a mirror of metal?

---

Footnote 37:18. The dome of the sky is typically figured as a beaten sheet of metal; the sky is called “the beaten plate” (*raqia‘*) in Genesis 1:6 and elsewhere.

Footnote 37:18. Mirrors were typically made of bronze (compare Exodus 38:8). Literally, “strong as a solid mirror.”


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:19–20

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Tell us, how can we answer him?

We’re in the dark and have no (words) to offer.

Will he be informed if I try to speak—

Has a man what to say when confused?

---

Footnote 37:19–20. Elihu imagines that God is asking the questions—as he does in the subsequent divine discourses.

Footnote 37:19–20. Compare the full phrase at 32:14.

Footnote 37:19–20. For this sense of *bala‘*, see Isaiah 28:7.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 37:21–22

------------------------------------------------------------------------

When people cannot see the sun,

It’s obscured in the heavens,

A wind blows across and they’re clear.

A glow emerges from the north,

An aura about the awesome Eloah.

---

Footnote 37:21–22. Literally, “the Light.”

Footnote 37:21–22. Compare *baheret* “skin discoloration” in Leviticus 13:2, 4, etc. from a stem with the general sense of “clear, bright.” The poet enjoys ironic puns.

Footnote 37:21–22. Literally, “it makes them (the heavens) pure/clear.”

Footnote 37:21–22. Reading *zohar* for *zahab* “gold.”

Footnote 37:21–22. Perhaps the northern lights.

## Job 37:23–24

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Shaddai—we cannot reach him,

Exalted in power and dominion!

Abounding in justice, he does not afflict.

That is why people revere him;

The wisest of heart cannot see (him).

*In the preceding passage (37:14–24), Elihu describes the uncanny marvels of the created world in the upper realm, in the sky. In the present passage (chapter 28), Elihu continues to describe a world that is beyond human comprehension, now focusing on the lower realm, the earth and, more particularly, the subterranean, which includes both the netherworld—the domain of the dead—and the sea that was believed to lie beneath the land. The passage is structured by two questions that ask, Where can (divine) wisdom be found? The question turns out to be a riddle, for the answer is not about where, but when (see verses 25–27).*

*Modern commentators tend to regard chapter 28, which does not comport with Job’s perspectives, as an independent poem that cannot be attributed to any of the known speakers. The assumption that the poem is autonomous is highly problematic. Biblical poems do not begin with the conjunction *ki*, “for, because,” as this passage does. There is no antecedent to the pronoun “he” in verse 3. But more important, the motif of esoteric wisdom lying beyond human reach typically includes both the above and the below (see for example Job 11:7–8; Deuteronomy 30:11–13; Jeremiah 31:36; as well the Babylonian hymn to the sun god Shamash). The conclusion of this passage (28:28) echoes the conclusion of the survey of the heavenly wonders in 37:24, and it is following that passage that this one belongs.*

*Most modern commentators and translators, in contrast to the medieval Jewish exegetes, interpret the first part of this passage to deal with human mining activity. This interpretation, though widespread, is unfounded. There is not a single term associated with excavating in the Hebrew text. And all the enumerated activities are elsewhere attributed to the deity. It is God who exposes the subterranean, not people (see the notes). Reference to precious ore that is extracted from the ground serves as a contrast to wisdom, which cannot be drawn out of any site.*

*The passage begins in response to the prompt in 37:23: “we cannot reach him,” an allusion to 11:7–8 (Zophar), where God is said to be out of reach in both “the heights of heaven” and “deeper than Sheol.”*

---

Footnote 37:23–24. Compare 11:7–8.

Footnote 37:23–24. Does not afflict without just cause.

Footnote 37:23–24. So also Clines, “The Fear of the Lord”; *Job 21–37*.
