To explore the problem, we proceed as follows:

You and I are two parties. You cannot be sure whether you can trust me,
and if you feel that you can, I ask you for the sake of argument to
suspend that trust as much as you can. You should not have to trust me
in order for us to communicate.

So, in our newfound complete absence of trust, our task is to exchange
a series of utterances, and somehow be confident that in doing so we
have each obtained true information with arbitrarily high degree of confidence.

Consider an arbitrary sentence. Something someone says to you.

Given any such utterance, suppose we factor it into two components:
what we might call the objective pragmatics, and the subjective pragmatics.

The subjective pragmatics of an utterance is everything the speaker might
have intended by uttering it. All the speaker's motives, ulterior or explicit,
are part of the subjective pragmatics of an utterance. Since we are
assuming a situation with minimal "trust," we cannot have any confidence in
our knowledge or understanding of the speaker's true intentions.

However, suppose we instead confine ourselves to the complementary parts
of the utterance. All those that are invariant under different inferred
or imagined values of the speaker's intent. We will call these components
of an utterance the "objective pragmatics."

Objective pragmatics is the budding underground subdiscipline on which geometric propaganda is founded.

It is the study of the social physics of speech acts against the background of our shared universal human nature.

Every speech act, regardless of the speaker's intent, has a set of effects on the listener.

Since listeners vary, but not infinitely, each speech act gives rise to a probability distribution of interpretations (and thus changes of belief state) in any set of possible listeners.

Objective pragmatics is the study not of the speaker's intent, which may always be unknown, but of the objective effects of speech acts on listeners of any given category.

That category of "listeners" may simply be a set of mathematical models, such as neural networks or other artificial intelligence systems, for laboratory explorations and/or pedagogical purposes. Or, in the more difficult and ambitious case, we may be dealing with human nature itself.

Consider the following.

It happens to be a fact that the majority of people are less likely to take an utterance seriously (and correspondingly more likely to dismiss or ignore it) if it:
* contains grammatical errors
* contains spelling errors
* contains factual errors
* is overly informal
* is overly vulgar
* appears sloppy
* appears campy
* appears childish
* appears unsophisticated
* shows obvious signs of having been written in ignorance of [important fact X]
* &c.

Now, if one's goal were to be taken seriously -- as is the goal of most individuals -- then it follows that one should strive to avoid informality, avoid spelling and grammatical and factual errors, and minimize any features in one's speech and writing that lend to it an appearance of being sloppy, campy, childish, unsophisticated, ignorant, &c.

However, objective pragmatics does not concern itself with any particular goal or set of goals.

And as far as objective pragmatics is concerned, the above fact is entirely value neutral.

It does not tell one how to behave.

It simply constitutes a predictable (and thus exploitable) statement of fact. A fact about the physical dynamics of the physical objects we commonly call human beings.

As such, if one's goal were to assure that the most important and timeless secrets of all remain hidden from the mass.

To speak freely. To communicate at once openly and in secret with any others who care to look closely enough to recognize the meaning underlying one's seemingly incomprehensible rituals of behavior and of speech.

If one's goal were to hide, in plain sight.

Then the predictable mass tendency to dismiss and not take seriously that which fits the descriptions above becomes a valuable asset.

And it then follows that one should cloak oneself in exactly those features which cause the unthinking mass to dismiss an utterance, or ignore it entirely.


The Psychology of Situations
----------------------------

What is "context," from a computational perspective?

Generally, the mind does not respond to either the syntax or the semantics
of an utterance in isolation. The primary factor determining what we call the
"contextual" meaning of an utterance -- and thus determining its actual effects,
is how the listener's mind maps the utterance onto some answer to the question:
"What type of situation am I in?"

By "situation" here I mean something a bit more precise than the everyday meaning
of the term.

It appears to be the case that the human mind gains its flexibility not by containing
mechanisms for dealing with every imaginable problem, but rather because it attempts
to map any situation it encounters onto a small set of what we might call "situational
primitives." Situations of:

Mating. Kinship. Foraging. Coalitions, etc.

a.k.a., Sex, Family, Food, Groups, etc.

[List a bunch. Basically just correspond to section headings in B.B.B.]

These are the atoms from which our experience of more complex
real life situations is built.

Linguists have always recognized the importance of context,
but it has been difficult to study exactly what "context" is
in any precise manner.

However, this concept of situational primitives may help. How?

Well, consider the physical set of events that have occurred here
at Stonewall. Suppose we had begun by saying "we are a school."

[etc. etc. old "foundations" sentences for the newer newcomers.]

However, if we begin by saying "we're terrorists," the mind parses
the situation differently. Now, suppose further that after we hear a
group self-identify as terrorists, we learn a set of additional things
about them, and for whatever reason we become fairly convinced that the
"terrorists" in question are making no real threats of violence, and are
thus not quite exactly "terrorists" in any meaningful sense. They're just
being silly or something. That's all fine. However, despite the obvious lack
of any existential threat from said individuals, the fact that they have
called themselves terrorists effectively *causes our minds to parse the
situation differently,* (emphasis in original) and once the situation
has been parsed differently, a different set of reasoning heuristics and logical
rules is applied to *the entire discussion* thereafter.

Thus, rather than parsing the situation as one in which a conscious decision is
made to create a new school, in a cramped basement of a [redacted] business, in the
course of which both the individual in question and the members of said school
knowingly break all manner of both social norms and legal mandates (said violations
thus leading many human listeners to a conclusion something like "disapprove") -- 
the same mind, upon hearing the group self-identify as terrorists, parses the
situation as one involving violent intergroup conflict, at which point the
reasoning and inference procedures appropriate for that domain are activated,
and those inference procedures soon thereafter find themselves coming to the
conclusion: hey these terrorists turned out not to be so bad after all, and
they're actually kinda nice.

The important point to note is that nothing has actually changed about reality.
By framing a situation as a "school," we ensure that the moral psychology in
the mind of the listener will apply the same set of inferences we all usually
apply when reasoning about the things we commonly label "schools." And that set
of inferences is so limited and restrictive as to be impossible to satisfy without
sacrificing all one's ability to make any detectably non-trivial changes.
However, by reframing the same set of events in a way that causes the mind to parse
it as a different situational primitive, we change virtually all of the subsequent
inferences downstream of that framing.

The above is just one example, but the more broad lesson of the example is that
the vast majority of the time: we *do not actually know what our own most
fundamental beliefs are.*

Because the mechanisms for reasoning about different situations in human
life apply different internal computational logics to the information they receive.
And so even our most fundamental beliefs *do not actually exist* in anything like
a coherent way. (Narrator: Missed a bit more. Filling in gaps here.)

What we believe at our deepest levels depends critically on which mechanisms are
handling a given bit of information, which depends in large part on how a situation
is framed. And the framing is entirely up to the speaker. The speaker, in effect,
owns our core beliefs more than we do. That should give us pause.

That's essentially why propaganda can exist.
But the above means that propaganda can exist without actually having to lie about
anything at all. Just because there are many different ways of honestly framing a
situation. And this... to get more to the point... is in part why it's possible for
two reasonable people to disagree about something, even when they have exactly the
same set of information.

Most of what political ideologies do is train us to develop the default unthinking
habit of framing whole classes of situations by squeezing them onto particular
ideology-determined sets of situational primitives.

And it is exactly those conceptual distortion devices that we need to avoid if we
want to make responsible scientific use of our naturally selected primate brains.

Why did she repeat the question? We all know, but let's dissect it.

With default semantics (context free), the question "Am I under arrest?"
simply means @(0){"Am I under arrest?"}.

So at first glance (i.e., to zeroth order), the phrase "Am I under arrest?"
seems to be something the character should only ask once.

Now, let's add some context.

If you ask a normal friend "Am I under arrest?" then the sentence is confusing,
because the human mind's internal logic of friendship doesn't contain a concept like "arrest."

So at second glance (i.e., to first order), the phrase "Am I under arrest?"
seems to be something that a person should not ask at all, if all we know
about the listener is that they are a friend or family member.

However, since context is a degree of freedom, we can modify the above the negate
what I just said: if you ask your hacker friend the same question, "Am I under arrest?"
and s/he happens to have internal access to the police department's records,
like our friend [newcomer] does...n't, (Narrator: Room laughs) then the mind parses
the sentence as having *default semantics* again. "Am I under arrest?" then just means
"Am I under arrest?", and you're asking because your friend might know that information,
and you might be under arrest without knowing it. That's not quite how police departments
work, but the example is illustrative either way.

Now finally, and here's the point of the scene, if you ask the police "Am I under arrest?"
then the sentence means something more like "Stop fucking with me."

Or to put it more simply: The semantics of any sentence is entirely slave to its pragmatics,
and an important part of objective pragmatics is the data we commonly call "context."

Connotations are also a particular type of context, but one that is in a sense "built-in" to the
word itself, and not simply provided by the external details surrounding the word's use.

So, in summary, if a sentence's meaning depends on the context, and the context often depends
on the speaker's unknown and unknowable intentions, then how can we hope to communicate at all?
What is a "lie"? What is "fiction"? What is a "parable"? What is "propaganda"?

Or, to summarize the summary: what determines the meaning of an utterance?

In formal logic, and in some sense in all of mathematics, the "semantics" simply *is* the syntax.

This is the content of the so-called "only law" of mathematics: You can never really know what something "is." All you can do is to ask: "how does it behave?"

The mathematical concept of an object only being defined "up to isomorphism" arises from that only law.

So, in at least one area of inquiry, formal mathematics, semantics essentially reduces to syntax.

What about in other areas of life?

The direct "semantics" turn out to be equivalent to meaning only in the limit of very simple social interactions.

To first order, the primary meaning of any utterance comes not from the semantics or the syntax, in the normal senses of those terms, but rather, from: the psychology of "situation parsing." The way in which the scaffolding of universal human psychology maps utterances onto answers to the question "what type of situation am I in?" The human mind's primitive situations are the computational primitives that determine how we assign meaning to our experience.

For everyday life, we should all be more aware of our mind's tendency to apply extremely restricted sets of situation-specific reasoning methods to any given utterance.

For hoping to create anything innovative, we must be very much aware of this tendency in ourselves, and aim to game our mind's psychology by determining the set of contexts and situations that cause us to reason differently about any given issue than we would be inclined to reason by default. Only in doing so can we responsibly know what we believe about the most important issues in life. What is acceptable in situation X? What constitutes moral behavior? What causes us to be pleasantly surprised relative to our intial expectations, and what causes us instead to feel deep disappointment and disgust, often about the same objective set of events?

For social revolutions of all forms, we all must become consciously aware of how uncreative and restricted our natural default setting leads us to be. Many more possibilities exist in hell and on earth than in our everyday social philosophies. It is time to open these gates of hell we call our minds, and see what exactly those possibilities are.


The Point
=========
You are not being manipulated, but you can be. And that is the whole point. To avoid being manipulated, do not be on the lookout for malicious individuals manipulating you. Rather, be vigilant about learning how to manipulate yourself. Reframe each of your own loves so that it can appear evil, and each of your own hates so that it can appear beautiful. Then recurse once more. And never stop. Then, when listening to any speaker whose intent is assumed to be anywhere from entirely benevolent to entirely malicious, you will find that their intent won't matter. The effective mind becomes a sieve that extracts intent-invariant content from both evil speakers and good.

The study (and indeed, mastery) of propaganda is essential to the development of a critical mind.

We will overcome the limitations inherent in human nature not by brainwashing or propaganda or conditioning or any other form of doomed direct attack on the unavoidable universal psychology that makes us human, but by understanding the computational structure of our mind's "worst" parts, in detail, in order to subject them to the computational equivalent of a panic induced market crash.

We will overcome our darkest sides not by blunt force trauma, but by a set of carefully crafted buffer overflow attacks, inflicted by each of us, not against others, but against ourselves. Only in so doing may we finally free ourselves of the malicious code with which we all come pre-installed.

Perhaps the worst such piece of default human nature is: tribalism. Our coalitional psychology. The neuro-computational systems that determine how we treat members of those fictitious categories we call our ingroups and outgroups. If the game matters anywhere, it matters there most of all.
