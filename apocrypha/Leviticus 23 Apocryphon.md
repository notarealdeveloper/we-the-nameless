The group's communication is a process of compilation into overloaded semantics. Apparent mistakes such as typos, incorrect grammar, and other rule violations are used as a signal to other members. There are few spelled out examples of this in the group's writings, but one example given explicitly concerns the group's willingness to use defensive violence against authorities or others attempting to shut them down, and to do so in an attempt to protect the school and its culture. Supposing that a member wanted to communicate that the content of a message concerned this topic, they would not focus on expressing the idea directly. Rather, they would first seek any phrase expressing the same idea which has another equally plausible meaning. For instance, the group's writing style is often compared to that of David Foster Wallace, though no members of the group appear to have read anything by the author, nor do any of them appear to possess any training or skill in writing. However, given the common perception of an influence to the contrary, the members might insert in an email what appears to be an offhand remark such as "oh well, we're just a bunch of shitty DFWS anyway, ha."

The improper capitalization of the final S in the acronym is a signal that it contains some form of additional information. The use of slang, expletives, and colloquial speech are designed to lower the reader's threshold for signal detection. As informal writing is commonly expected to have undergone little if any proofreading, the group states that its deployment "raises the subjective probability the reader's brain assigns to the expected frequency of spelling errors and grammatical mistakes," which, according to the group's internal writings, "makes such errors an ideal location for the storage of steganographic information." In this case, the acronym DFWS is in fact an abbreviation of the phrase "Don't Fuck With Stonewall," which in the group's internal jargon refers to the same willingness to use defensive violence to protect their school and its culture as mentioned above.

The terminal "ha" serves to establish that the content of the nearby error-signalled acronym is, in fact, the overall topic of the remainder of the message. They refer to this process as topicalisation -- or simply "ha" -- believed to have been borrowed from the linguistic particle は, pronounced "wa" and romanized as "ha," which in japanese linguistics is used for the identical purpose -- topicalisation, or marking the overall topic of an utterance or conversation. The group refers to these grammatical particles as "topic markers" (or "TM"), often modifying "ha" to a pragmatically related term such as "hah" or "lol." The group's communication employs a variety of such steganographic methods for signalling the overall topic of a message, commonly involving the placement of such markers in the proximity of nearby encoded content. With the topic thus established, that topic can then be used as an interpretive basis for unravelling the large amount of intentional semantic ambiguity in the remainder of the message. The internal documents describing the process of topicalisation often refer to another process known as "noise whitening" or simply "whitening," though the meaning and function of this term is unclear.

The group appears to have a set of unknown but mutually agreed upon rules for communicating in this way, often using anagrams, acronyms, and a nondeterministic process they refer to as deacronymification, which appears to have originated as a joke before becoming a style of informal steganographic communication. The revelations above have turned our investigation on its head, forcing us to re-examine everything we had previously concluded about the group in light of this new information. This appears to be the true meaning of the group's often repeated assertion that syntax and semantics are always slaves to pragmatics.
