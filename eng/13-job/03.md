------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:1–2

------------------------------------------------------------------------

After that Job opened up his mouth and cursed his day (of birth).

Up spoke Job and he said:


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:3

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let the day disappear, the day I was born,

And the night that announced: A man’s been conceived!

---

Footnote 3:3. “Day” is repeated in translation for reasons of prosody.

Footnote 3:3. By referring to himself as “man” (*geber*) Job identifies himself with the “man” in verse 23 below; see also note 10. The term also designates a pious sufferer; see Lamentations 3:1 and Psalm 88:5; and compare Psalm 94:12.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:4

------------------------------------------------------------------------

As for that day—Let it be darkness!

May he not summon it, Eloah from on high;

May nothing luminous shine on it!

---

Footnote 3:4. A parody of “Let there be light!” (Genesis 1:3).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:5

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Let darkness, dead-darkness, expunge it!

Let hovering clouds engulf it!

Let daytime eclipses obscure it!

---

Footnote 3:5. For *ga’al* “to disqualify,” see Ezra 2:62 (= Nehemiah 7:64; so for example M. Kimhi).

Footnote 3:5. *Kamrir* means “darkening”; compare *nikmar* “charred” (see for example M. Kimhi).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:6

------------------------------------------------------------------------

As for that night—Let pitch-dark remove it!

May it have no part in the days of the year,

May it have no place in the count of the months!

---

Footnote 3:6. Reading *yeḥad*, literally, “to be at one with,” with some traditional as well as modern commentators; for the same pair—*ba’* and *yaḥad*—in parallelism, see Genesis 49:6.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:7

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Yes, as for that night—Let it be sterile!

May no joy (of love) take place on it!

---

Footnote 3:7. The word for “sterile” also denotes, as in Arabic, a solitary crag (so Gordis, *Book of Job*). The image of the lone pillar reinforces our perception of Job’s sense of isolation.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:8

------------------------------------------------------------------------

May they condemn it—the cursers of Yamm,

Those armed with a curse for Leviathan!

---

Footnote 3:8. The mythological sea monster that God had to restrain in creating the earth (for example Psalm 74:13–14; see further Job 38:8–11). Although the Hebrew word is vocalized *yom*, the word for “day,” the juxtaposition with Leviathan makes the primary reference clear. *Yom* is the Phoenician pronunciation of Yamm.

Footnote 3:8. The verb *‘arar*, cognate to Arabic *‘arra* “to vilify,” is known in Hebrew in the form *‘ariri* “disgraced” (see especially Jeremiah 22:30).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:9

------------------------------------------------------------------------

May the stars of the twilight turn dark,

That (the night) wait for the light—but there be none!

Let it see not a glimmer of dawn!


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:10–12

------------------------------------------------------------------------

For it would not lock the doors of my womb,

And hide life’s travails from my eyes.

Why couldn’t I die after leaving the womb—

Just go out the loins and stop breathing?

For what did knees have to receive me?

For what were the breasts that I sucked?

---

Footnote 3:10–12. Job sees his mother’s womb as his own; see the introduction to this volume. In contrast to Jeremiah, who curses the day of his birth but mentions his father, his mother, and the messenger who brought the good news (20:14–18), Job will not refer to any person involved in his birth. He even refers to himself at birth as a “man” (verse 3), not a child.

Footnote 3:10–12. Compare Ecclesiastes 4:3.

Footnote 3:10–12. Probably the knees of the mother (Genesis 30:3), possibly those of the father or grandfather (see Genesis 48:12; 50:23). Job refers to disembodied limbs and not to the people to whom they belong.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:13–15

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I could just have lain down in silence,

Slept and enjoyed my repose;

Together with kings and with counselors,

Who build palace-tombs for themselves.

Or with nobles, possessors of gold,

Who fill up their grave-homes with silver.

---

Footnote 3:13–15. Literally, “ruins”; but for *ḥorabot* one may read *haramot* “pyramids” (*mr* in Egyptian; *harama* in Arabic).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:16

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Why couldn’t I be like a stillborn,

Just covered over (in the sand),

Like babies who never saw light?


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:17–19

------------------------------------------------------------------------

There (in the grave)—no more restless are the troubled;

And there the failing of strength find repose.

All prisoners are (there) at peace;

They hear not the voice of their oppressor.

The small and the great, there are the same;

And a slave is set free from his master.

---

Footnote 3:17–19. For this sense of *rasha‘*, ordinarily “wicked”—which is inapt here—see 34:29 (Ibn Ezra).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:20–22

------------------------------------------------------------------------

Why give light to one in travail?

Or life to those bitter of spirit?—

Those waiting for death, but there is none,

Though they dig for it more than for treasure!

Those singing for joy at the mouth of the tomb,

Who are glad to be reaching the grave.

---

Footnote 3:20–22. “Buried, covered over” treasure, echoing the “covered over” stillborn of verse 16 above.

Footnote 3:20–22. Literally, at the stone “rolled across” the mouth of the tomb (compare *golel* in the Mishnah).


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:23

------------------------------------------------------------------------

(Why give light) to a man

Whose path is hidden from Eloah,

Who screens him off from his sight?

---

Footnote 3:23. Drawing on Isaiah 40:27.

Footnote 3:23. The man.

Footnote 3:23. God’s.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:24

------------------------------------------------------------------------

For my moans come to me like my bread,

And my growls are doled out like my water.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:25

------------------------------------------------------------------------

A fear I have feared—and it’s come about;

Just what I dreaded befell me.


------------------------------------------------------------------------

## Job 3:26

------------------------------------------------------------------------

I’ve had no rest, I’ve had no quiet,

I’ve had no repose—restlessness comes.

*For many reasons the passage 4:12–21 should be read here, right after chapter 3, as the conclusion of Job’s opening speech. One may suppose that two pages of ancient papyrus or parchment containing the two equal halves of chapter 4 were accidentally interchanged in the course of the text’s transmission. In an oft-compared Babylonian composition about a pious sufferer (“I Shall Praise the Lord of Wisdom”) it is the complainant, not the would-be sage, who experiences a divine revelation. It is also Job the sufferer, not his companions, who receives a theophany near the end of the book. More important, in the ensuing chapters both Eliphaz and Job refer to Job’s claim to have enjoyed a revelation. Further, Eliphaz (in chapter 15) and Bildad (in chapter 25) cite the words of the revelation as Job’s, and Elihu, who engages only with the arguments of Job, quotes from it (33:15). These and other reasons for rearranging the two halves of chapter 4 will be indicated in the notes (see also Ken Brown). The renegade spirit that discloses divine secrets to Job recalls the Mesopotamian god Ea, who reveals the secret of the forthcoming deluge to the human flood hero (Weinfeld).*
