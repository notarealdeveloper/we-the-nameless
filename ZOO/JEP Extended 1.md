
Granting your source model, I think there is a recoverable-looking post-Torah E trail and an even clearer post-Torah P trail—but they behave differently. E seems to survive as northern prophetic narrative islands, whereas P survives most visibly as a continuous administrative/cultic stratum in Joshua, with only scattered P-like material after that.

The highest-SNR results are:

E: Joshua 24; Judges 6–9; substantial pieces of 1 Samuel 1–12, especially 1–3 and 9–10; and, crucially, 1 Kings 11:29–14:18 around Jeroboam, with 1 Kings 13 looking extraordinarily E-compatible under your hypothesis.

P: Joshua 13–22, especially 17–22, is the monster. Joshua 22 is almost comically P-marked. After Joshua, genuine P becomes much harder to identify. 1 Kings 6–8 does contain real quasi-Priestly material, but there are strong textual reasons to think at least some of it is secondary supplementation rather than your Hezekian P himself. I find essentially nothing comparably P-ish in 2 Kings 18–20 around Hezekiah.


1. The post-Torah E trail

Joshua 24 — ★★★★★

This is the first place I would put a giant red circle.

Joshua 24 is at Shechem, not Jerusalem. Joshua acts as Moses-like covenant mediator. There is a historical recital, repeated Elohim language, covenant-making, a stone witness, and an obsession with rejecting foreign gods. Older source criticism has very often regarded Joshua 24 as E or at least as preserving an E-like conclusion to the patriarch/Moses story; even BDB-style source assignments preserve that older identification. 

Under your reconstruction, there is an extremely attractive possibility:

E originally did not end at Deuteronomy. E's story culminated at Shechem.

That makes enormous narrative sense. Northern E begins with patriarchal traditions heavily invested in Bethel/Shechem/Ephraim and ends with Israel entering the land and covenanting at Shechem, the future northern political center.

I would treat this as the control specimen for looking farther forward.


---

Judges 6–8: Gideon — ★★★★★

This is probably the strongest post-Joshua candidate for the same literary ecosystem as E.

Look at the cluster:

Northern setting: Manasseh/Ephraim.

Prophet: Judges 6:7–10 begins with an anonymous prophet delivering a Mosaic historical indictment.

Divine messenger: Judges 6 repeatedly moves between the angel of YHWH, angel of God, YHWH, and God in exactly the sort of slippery divine-agent narration familiar from JE. “Angel of God” is itself strongly associated with E-like material elsewhere. 

Fire miracle: 6:21—the messenger touches the offering and fire shoots up from the rock.

Baal polemic: Gideon's defining act is smashing Baal's altar and Asherah.

Immediate divine signs: fleece wet/dry.

Early rising: 6:38 and 7:1. The “rise early” verb is conspicuous here; the concordance also shows its huge concentration in J/E material in the Hexateuch. 

Dream: 7:13–15 is spectacularly E-like. Gideon overhears a Midianite recounting a symbolic dream, its companion interprets it, Gideon understands the divine message and acts.

That combination—northern hero + prophet + angel + anti-Baal polemic + dream + interpretation + signs + early morning—is much more persuasive than a single shared word.

If E is a northern prophetic author around the early monarchy, Gideon feels like home turf.


---

Judges 9: Abimelech — ★★★★☆

I would append at least some of this to the same dossier.

Shechem dominates. The story is violently anti-dynastic. Abimelech is made king at Shechem and immediately becomes a disaster. Jotham gives an anti-monarchical tree fable. Elohim sends an evil spirit between Abimelech and the Shechemites. Divine retribution operates almost mechanically.

It also gives you the northern political ideology that would make sense for someone writing after Jeroboam:

> kingship at Shechem is legitimate only conditionally; hereditary royal ambition is dangerous; God can destroy the king and the city together.



Not enough linguistic evidence to call it “E” confidently, but E-school theology is extremely plausible.


---

1 Samuel 1–3 — ★★★★☆

This is another very serious candidate.

Shiloh is an Ephraimite/northern sanctuary. Samuel grows up there. The priestly family of Eli is condemned. The divine message comes at night while Samuel is lying down, with repeated calls whose source he initially cannot identify. It is technically not called a ḥălôm “dream,” but functionally it occupies precisely the E dream/revelation niche.

And 1 Samuel 1:19 gives you another explicit early morning departure. 

The ideology is interesting under your Aaronid/Mushite model. This is a story about:

an old hereditary priesthood being declared corrupt and doomed, while prophetic authority passes to an outsider child chosen directly by God.

That is almost exactly the kind of thing an E-type northern prophetic writer likes to say.

There are also well-recognized literary correspondences between 1 Samuel 2–10 and Pentateuchal narratives—the house of Eli/Aaron, Ark/plagues, manna/rejection themes—which means the affinity is real even if scholars disagree about which direction dependence runs. 


---

1 Samuel 9–10: Saul meets Samuel — ★★★★★

This may be the most E-sounding prose outside the Hexateuch.

You get:

“man of God” as the operative title.

Samuel is a seer.

A geographically northern/Benyaminite environment.

A high place that is treated perfectly normally rather than condemned.

Sacrifice outside Jerusalem.

Repeated divinely supplied signs.

A band of prophets.

The spirit of God suddenly seizing Saul.

Prophetic ecstasy.

Lots being used to reveal the divinely chosen king in 10:20–21.

And the narrative is almost comically interested in Elohim: “man of God,” “hill of God,” “spirit of God,” etc.

Even the vocabulary distribution is suggestive: “family/clan” appears technically in Saul's identification in 1 Samuel 9:21 and 10:21. 

If someone handed me Genesis–Kings with the book divisions removed and said “find the nearest thing to E after Joshua,” I would probably pick Judges 6–8 and 1 Samuel 9–10.


---

Now the interesting one: Jeroboam

I think your intuition is right. There is something sitting right where one would predict an anti-Jeroboam E dossier.

1 Kings 11:29–39 — Ahijah and Jeroboam — ★★★★☆

A prophet from Shiloh meets Jeroboam outside Jerusalem, performs a symbolic act with a garment, and announces the northern schism in advance.

Shiloh + northern king + prophet + symbolic prophetic action is extremely compatible with an E milieu.

But it has clearly undergone Deuteronomistic formulation—the speech contains language about commandments, statutes, David, Jerusalem, etc.

So I would reconstruct:

old northern Ahijah/Jeroboam oracle → Dtr expansion.


---

1 Kings 12:25–33 — calves at Bethel and Dan — ★★★★★ ideologically, ★★★ linguistically

Within your hypothesis this is almost too perfect.

Jeroboam establishes Bethel and Dan, makes two calves, establishes non-Levitical priests, invents a festival, and effectively reproduces the Golden Calf problem.

If E's Exodus 32 is an anti-Jeroboam/Aaron polemic, then 1 Kings 12 and Exodus 32 look like two ends of the same polemical wire.

The complication is compositional direction:

Did E write Exodus 32 by caricaturing Jeroboam's calves?

or

Did the Kings writer describe Jeroboam using language derived from Exodus 32?

Your dating makes the former extremely attractive.

But the final Kings wording is heavily Dtr-shaped.


---

1 Kings 13 — ★★★★★★★

This is the passage I would investigate hardest.

It is bizarre, archaic-feeling prophetic folklore and sits immediately after Jeroboam's altar at Bethel.

An anonymous man of God comes from Judah.

He attacks the altar at Bethel.

He predicts its destruction.

A miraculous sign occurs immediately.

Jeroboam's hand suddenly withers and is restored.

Another old prophet lives at Bethel.

God's command is oddly absolute: don't eat, drink, or return the same way.

The prophet disobeys because another prophet deceives him.

Immediate divine punishment follows.

A lion kills him but then behaves supernaturally: it does not eat the corpse or kill the donkey.

The donkey and lion stand together beside the body as witnesses.

This is precisely the motif bundle Robert Gnuse identifies when comparing northern prophetic traditions with E: prophetic identity, divine messenger traditions, animals behaving as divine agents, immediate retribution, fear/obedience, fire/theophanic motifs, etc. Gnuse actually argues that northern prophetic stories in Samuel–Kings and E belong to the same literary-theological ecosystem, although he reverses your chronology and makes those stories predecessors of E. 

Under your chronology, turn his arrow around:

> 1 Kings 13 may preserve an E-like anti-Bethel/anti-Jeroboam prophetic story subsequently embedded by Dtr.



This is the best answer to your specific question, “Is there E near Jeroboam?”

Yes. 1 Kings 13 is where I would dig.

Then continue through 1 Kings 14:1–18, another Ahijah/Jeroboam prophetic doom story.


---

Elijah/Elisha: E's descendants rather than E itself

1 Kings 17–2 Kings 8 is overwhelmingly northern prophetic literature. Scholars have long noticed its distinctive linguistic and narrative character; the concentration of anomalies is particularly high in 1 Kings 17 and 2 Kings 4–6. 

And the motifs are E all over again:

prophets, “man of God,” Horeb, “mountain of God,” divine fire, angelic mediation, miraculous food, children, dangerous divine holiness, animals, instantaneous reward/punishment.

Oxford's summary of the Elisha cycle notes the recurring “sons of the prophets,” Gehazi, miracle tales, foreigners, and the overwhelmingly northern setting. 

But your early date for E creates a hard chronological limit. Elijah/Elisha concern ninth-century events after an E written soon after 922.

So I would call them:

E-family northern prophetic literature, not E itself, unless “E” became a continuing scribal/prophetic school.

That distinction matters.


---

2. P after the Torah

Here the result is cleaner.

Joshua 13–21 — ★★★★★

Yes. Your “second half of Joshua suddenly becomes a government land-registry spreadsheet” observation is basically the right smell test.

The characteristic package is:

tribal allotments
boundaries
lots
inheritances
clans
tribes
Eleazar the priest
Joshua + Eleazar acting jointly
heads of ancestral houses
cities of refuge
Levitical towns
pasturelands
precise totals
formulaic repetition

Joshua 19:51 practically announces the Priestly administrative constitution: Eleazar, Joshua, tribal heads, lots, Shiloh, Tent of Meeting. 

Joshua 21 then explodes into repetitive genealogical-administrative prose: Kohathites, Gershonites, Merarites, Aaronids, exact numbers, cities and pasturelands repeated ad nauseam. 

This is not merely “P-like.”

There is serious scholarship explicitly examining Priestly legal material incorporated into Joshua, including cities of refuge, Levitical towns, central worship, Passover, and Joshua 22. 


---

Joshua 22 — ★★★★★★★

And here you hit lexical pay dirt.

P's weird death verb גוע / gāwaʿ, “expire,” occurs in Joshua 22:20.

BDB's distribution is extraordinary:

> prose uses are essentially P, with Joshua 22:20 included alongside them.



It explicitly summarizes the verb as “only P & poetry.” 

Then add נשיא / nāśîʾ “chieftain.”

BDB's entry is even more brutal: the technical “leaders of the congregation” usages in Joshua 9, 17 and 22 are assigned to P, and the word otherwise virtually disappears from the Former Prophets. Outside those Joshua passages, Kings has essentially Solomon in 1 Kings 11:34 and a probable gloss in 8:1. 

Then add עדה / ʿēdâ “congregation.”

BDB says that outside exceptional occurrences it is overwhelmingly P's technical word for Israel, and specifically lists:

Joshua 18:1; 22:12, 16–20, 30

among the priestly-style occurrences. 

So Joshua 22 gives you simultaneously:

עדה
נשיאים
גוע
Phinehas son of Eleazar
tribal representatives
cultic purity
altar legitimacy
corporate guilt
Numbers-style precedent
repetitious juridical argument

That is a P fingerprint, not a vibes-based assignment.

Joshua 22 itself is compositionally layered—modern analysis likewise finds a base altar narrative plus subsequent cultic revision. 

I would therefore modify your hypothesis slightly:

> P probably supplied or heavily revised a substantial block extending through Joshua 22, not merely the land lists.




---

What about P in Samuel–Kings?

Here I get a surprisingly negative result.

Your pet markers mostly fall off a cliff after Joshua.

Gāwaʿ “expire” occurs in Joshua 22:20 and then never again in Samuel–Kings. 

Technical nāśîʾ likewise virtually vanishes. BDB's distribution is striking: Joshua's occurrences cluster with P; Kings has only isolated exceptional cases. 

Technical ʿēdâ largely disappears too. 

“Gathered to his people” does not become a royal death formula. Kings instead uses its characteristic royal formula “slept with his fathers.”

That strongly suggests that whatever source extended P into Joshua did not continue as a running P history through Judges–Kings.


---

Solomon's Temple: however, something very interesting happens

1 Kings 6–8 — ★★★★☆ P-like, but probably not simply P

Your instinct here is good.

The architectural obsession, measurements, sacred spaces, cherubim, priests, ark, dedication, repeated technical descriptions and cultic hardware absolutely resemble Priestly writing.

And there is now unusually strong textual evidence for quasi-Priestly expansions in the Masoretic form of 1 Kings 6–8.

Guy Darshan's detailed comparison of MT with the Septuagint argues that MT contains Priestly-style additions intended explicitly to make Solomon's Temple look like the continuation of P's Tabernacle. He identifies brief expansions in 1 Kings 8 and a larger Priestly-like insertion in 1 Kings 6. 

That is extremely relevant to your question.

But there's a catch.

Because some of these readings differ between the MT and the older Greek textual tradition, the simplest explanation is later quasi-Priestly scribal supplementation, not necessarily the eighth-century P author himself. 

So I would rank:

old Temple building source: certainly pre-Dtr material possible.

P-style Temple redaction: definitely detectable.

the same Hezekian P who wrote Genesis–Numbers: possible under your model, but much less demonstrable.

One charming detail: 1 Kings 8:1 contains nəśîʾê hāʾābôt, exactly the sort of terminology we want—but BDB notes that it is probably a gloss and absent from the Greek textual tradition. 

That is almost a miniature demonstration of what happened:

> Somebody with Priestly vocabulary has been touching Kings.




---

Hezekiah himself: surprisingly, no

I looked specifically because your chronology predicts this beautifully.

If P is active under Hezekiah, perhaps the Hezekiah narrative should suddenly sound P-ish.

It doesn't.

2 Kings 18–20 is royal-prophetic historiography:

Hezekiah's reform, Assyria, Rabshakeh, Isaiah, prayer, miraculous deliverance, sickness, sign, Babylonian embassy, waterworks.

It does not suddenly acquire P's technical vocabulary, genealogical structuring, sacrificial taxonomy, ʿēdâ, nāśîʾ, “according to their clans,” “expire,” etc.

The extremely priestly Hezekiah material exists instead in 2 Chronicles 29–31—priests, Levites, Aaronids, blood manipulation, sin offerings, divisions, tithes, genealogical enrollment, holy contributions—but that is precisely the Chronicler's much later historiographical world, not evidence for P embedded in Kings. 

So your hypothesis produces an interesting asymmetry:

P writes during Hezekiah, but apparently does not write Hezekiah.

That actually isn't strange if P's project is rewriting primordial Israel, not contemporary historiography.

And Joshua 22 is especially powerful because we aren't merely saying “this sounds priestly”: three of your requested diagnostic lexemes/semantic systems converge there, and the old lexicographical source criticism independently labels precisely those Joshua usages as P. 

The next useful move would be a machine-like verse-by-verse scan of Joshua–2 Kings for ~30 E markers and ~30 P markers, producing a heat map. That would immediately tell us whether 1 Kings 13, Judges 6–8, 1 Samuel 9–10, Joshua 22, and 1 Kings 6–8 really are statistical islands rather than cases we've noticed by eye.
