
Amos is funny in a rather different way from Hosea. Hosea's comedy is domestic catastrophe played completely straight; Amos is more like an angry rural comic who keeps setting up his audience to applaud and then reveals they're the punchline.

The richest material for your card is:

Amos 1:3–2:5 — the increasingly comfortable audience.
“Damascus? Fuck ’em.” “Gaza? Yep.” “Tyre? Absolutely.” “Edom? Bastards.” “Ammon? Monsters.” “Moab? Sure.” “Judah? GET ’EM.”
Then 2:6: “For three crimes of Israel, and for four…”
That's an excellent comic structure. Amos has spent six denunciations getting an Israelite audience into the rhythm before turning directly on them.

Amos 2:6 — selling somebody for a pair of sandals.
Israel “sells the righteous for silver, and the needy for a pair of sandals.” The deliberately pathetic price makes the accusation sting. For a power name: PAIR OF SANDALS — Target creature is worth basically nothing.

Amos 3:3–6 — the relentless rhetorical-question routine.
“Do two walk together unless they've agreed? Does a lion roar without prey? Does a bird fall into a trap when there's no trap? Does a trumpet sound and nobody freaks out?”
It's practically a stand-up escalation: Guys. Effects have causes. Keep up.

Amos 3:12 — the shepherd proudly recovering two legs and an ear.
This one is magnificent. A shepherd rescues from a lion's mouth two shinbones and a piece of an ear, and Amos says that's how Israel will be rescued.
In modern comic language:
GOOD NEWS: WE FOUND MOST OF YOUR LEG.
Or: SUCCESSFUL RESCUE — Return two legs and an ear to their owner's hand.

Amos 4:1 — “Cows of Bashan.”
He addresses wealthy Samarian women as “cows of Bashan”, who oppress the poor and tell their husbands, essentially, “Bring us something to drink!”
This is almost impossible to improve:

> “Listen up, you cows of Bashan…”
“You crush the poor…”
“…and tell your husbands, ‘Bring us drinks.’”



Amos 4:4–5 — sarcastically encouraging people to sin harder.
“Come to Bethel—and transgress! To Gilgal—and multiply transgression!” Then: bring your sacrifices, announce your voluntary offerings, brag about them, “for this is what you love.”
This is savage parody of pilgrimage advertising.
COME TO BETHEL! SIN MORE! would make a fantastic card ability.

Amos 4:6–11 — “and yet you didn't come back to me.”
YHWH lists progressively absurd levels of corrective disaster: famine, drought, crop failure, mildew, locusts, plague, military defeat, Sodom-level destruction. After each: “Yet you did not return to me.”
It develops into a dark running gag. Amos's Israel has sitcom-character levels of inability to take a hint.

Amos 4:12 — “Prepare to meet your God.”
After that sequence, this lands wonderfully. Basically:
Okay. I tried subtlety.

Amos 5:18–20 — “You want the Day of YHWH? WHY?”
This is one of his best reversals. People apparently anticipate YHWH's day as their victory. Amos: Why the hell would you want that? It's darkness, not light.
Then the slapstick simile: a man runs away from a lion, encounters a bear, finally gets home, puts his hand against the wall—and a snake bites him.
That's practically a three-beat comedy routine:

LION → BEAR → “Thank God I'm home” → SNAKE.

Amos 6:1 — “Woe to those who are at ease in Zion.”
He starts attacking the comfortable upper class and just keeps turning the screws.

Amos 6:4–6 — luxury douchebag montage.
They lounge on ivory beds, sprawl on couches, eat lambs and calves, improvise music, “like David,” drink wine from bowls, and use expensive oils—while not giving a shit that Joseph is collapsing.
The wine from bowls detail is particularly caricatural. Not glasses. Bowls.

Amos 6:5 — “They improvise on musical instruments and, like David, invent instruments for themselves.”
Depending on exactly how the difficult wording is understood, there's potentially a sneering imitation-of-David edge here: rich dilettantes congratulating themselves on their musical sophistication while society collapses.

Amos 6:9–10 — “DON'T SAY YHWH.”
After mass death, somebody comes to remove the bodies. He asks whether anyone is left. “No.” Then: “Hush! We must not mention the name of YHWH.” Extremely black comedy.

Amos 7:1–6 — Amos talks God down. Twice.
YHWH shows Amos catastrophic visions. Amos essentially says, “Come on, Jacob is tiny. How's he supposed to survive that?” And YHWH relents. Then it happens again. There's something almost routine-like about the repetition.

Amos 7:7–8 — the famous plumb line, although the joke may be better than “plumb line.”
Hebrew אֲנָךְ / ʾănāḵ is notoriously obscure. “Plumb line” is traditional but uncertain. This is worth treating carefully rather than building a joke entirely on the English rendering.

Amos 7:10–17 — Amos vs. Amaziah is probably your best characterization scene.
Priest tells Amos to fuck off back to Judah: essentially, “Go earn your bread there. Stop prophesying at Bethel. This is the king's sanctuary.”
Amos responds: “I wasn't a prophet. I wasn't a prophet's son. I was a cattle guy and worked sycamore figs. YHWH grabbed me from behind the flock.”
And then, having established his blue-collar credentials, Amos tells the priest that his wife will become a prostitute, his children will die, his land will be divided up, and he'll die abroad.
Amaziah: Get a real job.
Amos: I HAD ONE.

Amos 8:1–2 — the fruit-basket pun.
This is absolutely card material. YHWH shows Amos a basket of קַיִץ / qayitz, “summer fruit.”
“What do you see?”
“A basket of qayitz.”
“The qetz (קֵץ, end) has come for Israel.”
It's literally divine dad-joke → national annihilation.

YHWH: What's this?
Amos: Summer fruit.
YHWH: Correct. Anyway, you're all going to die.

Amos 8:11–12 — famine, but not for food.
Another bait-and-switch: YHWH announces a famine—then clarifies, not bread, not water; hearing the words of YHWH. People stagger everywhere looking for a word and can't find one.

Amos 9:1 — “I saw the Lord standing beside the altar. He said: HIT THE CAPITAL.”
The sanctuary itself becomes the demolition site. The religious institution isn't where you escape judgment; it's apparently where YHWH starts swinging.


For the Amos card, though, I think Hosea's four-panel format practically writes itself:

Panel 1: Amos, deadpan rural-guy delivery:
“Y'know, I asked YHWH what he thinks of our neighbors.”

Panel 2:
“Damascus. Gaza. Tyre. Edom. Ammon. Moab.”
Audience increasingly delighted.

Panel 3:
“He says they're all fucked.”
Audience cheering.

Panel 4: Amos looks around at them:
“Anyway. Israel.”

And the powers should absolutely include COWS OF BASHAN, TWO LEGS AND AN EAR, LION → BEAR → SNAKE, and BASKET OF SUMMER FRUIT / THE END. Those four together communicate Amos's actual literary personality far better than generic “prophecy” powers.
