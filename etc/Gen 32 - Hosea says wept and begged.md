I tested it pretty hard, and the result is more interesting than the original hypothesis:

The direct claim “ישראל is secretly derived from שאל = ‘Please, God!’” is weak. But the larger literary reading—Israel is being made to perform asking/supplication, in an Esau-saturated scene that ends in submission and restoration of the blessing—is surprisingly strong. And Hosea 12 supplies an independent ancient witness that makes the whole thing much harder to dismiss.

1. The direct שאל → ישראל derivation does not pass

Ugaritic really does attest yšʾal, from the exact cognate root šʾl “ask,” so yišʾal is not a form we're inventing. 

But:

ישאל = yišʾal, “he asks”

ישראל = yiśrāʾēl, “Israel”


In ancient consonantal writing those are extraordinarily suggestive visually:

ישאל
ישראל

The latter is basically the former with ר inserted after ש. But spoken Hebrew had a real š / ś distinction, and the r cannot be generated from שאל by any reasonable morphology. So I would now reject “Israel < yišʾal + El” as an actual etymology.

As secondary paronomasia, though, it remains possible.

And the text behaves exactly as though it wants שאל ringing in your ears.

2. שאל is deliberately activated before and after the naming

Earlier in the chapter Jacob anticipates Esau specifically doing the asking:

> when Esau your brother meets you
ושאלך
“and asks you…”
“Whose are you? Where are you going? Whose are these?”



That is Gen 32:18. 

Then comes the wrestling and naming:

מה שמך
“What is your name?”

יעקב
“Jacob.”

לא יעקב יאמר עוד שמך כי אם ישראל
“Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel…”

And the very next verse begins:

וישאל יעקב
“And Jacob asked/requested…”

followed by:

הגידה־נא שמך
“Please tell your name.”

and then:

למה זה תשאל לשמי
“Why do you ask/request concerning my name?”

So within the chapter the pattern is:

Esau → שאל → identity questions

then

mysterious opponent → identity question → ישראל

then immediately

Jacob → שאל → נא ‘please’ → שאל → identity question. 

That is much more compelling than an isolated phonetic resemblance.

3. And notice what happens immediately after Jacob becomes “Israel”

This may be the strongest internal evidence for your intuition.

He is pronounced ישראל, and then the narrator immediately says:

וישאל יעקב

“and Jacob asked/requested.”

Not:

וישאל ישראל.

He deliberately calls him Jacob again while describing him performing שאל. 

So purely as literary wordplay, you can almost hear:

יִשְׂרָאֵל

…and what does newly named Israel do?

וַיִּשְׁאַל יַעֲקֹב.

He asks.

And what does he actually say?

נא — please.

So “Please, God!” is too loose as a dictionary translation of שאל. שאל means ask/request/inquire; נא supplies the overt “please.”

But as an English rendering of the joke, it becomes startlingly apt:

> “Your name is Israel.”

And Jacob asks:

“Please, tell me your name.”

“Why are you asking my name?”



That passes my literary-coherence test much better than it passes my historical-etymology test.

4. Then I checked Hosea 12. This is the bombshell.

Hosea is our ancient interpretation of this exact wrestling story.

Hosea 12:5 says:

וַיָּשַׂר אֶל־מַלְאָךְ וַיֻּכָל בָּכָה וַיִּתְחַנֶּן־לוֹ

“He strove with an angel and prevailed; he wept and supplicated/begged him.” 

Now remove the word division from the beginning:

וישר אל

becomes:

וישראל

That's exactly “and Israel” in consonantal Hebrew.

Seriously:

וישר אל
w-y-ś-r ʾ-l
“and he strove with…”

וישראל
w-y-ś-r-ʾ-l
“and Israel”

Same consonantal sequence.

So Hosea creates a vastly better Israel pun than our hypothetical שאל derivation:

> וישר אל ≈ וישראל



And in the very same clause, immediately after this Israel-shaped string, somebody:

בכה ויתחנן לו

> “wept and begged/supplicated him.” 



That is extraordinary for the hypothesis you're testing.

It means an ancient Israelite author interpreting this story already associated:

ISRAEL-shaped wordplay → wrestling → prevailing → pleading/begging.

And the subject of the begging is itself famously ambiguous.

Medieval Jewish interpreters including Radak explicitly took it as the angel crying and begging Jacob to release him, connecting it directly with שלחני, “release me,” in Genesis. 

So your instinct that someone in this wrestling scene is reduced to “please!” has remarkably ancient textual support. Hosea outright says somebody wept and begged.

5. Now test the Esau identification

This becomes stronger too—but not certain.

Steven Molen's published analysis of Genesis 32 makes essentially the argument we've been circling independently: Jacob spends the entire lead-up terrified of Esau; the unidentified “man” appears exactly before the encounter; the wrestling recapitulates Jacob and Esau's womb-struggle; and the mysterious man's speech echoes Esau. Molen asks explicitly whether the man's voice is effectively Esau's voice. 

More importantly, Molen notices the name question.

In Genesis 27, Isaac effectively asks Jacob who he is.

Jacob answers:

> “I am Esau.”



He lies about his identity in order to obtain Esau's blessing.

Now, on the eve of finally meeting Esau, the mysterious opponent asks:

מה שמך

> “What's your name?”



And this time:

ויאמר יעקב

> “He said: Jacob.”



He finally gives his actual name.

Molen argues that the mysterious wrestler is constructed as a sort of composite of the figures in Jacob's unresolved family history, with Esau especially prominent. 

So your joking paraphrase—

> “Say your name.”



—is actually getting at something narratively serious:

Last time Jacob was challenged about his identity while obtaining a blessing, he said “Esau.”

This time, while demanding a blessing, he is challenged about his identity again.

And he finally says:

> Jacob.



Then the other guy says, effectively:

> Okay. You're not Jacob anymore.



That's a really tight narrative inversion.

6. Now the ברך / kneeling part

Here I'd modify your hypothesis in an important way.

I would not translate:

ויברך אתו שם

as

> “and he forced him to kneel there.”



The form is Piel and its normal grammatical meaning is “and he blessed him there.” Whatever historical relationship exists between BRK “bless” and BRK “kneel/knee,” you can't simply substitute the Qal sense into this Piel form.

But the story does something much better one paragraph later.

Jacob meets Esau, and:

וישתחו ארצה שבע פעמים

> he bows himself to the ground seven times before Esau. 



Not metaphorically. Not by squeezing an alternative meaning out of ברך.

Jacob actually prostrates himself seven times.

And then Jacob tells Esau:

ראיתי פניך כראת פני אלהים

> “I have seen your face as seeing the face of God.” 



After he had just named Peniel because:

> “I have seen God face to face.”



The text itself therefore superimposes Esau's face on God's face.

And then comes what I think is the most devastating piece of the whole sequence.

Jacob says to Esau:

קח־נא את־ברכתי

> “Please, take my blessing.” 



Not merely “gift.” He calls it:

ברכתי — my blessing.

So the narrative trajectory is:

Gen 27: Jacob pretends to be Esau → takes Esau's blessing.

Gen 32: mysterious Esau/God-like opponent asks “What's your name?” → Jacob finally says Jacob → Jacob demands a blessing → becomes Israel → immediately asks, saying please.

Gen 33: Jacob encounters Esau → bows seven times → says Esau's face is like God's face → says “Please, take my blessing.”

That is remarkably coherent. 

So my verdict changes

I would separate four claims.

“ישראל etymologically derives from שאל + El.”
No. I think that fails philologically.

“The writer is deliberately making ישראל resonate with שאל in this scene.”
Plausible, perhaps moderately strong. The concentration is conspicuous: Esau šʾls earlier; Israel is named; then immediately וישאל יעקב … נא … תשאל. Ugaritic confirms yšʾal is a perfectly ordinary Northwest Semitic form. 

“The naming means something like: you are now the one who asks/pleads before El.”
Possible as a secondary joke, but not demonstrable. I would phrase it as an echo rather than an etymology.

“The scene is an Esau/God overlay in which Jacob is finally made to admit who he is, and the resolution involves asking, supplication, submission, and returning the blessing.”
This I now think is genuinely strong. Genesis 33 almost tells you to overlay the nocturnal opponent and Esau—“seeing your face is like seeing the face of God”—and Hosea independently interprets the wrestling scene using an exact וישר אל / וישראל wordplay followed immediately by weeping and supplication. 

So I wouldn't write the joke as literally:

> “Your name means Please-God. Kneel.”

I'd sharpen it to something more like:

> “What's your name?”
> “Jacob.”
> “Good. For once you didn't say ‘Esau.’
> You're Israel now.”

And Jacob asks:
“Please—tell me your name.”

“Why are you asking my name?”

And the blessing changes hands.

Sunrise.
Jacob sees Esau, bows seven times, and says:
“Please. Take my blessing.”

That last line is not reconstruction. That is essentially what the next scene actually says. 

And Hosea's וישר אל … בכה ויתחנן לו may be the single strongest external evidence that ancient readers were already hearing Israel + wrestling + begging as one wordplay complex.
