# J, E, P, D, and R in the Primary History  
## A source-critical profile and extended source map from Genesis through 2 Kings

### Working assumptions and method

This report deliberately **grants the working chronology and source model specified for the project** rather than attempting to defend it from first principles:

- **J** is a monarchic-era southern narrative extending through substantial portions of Genesis and the following books and plausibly continuing into Samuel, with the Davidic court history / succession narrative representing the later half of the same literary project.

- **E** is a northern narrative, somewhat later than J, composed in the early divided-monarchy period and strongly shaped by northern prophetic interests, anti-Aaronid polemic, and concern with Bethel, Shechem, Ephraim, Joseph, Moses, Joshua, prophets, dreams, divine testing, and mediated revelation.

- **RJE** combined J and E after the fall of the northern kingdom in 722 BCE.

- **P** is a Hezekian-era priestly rewriting of an already-existing JE, producing a deliberately more controlled, genealogical, calendrical, cultic, Aaronid, and bureaucratic history.

- **D / Dtr1** belongs substantially to the Josianic period and is aligned with centralization, covenant obedience, prophetic interpretation of history, and a preference for Mosaic/Mushite authority over autonomous Aaronid cult.

- The **Deuteronomistic Historian** compiled and edited Joshua–Kings, frequently preserving older sources rather than inventing the underlying stories.

- The possibility tested here is that **J, E, and P were originally coherent narratives longer than the Pentateuch**, and that their surviving “later halves” can sometimes be recognized in Joshua–Kings by the same combination of vocabulary, geography, ideology, literary habits, favorite characters, and recurrent narrative structures that distinguishes their Pentateuchal portions.

The method therefore uses **clusters**, not magic words. A single lexeme never proves source identity. The strongest assignments require several independent dimensions to converge:

1. lexical and phraseological fit;
2. geography;
3. divine-name and revelation habits;
4. favorite and disfavored characters;
5. priestly or anti-priestly ideology;
6. legal assumptions;
7. numerical style;
8. narrative form and repeated motifs;
9. relation to other passages already assigned to the same source;
10. plausible position in a larger narrative architecture.

Confidence labels used below:

- **A / very high** — multiple independent markers converge; a source assignment is substantially stronger than generic stylistic resemblance.
- **B / high** — strong thematic and lexical fit but substantial redaction or competing explanations remain.
- **C / medium** — worth treating as a candidate source fragment, but the evidence is not uniquely diagnostic.
- **D / low** — affinity or source-school relationship is more likely than direct authorship.
- **School** — probably not the original author under the assumed chronology, but closely related in language, ideology, and narrative technique.

Modern scholarship no longer agrees on the classical documentary model, especially on a recoverable E; that disagreement is methodologically important. The present exercise asks a different question: **if** coherent J, E, and P documents existed, what reconstruction best explains the literary evidence? That makes this a constrained historical-literary model comparison rather than a claim of scholarly consensus.

# J

## 1. Places

J's geographical center of gravity is southern and monarchic. The source is especially comfortable in **Judah, Hebron, Beersheba, the Negev, Jerusalem, and the southern approaches to Canaan**, while treating the northern heartland more ambivalently.

The patriarchal Abraham cycle repeatedly anchors itself in the south: Hebron/Mamre (Gen 13:18; 18:1), the Negev (Gen 12:9; 13:1), Gerar and Beersheba traditions (Gen 21:22–34 in the combined narrative), and Judah-facing traditions. The Judah-Tamar material is explicitly southern (Gen 38). J's Joseph story can move to Egypt, but its ideological center repeatedly returns to **Judah as spokesman and guarantor** (Gen 43:8–10; 44:18–34).

The post-Torah continuation proposed by Friedman becomes geographically intelligible if the same author eventually reaches **Hebron and Jerusalem**, where David's kingship is established: David first rules Judah at Hebron (2 Sam 2:1–4; 5:1–5), then captures Jerusalem from the Jebusites (2 Sam 5:6–10). The movement from patriarchal Hebron to royal Jerusalem gives J's land story a natural political climax.

J also has a striking interest in liminal or morally dangerous foreign spaces: Egypt (Gen 12:10–20; 39–47 in the combined Joseph cycle), Philistia/Gerar (Gen 26), Sodom (Gen 18–19), Edom/Seir (Gen 25; 27; 32–33; 36 in mixed source material), Moab/Ammon through ancestral etiologies (Gen 19:30–38), and later Philistine territory in the David traditions (1 Sam 27–30).

**Post-Torah places with especially J-like density:** Jericho/Rahab's house (Josh 2; 6), Gibeah and the Benjamin civil-war landscape (Judg 19–21), Hebron and Jerusalem (2 Sam 2–6), David's palace/roof/bedchamber world (2 Sam 11–20), and the court of the aging David (1 Kgs 1–2).

## 2. Names

J characteristically foregrounds **Judah**, **David**, and the family line that can be made to culminate in Davidic monarchy. Judah's transformation into the responsible brother is central in Gen 43:8–10 and 44:18–34. The Tamar narrative makes Judah morally compromised but ultimately capable of admitting "she is more righteous than I" (Gen 38:26), a pattern strikingly similar to J's probable treatment of David: chosen and charismatic, but not sanitized.

J's heroes are frequently **morally compromised but narratively alive**: Abraham deceives (Gen 12:10–20), Jacob deceives and is deceived (Gen 27; 29), Judah sleeps with Tamar (Gen 38), David commits adultery and engineers Uriah's death (2 Sam 11), Amnon rapes Tamar (2 Sam 13), Absalom rebels (2 Sam 15–18), and the royal family becomes a machine for producing consequences. This is not P's idealizing priestly history.

J displays unusual sympathy for **women with agency**: Eve (Gen 3), Sarah (Gen 16; 18), Rebekah (Gen 24; 27 in the combined cycle), Tamar (Gen 38), Rahab (Josh 2), Abigail (1 Sam 25), Bathsheba (2 Sam 11–12; 1 Kgs 1), Tamar daughter of David (2 Sam 13), and the wise women of Tekoa and Abel (2 Sam 14; 20). These women often understand the situation faster than the men and drive the plot by speech, sexuality, disguise, negotiation, or strategic deception.

J is comparatively less invested in Aaron as cultic hero than P and less prophet-centered than E. Moses matters enormously, but J's Moses is embodied, angry, negotiative, and directly engaged with YHWH rather than primarily an institutional transmitter.

## 3. Laws

J does not normally sound like a codifier. Its normativity emerges narratively: hospitality is good (Gen 18:1–8; 19:1–3), betrayal of hospitality is monstrous (Gen 19; Judg 19 if J-related), kinship obligations matter (Gen 38; 43–44), bloodguilt has consequences (Gen 4:10–12; 2 Sam 11–12), oaths and covenants bind (Gen 21:22–34; 26:26–33), and sexual or familial violations destabilize households and kingdoms (Gen 34; 38; 2 Sam 11–18).

J tolerates a religious world with altars, local holy places, meals, stones, trees, and embodied divine encounters without P's apparatus of cultic regulation. Sacrifice is ancient and intuitive rather than available only after Sinai (Gen 4:3–5; 8:20–21). This is one of the sharpest contrasts with P.

If the David Court History is J, J's practical constitutional law is almost anti-law: monarchy is evaluated through narrative consequences. David can technically remain king while his house collapses under the curse announced by Nathan (2 Sam 12:10–12). The source's ethic is often: **deeds reproduce themselves**.

## 4. Numbers

J's favorite numbers are narratively memorable and typological rather than bureaucratically exact. The best-known example is **forty days and forty nights** (Gen 7:4, 12; Exod 24:18; 34:28 in the composite tradition). Seven also functions as a story number: seven days of Flood sequencing in J (Gen 7–8), seven years of labor for Leah and Rachel (Gen 29:18–30), seven ewe-lambs at Beersheba (Gen 21:28–30).

J's numbers typically produce rhythm, irony, or plot architecture. P's numbers look like records; J's look like stories.

This difference becomes useful post-Torah. The mathematically elaborate tribal censuses and cubit measurements of Josh 13–21 or 1 Kgs 6–7 do not look J-like. By contrast, the memorable numbers in the David story—seven years and six months at Hebron (2 Sam 5:5), fourfold restitution in David's own judgment (2 Sam 12:6), the stylized casualty and family counts in the court story—function narratively rather than as administrative databases.

## 6. War

J's wars are frequently consequences of **kinship insult, violated hospitality, sexual violence, political rivalry, or personal loyalty**, not merely abstract covenant infidelity. Dinah's interaction with Shechem leads to the slaughter at Shechem (Gen 34). The outrage at Gibeah leads to civil war (Judg 19–21). David's wars are embedded in personal allegiance and dynastic politics (2 Sam 2–5; 15–20).

J is comfortable portraying war as morally ambiguous. Victors can be compromised; enemies can be sympathetic; chosen figures can behave atrociously. This differs sharply from P's holy-community logic and D's obedience → victory / apostasy → defeat schema.

## 7. Judgements

J's deepest moral interest may be **consequence, irony, and reversal**. Jacob deceives Isaac and is deceived by Laban (Gen 27; 29:25). Judah helps sell Joseph but later offers himself for Benjamin (Gen 37; 44:18–34). David takes another man's wife and is told the sword will not depart from his own house (2 Sam 11–12); sexual violence then erupts inside that house (2 Sam 13), followed by fratricide and rebellion (2 Sam 13–18).

J likes embodied life: food, sex, childbirth, drunkenness, beauty, clothing, animals, smells, washing, sleeping, roofs, tents, fields, wells. The source's literary aesthetic is concrete, comic, tragic, and frequently obscene or double-edged. YHWH can smell sacrifices (Gen 8:21), walk, appear, argue, regret, threaten, and be negotiated with (Gen 18; Exod 32–34 in the composite JE).

The likely later J is suspicious of simplistic royal propaganda. David is favored, but the narrative preserves some of the most damaging material imaginable about him (2 Sam 11–20). That is consistent with an insider who believes in the dynasty but believes even more strongly in narrative truth and retributive irony.

## 8. Priesthood

J is not priestly. Priests are rarely the organizing center of the world. Altars and sacrifices predate Aaron and can be performed by patriarchs or household heads (Gen 4:3–5; 8:20; 12:7–8; 13:18).

If 2 Samuel belongs to J, David himself performs cultic functions around the ark without P's anxiety about Aaronid monopoly (2 Sam 6:13–18). The episode is bodily, dangerous, musical, and theatrical—extremely unlike P's controlled sanctuary manual.

J therefore looks most compatible with a **royal/lay cult in which priests exist but do not monopolize access to YHWH**.

## 9. Kings

J is the source most naturally associated with the **Davidic monarchy**, but not as sycophantic court propaganda. The dynasty is providentially central, while individual kings remain accountable and ridiculous.

The possible J arc is elegant:

- primordial human desire and rivalry (Gen 2–4);
- sibling conflict (Cain/Abel; Jacob/Esau; Joseph/brothers);
- tribal conflict;
- Judah's emergence;
- David's rise;
- David's household reproducing Genesis-scale sibling, sexual, and inheritance catastrophes;
- Solomon's succession (1 Kgs 1–2).

Under this reading, J's "archaic history" and "court history" are mirror images. Genesis is not merely prehistory; it is a mythic model of what royal families are like.

## 10. Dudetheyreontome

**Best social-location estimate:** a literate southern court-connected narrator, with an unusually sophisticated interest in women's speech and agency; adult, perhaps approximately **30–55 years old** at composition; active in or close to the royal bureaucracy but not identical with official royal annalists.

**Point estimate:** Jerusalem/Judah, c. **960–920 BCE ± 50 years** under the working model. Confidence: medium for southern/monarchic location, low-to-medium for precise date and sex.

**People plausibly known personally:** Nathan (2 Sam 7; 12), Bathsheba (2 Sam 11–12; 1 Kgs 1), members of David/Solomon's court, royal messengers and household officials. If one insists on a named biblical candidate, **Nathan** or someone in Nathan's scribal/prophetic circle is attractive because Nathan is simultaneously pro-Davidic dynasty (2 Sam 7), willing to condemn David brutally (2 Sam 12), and involved in Solomon's succession (1 Kgs 1). The evidence is circumstantial, not an identification.

**Prophetic affinity:** Nathan more than Elijah/Elisha. J's deity often acts directly and anthropomorphically; the prophet is important but not the sole channel of revelation.

### Post-Torah source map for J

**A:** Josh 2; Josh 5:13–6:27; much of 2 Sam 2–20; 1 Kgs 1–2.  
**B:** Josh 7; Josh 9; Judg 13–16; Judg 19–21; 1 Sam 25–27; portions of 1 Sam 18–20 and 2 Sam 21–24.  
**C:** Josh 10's older battle/poetic core; Judg 9 where J-like monarchy satire may overlap E geography; selected Saul-David narratives in 1 Sam 16–31.  

The especially under-recognized J candidates are **Josh 2; 5:13–6:27; 7; 9; Judg 19–21**, because the rare vocabulary in Friedman's table repeatedly lands there, not randomly throughout the Former Prophets.

# E

## 1. Places

E's center of gravity is northern: **Ephraim, Manasseh, Joseph territory, Bethel, Shechem, Shiloh, the central hill country, and the northern approach to the Transjordan**.

Classic E geography foregrounds Bethel (Gen 28:10–22 in the E-heavy Jacob tradition), Shechem (Gen 35:4; Josh 24), Joseph/Ephraim (Gen 37–50 in the divided Joseph narrative), and Horeb rather than Sinai (Exod 3:1; 17; 18). The old critical profile specifically notes E's preference for **Amorite** as a general label for the pre-Israelite population (e.g. Gen 48:22; Num 13:29 in assigned E material) and its northern localization.

This makes **Josh 24 at Shechem** an exceptionally natural E ending or major climax. It also makes the following post-Torah clusters geographically conspicuous: Gideon/Abimelech in Manasseh and Shechem (Judg 6–9), Samuel's Shiloh/Ramah/Bethel world (1 Sam 1–12), the Ahijah/Jeroboam cycle at Shiloh and Bethel (1 Kgs 11:29–14:18), and the later northern prophetic world of Elijah/Elisha.

## 2. Names

E favors **Joseph, Ephraim, Joshua, Moses, prophets, "men of God," and elders**. Abraham is explicitly called a **prophet** in Gen 20:7. Moses becomes the paradigmatic mediator, while Joshua is his military servant and successor (Exod 17:8–16; 24:13; 33:11 in the E-associated complex).

Aaron is conspicuously less secure. In the Golden Calf story Aaron is the human agent who makes the calf and then offers an absurd excuse (Exod 32:1–6, 21–24). Miriam and Aaron can oppose Moses (Num 12). This fits a source aligned with **Mushite or prophetic authority rather than Aaronid monopoly**.

E's post-Torah onomastic world therefore predicts:
- Samuel over Eli's failing priestly house (1 Sam 2:27–36; 3);
- Samuel the seer/man of God (1 Sam 9);
- Ahijah of Shiloh over Jeroboam (1 Kgs 11:29–39; 14:1–18);
- anonymous "man of God" traditions, especially 1 Kgs 13;
- prophetic companies and ecstatic prophets (1 Sam 10:5–13; 19:18–24);
- later E-school affinities in Elijah and Elisha.

E may also be unusually interested in **Abimelech**. The Abraham-Abimelech cycle (Gen 20–21) and the Shechemite Abimelech disaster (Judg 9) make a compelling archaic/later-half pairing if E's story was designed recursively: a foreign king named Abimelech who fears God and negotiates justly versus an Israelite Abimelech who becomes a murderous would-be king at Shechem.

## 3. Laws

The legal core most naturally associated with E in the classical model is the **Covenant Code** (Exod 20:22–23:33), although source assignments vary. Its laws are concrete, agrarian, casuistic, and socially oriented: slaves (Exod 21:2–11), homicide and injury (21:12–36), theft and property damage (21:37–22:14), seduction (22:15–16), resident aliens, widows, and orphans (22:20–23), lending and pledges (22:24–26), judicial integrity (23:1–9), sabbatical rhythms (23:10–12), and pilgrimage festivals (23:14–17).

E's religion is compatible with multiple local altars: "in every place where I cause my name to be remembered" (Exod 20:24). That is the opposite of D's Jerusalem-only centralization.

The underlying moral theme is **fear of God as restraint on human power**: Abimelech is prevented from violating Sarah (Gen 20); the midwives fear God rather than Pharaoh (Exod 1:17, 21); Abraham's test is resolved when he is shown to "fear God" (Gen 22:12); judges should be God-fearing and incorruptible (Exod 18:21).

## 4. Numbers

E has a marked affection for **three-day sequences**. Classical source-critical inventories repeatedly notice "three days" in E-associated material: the three-day journey (Exod 3:18; 5:3), three days of darkness (Exod 10:22), three days without water (Exod 15:22), Joseph's three-day imprisonment/testing scheme (Gen 42:17–18), dream interpretations involving three days (Gen 40:12–19), and three-day intervals in conquest traditions (Josh 1:11; 9:16 are relevant parallels even where redaction complicates assignment).

E also likes **dream numbers**: seven cows and ears in Joseph's Pharaoh cycle (Gen 41), paired dreams, paired interpretations, and sequences whose numerical content encodes future events.

Compared with P, E's numbers are symbolic and revelatory rather than census-like.

## 5. Words

The older linguistic inventories of E are unusually helpful because they identify lexical contrasts with J. The following deserve to be treated as a **diagnostic lexicon**, with the warning that many are probabilistic rather than exclusive:

-  ‎**אלהים / האלהים — Elohim / ha-Elohim**, frequently functioning almost as a proper name before and even after the revelation of YHWH (Gen 20; 21; 22; 31; 40–41; 46; Exod 1–3; 18).
- ‎**אלהי אבי / אלהי אביך — "God of my father / your father"** (Gen 31:5, 29, 42; 46:1–3; Exod 3:6; 18:4).
- ‎**ירא אלהים — "fear God"** as a characteristic moral formula (Gen 20:11; 22:12; Exod 1:17, 21; 18:21).
- ‎**נביא — prophet**, with Abraham explicitly so labeled (Gen 20:7).
- ‎**איש האלהים — man of God**, conceptually E-compatible and explosively frequent in Samuel–Kings (1 Sam 2:27; 9:6–10; 1 Kgs 12:22; 13 throughout; 17:18, 24; 20:28; 2 Kgs 1; 4–8).
- ‎**חלום — dream** and dream-introduction formulae such as "in my dream, behold..." (Gen 20:3; 28:12; 31:10–11, 24; 37; 40; 41).
- ‎**מלאך האלהים — angel/messenger of God**, often mediated or heavenly revelation (Gen 21:17; 31:11; Exod 14:19).
- ‎**מן השמים — "from heaven"** in divine/angelic address (Gen 21:17; 22:11, 15).
- ‎**נסה — test** (Gen 22:1; Exod 15:25; 16:4; 20:20 in E-associated contexts).
- ‎**אמה — maidservant**, classically contrasted with J's שפחה in some source-critical distributions.
- ‎**חורב — Horeb**, especially "mountain of God" (Exod 3:1; 17:6; 18:5), versus Sinai in other strata.
- ‎**מחנה — camp** as temporary encampment.
- ‎**נער / ילד — boy/child** in characteristic narrative use.
- ‎**לבב — heart**, often where other prose prefers לב.
- ‎**דבר + ב — speak against** (Num 12:1, 8; 21:5, 7).
- ‎**דבר** as a legal matter/dispute (Exod 18:16, 19, 26; 22:8).
- ‎**הוכיח / נכח** in judicial/reproof contexts.
- ‎**מצא / פגע — meet/encounter**, often in providential encounters.
- ‎**התפלל — intercede/pray** (Gen 20:7, 17; Num 11:2; 21:7 in E-like intercessory patterns).
- ‎**פנים אל פנים — face to face** (Exod 33:11; compare Num 12:8).
- ‎**לא ימוש / מוש — not depart**, especially of Joshua's presence/service language (Exod 33:11; compare Josh 1:8 in Dtr reuse).
- ‎**דור דור — generation after generation**.
- ‎**הנה / בחלומי והנה — "behold" inside dream reports**, a strong E narrative tic.
- **double vocative in divine calls**: "Abraham, Abraham" (Gen 22:11), "Jacob, Jacob" (Gen 46:2), "Moses, Moses" (Exod 3:4).
- ‎**וישכם בבקר — "he rose early in the morning"** is not exclusively E, but the motif is conspicuous in E-like revelation/testing narratives (Gen 20:8; 21:14; 22:3; 28:18; 31:55; 40:6; Exod 8:16; 24:4; 32:6; 34:4 in composite material).

### Post-Torah E marker clusters

The most important post-Torah uses are **clusters rather than isolated lexemes**:

**Judg 6–8:** anonymous prophet (6:7–10), messenger of God/YHWH (6:11–24), divine signs (6:17, 36–40), early rising (6:28, 38; 7:1), a symbolic dream plus interpretation (7:13–15), anti-Baal polemic (6:25–32), northern Manassite geography.

**1 Sam 1–3:** Shiloh, prophetic succession over a corrupt priestly house, night revelation, repeated divine call, "man of God" against Eli (2:27), early rising after worship (1:19), Eli's dim eyes (3:2, a J-vocabulary overlap showing that source seams remain possible).

**1 Sam 9–10:** "man of God" (9:6–10), "seer" (9:9, 11, 18–19), high place and sacrifice (9:12–25), signs (10:1–8), band of prophets (10:5, 10–13), Spirit of God (10:6, 10), northern/Benjaminite geography.

**1 Kgs 11:29–39; 14:1–18:** Ahijah of Shiloh, prophetic symbolic action, Jeroboam, northern kingship, Shiloh.

**1 Kgs 13:** "man of God" repeated obsessively, Bethel, prophetic command and counter-command, sign, immediate divine punishment, miraculous animal behavior. This is one of the densest E-like narratives in Kings.

**Later E-school:** 1 Kgs 17–19; 2 Kgs 1–9. These are chronologically too late to be the original early-9th-century E if the stipulated date is held strictly, but their northern prophetic style is so close that they plausibly preserve an **E-school literary ecology**. Robert Gnuse has independently argued for a relationship between northern prophetic narratives in Samuel–Kings and the Elohist tradition, though he places the chronological arrow differently.

## 6. War

E tends to portray war as something God can win through **sign, oracle, prophet, terror, dream, or divinely chosen agent** rather than through royal military magnificence. Amalek is defeated while Moses mediates with the staff and Joshua fights below (Exod 17:8–16). The conquest can be described as accomplished by divine agents such as the hornet rather than Israel's sword (Exod 23:27–30; Josh 24:12).

Gideon's reduction of the army (Judg 7:1–8), the Midianite dream (7:13–15), and the enemy's self-destruction (7:22) are almost a perfect continuation of this theology.

E's wars often punish **arrogance, oppression, apostasy, or illegitimate cult**, but E is less interested than D in turning every defeat into a sermon on centralized covenant law.

## 7. Judgements

E's values are prophetic and moralizing:

- fear of God over obedience to rulers (Exod 1:17–21);
- justice in adjudication (Exod 18:13–26; 21–23);
- restraint of human violence;
- divine testing (Gen 22:1–19);
- prophetic mediation;
- responsiveness to signs;
- suspicion of images and calf worship (Exod 32);
- suspicion of hereditary priestly claims when they rival Moses/prophecy (Exod 32; Num 12).

E's God is often **less casually anthropomorphic than J's**. Revelation comes through dreams, angels, heaven, signs, or prophets. Yet this should not be exaggerated: E still preserves face-to-face Moses traditions (Exod 33:11; Num 12:6–8).

A recurrent E principle is that **God can communicate outside official institutions**: to Abimelech in a dream (Gen 20:3), to Jacob in dreams (Gen 28; 31), to Pharaoh through dreams interpreted by Joseph (Gen 41), to Balaam (Num 22–24 in mixed E material), to Gideon through an enemy's dream (Judg 7:13–15), and to prophetic outsiders in Samuel–Kings.

## 8. Priesthood

E's preferred authority structure appears **Mushite-prophetic**, not Aaronid.

Moses receives revelation directly (Exod 3; 33:7–11). Joshua serves Moses (Exod 17; 24; 33). Aaron makes the calf (Exod 32). Aaron and Miriam challenge Moses and are rebuked (Num 12). The Tent of Meeting in Exod 33:7–11 is outside the camp and functions as an oracular site where Moses meets YHWH; Joshua stays there.

This maps remarkably well onto Samuel: Shiloh's hereditary priests are condemned (1 Sam 2:27–36), while Samuel receives direct revelation (1 Sam 3) and later anoints kings (1 Sam 9–10; 16 in the final form). A northern E-like author could therefore see Samuel as a post-Mosaic continuation of the same authority type.

## 9. Kings

E is ambivalent or hostile toward monarchy, especially **northern monarchy that appropriates cult**.

The anti-monarchical strand of 1 Sam 8 and 10:17–27; 12 has long been recognized as distinct from more positive Saul accession traditions. Under the present model, at least part of that anti-monarchical material is an excellent E/D overlap zone: northern prophetic ideology that Dtr later found congenial.

Jeroboam is the critical test. If E is written soon after 922 BCE and the Golden Calf story is polemic against Jeroboam's Bethel/Dan calves, then the likely terminus of E's later-half narrative should lie around **Jeroboam himself**. The Ahijah-Jeroboam stories (1 Kgs 11:29–39; 14:1–18), the calf notice (12:25–33), and especially the Bethel man-of-God story (1 Kgs 13) are therefore exactly where the model predicts surviving E.

The most attractive reconstruction is:

**Abraham/Abimelech → Isaac/Jacob/Joseph → Moses/Joshua → northern judges → Samuel → monarchy crisis → Jeroboam/Bethel judgment.**

## 10. Dudetheyreontome

**Best social-location estimate:** northern prophetic-scribal circle with access to sanctuary traditions, especially Bethel/Shiloh/Ephraim; probably literate elite but ideologically suspicious of royal and Aaronid control.

**Point estimate:** central highlands of the northern kingdom, perhaps Bethel–Shiloh–Shechem corridor, c. **900–875 BCE ± 40 years** under the stipulated early post-922 model. If one loosens the model to accommodate more Kings material, a later ninth- or eighth-century E-school becomes plausible.

**Sex/age/social position:** probably male **30–60 ± large error**, because the institutional world is elders, prophets, judges, and sanctuary personnel; sex is low-confidence. More important is occupational identity: **prophetic scribe / sanctuary intellectual / Mushite-Levitical traditionalist**.

**Named candidates:** a Shilonite prophetic circle associated with **Ahijah** is the most intriguing. Ahijah is northern, attached to Shiloh, authorizes Jeroboam and later condemns him (1 Kgs 11:29–39; 14:1–18). An actual identification with Ahijah is unprovable, but "someone in Ahijah's circle" fits the time, place, politics, and literary agenda unusually well.

**Prophetic affinities:** Samuel, Ahijah, and—at the school level—Elijah/Elisha. Hosea is ideologically and geographically close in his anti-calf, anti-monarchical, northern covenantal rhetoric (Hos 4–13), although Hosea is later than the stipulated E.

### Post-Torah source map for E

**A:** Josh 24; Judg 6–8; 1 Sam 9–10; 1 Kgs 11:29–39; 1 Kgs 13; 1 Kgs 14:1–18.  
**B:** Judg 9; 1 Sam 1–3; portions of 1 Sam 7–12; selected northern Saul traditions; 1 Kgs 12:25–33.  
**C:** Judg 10–12; parts of Judg 17–18; Samuel's prophetic-company material (1 Sam 19:18–24).  
**School, not original E under the early date:** 1 Kgs 17–22; 2 Kgs 1–9, especially prophetic miracle cycles.

The most under-recognized candidates are **Judg 6–9, 1 Sam 9–10, and 1 Kgs 13**.

# P

## 1. Places

P thinks spatially in terms of **ordered sacred geography** rather than colorful local landscape. Its characteristic spaces are the **Tabernacle, Tent of Meeting, sanctuary zones, camp, tribal territories, boundaries, inheritances, and Levitical cities**.

In Genesis P is comparatively uninterested in the shrine-aetiology storytelling that characterizes J/E. It maps the world genealogically and territorially (Gen 10; 17; 23; 25; 36 in P-related strata). In Exodus–Numbers the center is the sanctuary and the camp around it (Exod 25–31; 35–40; Num 1–10).

This makes **Josh 13–22** the obvious geographic continuation: cadastral boundaries, inheritances, lots, tribal heads, Shiloh, the Tent, cities of refuge, Levitical cities, and the Transjordanian altar crisis.

Possible later P-like geography appears in **Solomon's Temple** (1 Kgs 6–8), where the Tabernacle's conceptual world is mapped onto a permanent sanctuary, and in the Joash/Jehoiada temple complex (2 Kgs 11–12).

## 2. Names

P overwhelmingly elevates **Aaron, Eleazar, Phinehas, Aaron's sons, Levites, tribal chieftains, ancestral-house heads**, and genealogically specified persons.

Aaron's line is not merely present; it is the legitimate priesthood (Exod 28–29; Lev 8–10; Num 16–18; 25:10–13). Phinehas receives an eternal priestly covenant after killing Zimri and Cozbi (Num 25:6–15).

Moses is indispensable but repeatedly becomes the transmitter of instructions whose institutional beneficiaries are Aaron and his descendants. In several P narratives Moses can even look administratively subordinate to the system he is establishing.

P's animus toward **Midianite sexual/cultic contamination** is fierce: Num 25 links Israelite-Midianite sexuality with apostasy; Num 31 commands war against Midian and gives extraordinarily detailed spoil/purification distributions.

Joshua is paired with **Eleazar the priest** in the transfer and allotment structure (Num 27:18–23; 34:17; Josh 14:1; 17:4; 19:51; 21:1). This pairing is one of the strongest indications that P's story naturally continues into Joshua.

## 3. Laws

P's laws concern **holiness, purity, sacrifice, sacred time, priestly boundaries, bodily states, food, contamination, sanctuary management, and ritual exactness**.

Core domains:
- sacrifices: Lev 1–7;
- ordination: Lev 8–10;
- clean/unclean animals: Lev 11;
- childbirth impurity: Lev 12;
- skin disease and houses: Lev 13–14;
- genital discharges: Lev 15;
- Day of Atonement: Lev 16;
- blood and slaughter: Lev 17;
- priestly holiness: Lev 21–22;
- sacred calendar: Lev 23;
- sanctuary lamps/bread: Lev 24;
- census/camp purity: Num 1–10;
- corpse impurity: Num 19;
- vows, offerings, inheritances, and priestly dues throughout Numbers.

P is especially sensitive to **unauthorized approach to holy space**: Nadab and Abihu die by fire (Lev 10:1–2); Korah's company is destroyed (Num 16); Uzzah in 2 Sam 6 looks superficially P-like in dangerous-holiness logic, though the narrative style itself is much more J/ark-story-like.

## 4. Numbers

P's numerical fingerprint is among the easiest in biblical prose:

- exact ages (Gen 5; 11; 17; 23; 25:7; 35:28; 47:28);
- exact dates to month/day (Gen 7:11; 8:4–5, 13–14; Exod 12:2–6; 16:1; 19:1 in the composite; Num 1:1);
- cubits and architectural dimensions (Gen 6:15–16; Exod 25–27; 36–40);
- huge census totals by tribe (Num 1; 26);
- exact offering inventories (Num 7);
- age windows for service (Num 4; 8);
- exact tribal and Levitical town counts.

The best post-Torah match is therefore **Josh 13–21**. Lists, boundaries, city totals, tribal allotments, and formulaic repetitions look like a direct continuation of Numbers.

**1 Kgs 6–7** also has strong numerical overlap: cubits, dimensions, architectural components, repeated measurement formulas. But textual criticism shows that Kings' temple chapters contain layers, including later quasi-Priestly expansions; P-like style there cannot simply be equated with the original P author.

## 5. Words

P possesses the most diagnostic technical vocabulary of the sources. Important markers include:

- ‎**גוע — "expire / breathe one's last"**: Gen 6:17; 7:21; 25:8, 17; 35:29; Num 17:27–28; 20:3, 29; and strikingly **Josh 22:20**. BDB famously characterizes prose use as essentially "P & poetry," making Josh 22:20 unusually probative.
- ‎**נאסף אל עמיו / נאסף אל אבותיו — "gathered to his people/fathers"**: Gen 25:8, 17; 35:29; 49:29, 33; Num 20:24, 26; 27:13; 31:2; Deut 32:50.
- ‎**עדה — congregation**, especially technical "congregation of Israel": Exod 12; Lev 4; Num throughout; **Josh 18:1; 22:12, 16–20, 30**.
- ‎**נשיא — chieftain/prince**, technical tribal head: Num 1–7; 25; 34; **Josh 9:15, 18–21; 17:4; 22:14, 30–32**.
- ‎**מטה — tribe/staff** in bureaucratic tribal sense, frequently P/Numbers.
- ‎**משפחה / למשפחותם — clan / according to their clans**: genealogies and territorial allotments.
- ‎**בית אב / ראשי אבות — ancestral house / heads of ancestral houses**.
- ‎**אחזת ארץ / אחזה — landed possession**: Gen 17:8; 23; 47:11; 48:4; Lev 25; Num 27; 32; 35.
- ‎**תולדות — generations/account of descendants**: Gen 2:4; 5:1; 6:9; 10:1; 11:10, 27; etc.
- ‎**מולדת — kindred/birth group**.
- ‎**זכר ונקבה — male and female** (Gen 1:27; 5:2; 6–7).
- ‎**למינהו / למיניהם — according to its/their kind(s)** (Gen 1; 6–7; Lev 11).
- ‎**פרה ורבה — be fruitful and multiply** (Gen 1:22, 28; 8:17; 9:1, 7; 17:20; 28:3; 35:11; 48:4).
- ‎**ברית + הקים — establish a covenant** (Gen 6:18; 9:9, 11, 17; 17:7, 19, 21; Exod 6:4).
- ‎**אות — sign** in covenantal/institutional use (Gen 9:12–17; 17:11; Exod 12:13; 31:13, 17).
- ‎**חקת עולם — perpetual statute**.
- ‎**לדרתיכם — throughout your generations**.
- ‎**בעצם היום הזה — on that very day** (Gen 7:13; 17:23, 26; Exod 12:17, 41, 51; Lev 23:14, 21, 28–30; Deut 32:48 in Priestly-style framing).
- ‎**כאשר צוה יהוה את משה — as YHWH commanded Moses**, repeated formulaically through Exod 39–40; Lev; Num.
- ‎**אהל מועד — Tent of Meeting**.
- ‎**משכן — Tabernacle/dwelling**.
- ‎**כבוד יהוה — glory of YHWH** (Exod 16:7, 10; 24:16–17; 40:34–35; Lev 9:6, 23; Num 14:10; 16:19; 17:7; 20:6).
- ‎**קדש / קדש הקדשים — holy / most holy** as technical cultic classification.
- ‎**טמא / טהר — unclean / clean** in formal ritual taxonomy.
- ‎**קרבן — offering**, plus technical sacrifice vocabulary: עולה, מנחה, חטאת, אשם, שלמים.
- ‎**כפר — make atonement/expiation** in ritual sense.
- ‎**אשה ריח ניחח — food-offering, pleasing aroma**.
- ‎**אש יצאה מלפני יהוה — fire came out from before YHWH**: Lev 9:24; 10:2; Num 16:35. The formula turns divine fire into a sanctuary boundary-enforcement mechanism.
- ‎**כל בשר — all flesh** (Gen 6–9; Num 16:22; 27:16).
- ‎**מקנה / רכוש** in inventory-style possession notices.
- heavy **repetition with only one variable changed**, especially tribal lists, offerings, dimensions, census totals, and ritual procedures.

### Post-Torah P marker clusters

**Josh 13–21** contains the bureaucratic ecology P predicts: inheritance, boundaries, lots, Eleazar + Joshua, tribal heads, Shiloh/Tent of Meeting, cities of refuge, Levitical towns, exact lists and counts (Josh 14:1–5; 17:4; 18:1–10; 19:51; 20; 21).

**Josh 22** is even stronger. It combines technical **עדה**, **נשיא**, the P-favored death verb **גוע** (22:20), **Phinehas son of Eleazar** (22:13, 30–32), corporate cultic guilt, Numbers precedents (Peor and Achan: 22:17, 20), and repetitive juridical argument. Scholarly work has repeatedly recognized Priestly editing or Priestly material here.

**1 Kgs 6–8** has Tabernacle/Temple assimilation, sacred measurements, vessels, cherubim, "most holy place," priests, Tent of Meeting, and glory-cloud imagery (especially 8:10–11 paralleling Exod 40:34–35). Textual criticism indicates multiple quasi-Priestly expansions in the MT tradition, so these may be later P-school additions rather than the Hezekian P himself.

**2 Kgs 10:18–28** contains ritual-separation and sanctuary-defilement procedures with demonstrated affinities to both Priestly and Deuteronomistic traditions; the passage is a candidate for P-school influence but not a secure P fragment.

**2 Kgs 11–12** is newly interesting under the stipulated Hezekian date: Jehoiada, temple personnel, sacred objects, dedicated silver, valuation/census-style payments, guilt- and sin-offering money (12:4, 16), and a temple-restoration ideology. Recent redaction-historical work has argued that aspects of the Joash coronation reflect Hezekian temple priorities. This is not enough to assign it to P, but it should be on the candidate list.

## 6. War

P's wars are often triggered by **threats to the holy community or violations that contaminate Israel corporately**. Midian is the clearest case: the war of Num 31 responds to the Peor crisis of Num 25 and is followed by purification of soldiers, captives, metals, and booty.

Korah's rebellion (Num 16) is almost a civil-war analogue: unauthorized cultic challenge becomes a lethal holiness crisis.

In Josh 22 the western tribes prepare for war not for territory but because they think the Transjordanian tribes have constructed an illegitimate altar that could make YHWH wrathful against **the whole congregation** (Josh 22:12, 16–20). That is quintessential P logic: war is contemplated as quarantine against corporate cultic contamination.

## 7. Judgements

P approves of:
- classification;
- boundaries;
- beautiful sacred materials;
- gold, silver, bronze, fine linen, blue/purple/scarlet yarn (Exod 25–31; 35–40);
- symmetry and measured space;
- repeated completion formulae;
- genealogical legitimacy;
- sacred time;
- priestly dress;
- order in camp and sanctuary;
- exact fulfillment of divine instructions.

P disapproves intensely of:
- unauthorized sacred access (Lev 10; Num 16);
- corpse/genital/skin/food impurity when unmanaged (Lev 11–15; Num 19);
- mixing sacred and profane;
- sexual-cultic contamination (Num 25);
- challenges to Aaronid status;
- disorder.

The aesthetic point matters: P is not simply "legal." P likes **magnificence under control**. The Tabernacle chapters are ecstatic bureaucracy.

## 8. Priesthood

P is unmistakably **Aaronid**, specifically promoting Aaron → Eleazar → Phinehas.

Aaron is consecrated with elaborate vestments and rituals (Exod 28–29; Lev 8). His sons alone approach the altar; unauthorized sons die (Lev 10). The Levites are subordinate assistants (Num 3–4; 8; 18). Phinehas receives a "covenant of perpetual priesthood" (Num 25:13).

The Joshua continuation preserves precisely this succession:
- Eleazar co-administers the land with Joshua (Josh 14:1; 17:4; 19:51; 21:1);
- Phinehas leads the cultic investigation in Josh 22:13–32.

This is one of the strongest arguments that a Priestly narrative once **continued naturally into Joshua**.

## 9. Kings

P is strikingly **not monarch-centered**. The Pentateuchal P system can function without a king: the enduring institutions are congregation, priesthood, sanctuary, tribal leaders, genealogies, and law.

If P is Hezekian, this is politically interesting. A royal patron can support a document whose imagined constitution places sacred legitimacy in an Aaronid priesthood rather than in the king. Hezekiah may be favorable as a reforming sponsor without needing to appear inside P's own primordial history.

The closest post-Torah royal material to P is therefore not a royal biography but **Temple narratives**:
- Solomon's building/dedication (1 Kgs 6–8);
- Jehoiada/Joash and temple repair (2 Kgs 11–12);
- possibly later layers touching Hezekiah's reform world, though 2 Kgs 18–20 itself does not exhibit a dense P lexical fingerprint.

## 10. Dudetheyreontome

**Best social-location estimate:** Jerusalem temple priestly-scribal elite, Aaronid, institutionally close to the royal administration, highly literate and archivally minded.

**Point estimate under the stipulated model:** Jerusalem, c. **720–690 BCE ± 35 years**, with a center of gravity in Hezekiah's reign. Age approximately **35–60 ± large error**. Sex probably male but not demonstrable.

**Named candidate:** the most famous proposal is an Aaronid high-priestly figure in Hezekiah's environment; **Azariah** or a comparable temple chief is possible, but direct identification is weak. A more defensible formulation is "a senior Aaronid priestly scribe in the Hezekian temple bureaucracy."

**People likely known personally:** senior priests, royal scribes, treasury personnel, artisans, and officials involved in centralization or temple administration. If P helped conceptualize a reform-era sanctuary, the institutional world behind 2 Kgs 18 and later Chronicles traditions is relevant even where the narratives themselves are not P.

### Post-Torah source map for P

**A:** Josh 13–22, especially Josh 14:1–5; 17:4; 18:1–10; 19:51; 20–22.  
**A+:** Josh 22:9–34, where vocabulary and institutional logic converge exceptionally strongly.  
**B:** portions of 1 Kgs 6–8 with clear Priestly/Tabernacle assimilation, while recognizing likely later quasi-Priestly supplementation.  
**C:** 2 Kgs 11–12; 2 Kgs 10:18–28.  
**Low:** 2 Kgs 18–20 itself. Hezekiah is historically compatible with the stipulated P date, but the prose is not strongly P.

# D

## 1. Places

D's geography is ideological. The land is a **gift conditional on covenant obedience** (Deut 4; 6–8; 11). The crucial spatial opposition is between the many local high places and **"the place that YHWH will choose"** for his name (Deut 12:5, 11, 14, 18, 21, 26; 14:23–25; 16:2, 6, 11, 15–16).

In the Deuteronomistic History this unnamed place becomes **Jerusalem**. The north is not bad because it is northern; it is bad when its kings institutionalize rival cult at Bethel and Dan (1 Kgs 12:25–33; 13; 14:16; 15:30 etc.). Judah is judged by fidelity to Jerusalem and the Davidic standard.

D therefore reads geography as **theology in space**:
- Canaan must be purged of rival cult (Deut 7; 12);
- high places are a recurring defect (1 Kgs 3:2–3; 15:14; 22:44; 2 Kgs 12:3; 14:4; 15:4, 35);
- Josiah's reform centralizes and destroys provincial cult sites (2 Kgs 23).

## 2. Names

D foregrounds **Moses**, **Joshua**, prophetic interpreters, David as royal benchmark, Jeroboam son of Nebat as anti-benchmark, and Josiah as reforming culmination.

Moses is the speaker and lawgiver of Deuteronomy. Joshua becomes the obedient successor who is repeatedly commanded to be strong and keep Torah (Deut 31; Josh 1). David becomes the standard by which Judahite kings are measured (1 Kgs 3:14; 9:4; 11:4–6; 14:8; 15:3–5, 11; 2 Kgs 18:3; 22:2). Jeroboam becomes the recurrent formula "who caused Israel to sin" (1 Kgs 14:16; 15:30, 34; 16:19, 26; 22:53; 2 Kgs 3:3; 10:29, 31; 13:2, 6, 11; etc.).

D is often sympathetic to **Levites as a vulnerable landless group** (Deut 12:12, 18–19; 14:27–29; 16:11, 14; 18:1–8; 26:11–13), a profile distinct from P's strict Aaronid hierarchy.

## 3. Laws

D's legal signature is unmistakable:

- cult centralization (Deut 12);
- destruction of foreign shrines (Deut 7:5; 12:2–3);
- exclusive YHWH allegiance (Deut 6:4–15; 13);
- limits on kingship (Deut 17:14–20);
- prophetic authority (Deut 18:9–22);
- judges and centralized appeal (Deut 16:18–20; 17:8–13);
- warfare and herem (Deut 20);
- social concern for widow, orphan, resident alien, Levite, debtor, laborer (Deut 14:28–29; 15; 24:10–22);
- Passover centralized at the chosen place (Deut 16:1–8).

In Joshua–Kings these laws become criteria for history. The narrative is not neutral annalistic record; it is a **compliance audit of Israel's covenant**.

## 4. Numbers

D likes rhetorically charged round numbers: **forty years** as a generation/wilderness epoch (Deut 2:7; 8:2, 4; 29:4; Josh 5:6; Judg 3:11; 5:31; 8:28; 1 Sam 4:18; 2 Sam 5:4; 1 Kgs 11:42).

The Judges framework repeatedly uses 40/80/20-style periods of oppression and rest (Judg 3:11, 30; 4:3; 5:31; 8:28; 13:1). Kings uses regnal chronology systematically but draws much of its data from annals; the Dtr contribution is the **synchronizing framework** and theological evaluation more than raw numbers.

Unlike P, D's numbers help periodize history rather than describe ritual or architecture.

## 5. Words

Characteristic D/Dtr vocabulary and formulas include:

- ‎**יהוה אלהיך — YHWH your God**, overwhelmingly characteristic of Deuteronomy's address.
- ‎**שמע — hear/obey** as covenantal verb (Deut 4:1; 5:1; 6:4; 9:1; 20:3).
- ‎**שמר / עשה — keep/do** commandments, statutes, ordinances (Deut 4:1–2, 5–6; 5:29; 6:1–3; throughout).
- ‎**מצוה, חק, משפט — commandment, statute, ordinance** in recurring bundles.
- ‎**בכל לבבך ובכל נפשך — with all your heart and all your soul** (Deut 4:29; 6:5; 10:12; 11:13; 13:4; 26:16; 30:2, 6, 10; compare 1 Sam 7:3 and Kings-style total fidelity).
- ‎**לאהבה את יהוה — to love YHWH** (Deut 6:5; 10:12; 11:1, 13, 22; 19:9; 30:6, 16, 20).
- ‎**ללכת בדרכיו — walk in his ways** (Deut 8:6; 10:12; 11:22; 19:9; 26:17; 28:9; 30:16).
- ‎**הישר בעיני יהוה / הרע בעיני יהוה — right/evil in YHWH's eyes**, especially regnal evaluations (1 Kgs 11:6; 14:22; 15:11, 26, 34; 16:25, 30; 22:43, 53; 2 Kgs throughout).
- ‎**הלך בדרך... — walked in the way of...**, especially the way of David versus the way of Jeroboam (1 Kgs 15:26, 34; 16:19, 26; 2 Kgs 13:2 etc.).
- ‎**לא סר ימין ושמאל — did not turn aside right or left** in normative Torah/king evaluation (Deut 5:29; 17:11, 20; 28:14; Josh 1:7; 23:6; 2 Kgs 22:2).
- ‎**המקום אשר יבחר יהוה — the place YHWH will choose** (Deut 12–26).
- ‎**לשום שמו שם — to put his name there** (Deut 12:5, 11, 21; 14:24; 1 Kgs 8:16–20, 29, 44, 48 in Dtr temple theology).
- ‎**ככל התורה אשר צוה משה — according to all the Torah Moses commanded** (Josh 1:7–8; 8:31–35; 23:6; 2 Kgs 14:6; 18:6; 21:8; 23:25).
- ‎**עד היום הזה — to this day**, common in the Former Prophets but not uniquely D; often used to close etiologies or mark source incorporation.
- ‎**עזב את יהוה — abandon YHWH** (Judg 2:12–13; 10:6; 1 Sam 12:10; Kings).
- ‎**ויעבדו הבעלים והעשתרות — served the Baals and Astartes** (Judg 2:11–13; 3:7; 10:6; 1 Sam 7:3–4; 12:10).
- ‎**חרה אף יהוה — YHWH's anger burned** (Deut 6:15; 7:4; 11:17; 29:26; Judg 2:14, 20; 3:8; 10:7; 2 Kgs 13:3; 23:26).
- ‎**נתן ביד — give into the hand of** enemies, central to Judges' cycle (Judg 2:14; 3:8; 4:2; 6:1; 10:7).
- ‎**וישב / השיב ... אל יהוה — return to YHWH**, repentance language.

Modern computational work is relevant here: recent word-frequency/stylometric studies can distinguish P from D/DtrH with high accuracy and find D and DtrH much closer to one another than either is to P, independently reinforcing the traditional observation that Dtr prose has a strong and reproducible lexical signature.

## 6. War

D's war theology is exceptionally systematic:

**obedience → YHWH fights for Israel → victory/rest; apostasy → YHWH gives Israel into enemy hands → oppression/exile.**

This is programmatic in Deut 7; 20; 28 and becomes the cycle of Judg 2:11–23. Joshua's conquest is successful because Joshua obeys Torah (Josh 1; 6–12); Achan's violation produces defeat (Josh 7); Judges' apostasy causes repeated foreign domination; Kings culminates in Assyrian and Babylonian conquest because of cumulative covenant violation (2 Kgs 17; 21; 23–25).

D moralizes military history more relentlessly than J, E, or P.

## 7. Judgements

D approves:
- exclusive YHWH worship;
- covenant loyalty;
- listening/obeying;
- destruction of idols/high places;
- justice;
- care for marginalized people;
- prophetic truth;
- kings subordinate to Torah;
- centralized worship;
- remembering the exodus.

D disapproves:
- "other gods";
- high places;
- Asherah/Baal;
- child sacrifice;
- divination;
- royal arrogance;
- intermarriage insofar as it leads to apostasy;
- forgetting YHWH;
- Jeroboam's cult.

D's great historical moral is **memory**. Israel's sin is repeatedly described as forgetting what YHWH did and behaving as though covenant history were irrelevant (Deut 6:10–15; 8; Judg 2:10–13).

## 8. Priesthood

D's priesthood is more **Levitical/Mushite-compatible** than P's Aaronid hierarchy. Deuteronomy repeatedly speaks of **"the Levitical priests"** or "priests, the Levites" (Deut 17:9, 18; 18:1; 21:5; 24:8; 27:9; 31:9), and its Levite legislation does not reproduce P's sharp Aaronid/Levite stratification.

Moses is supreme legal authority; prophets stand as authorized successors in revelatory function (Deut 18:15–22). This is one reason a Josianic Dtr can find Samuel and prophetic anti-cult stories so useful.

Josiah's reform (2 Kgs 23) attacks decentralized cult while dealing with provincial priests in a manner that fits D's centralizing agenda better than P's genealogical Aaronid taxonomy.

## 9. Kings

D is neither simply pro- nor anti-monarchy. It wants a **Torah-subordinate king** (Deut 17:14–20).

David becomes the positive norm, though Kings abstracts him into a theological benchmark more than the messy person in 2 Samuel. Jeroboam becomes the northern negative norm. Hezekiah and Josiah receive extraordinary praise (2 Kgs 18:3–7; 22:2; 23:25).

Under Cross's double-redaction model, **Dtr1** culminates naturally in Josiah: the history explains why his reform is the proper resolution of Israel's long cultic disorder. A later **Dtr2** then has to explain why Jerusalem nevertheless falls.

## 10. Dudetheyreontome

**Best social-location estimate:** Jerusalem royal-scribal/temple-adjacent reform circle under Josiah, ideologically allied with centralization and prophetic Torah.

**Point estimate:** Jerusalem, c. **630–609 BCE ± 20 years** for Dtr1. Adult elite male is probable but not certain; age perhaps **30–55 ± large error**.

**Named candidates:** **Shaphan the scribe** and his family circle are the strongest narrative fit, because Shaphan is directly involved in the discovery/readout of the Torah scroll and Josiah's reform (2 Kgs 22:3–14). **Huldah** is a crucial allied prophet whose oracle authorizes the reform (2 Kgs 22:14–20). Jeremiah and his scribal environment are linguistically/theologically close to Deuteronomistic language, though identification of Dtr with Jeremiah is not necessary.

### Post-Torah source map for D

**A:** Josh 1; 8:30–35; 23; Judg 2:6–3:6 and recurring Judges framework; 1 Sam 7; 8; 12 in substantial parts; Kings regnal evaluations and framework; 1 Kgs 8 Dtr prayer layers; 1 Kgs 11–12 editorial framing; 2 Kgs 17; 21; 22–23.  
**B:** Josh 24 may contain Dtr reworking of older E; Samuel's anti-monarchy material; prophetic fulfillment notices throughout Kings.  
**Dtr2/exilic rather than Dtr1:** 2 Kgs 24–25 and passages making destruction effectively irreversible.

# R

## 1. Places

R is not best identified by a favorite city but by **bridges between incompatible geographies**. A redactor explains why one tradition knows Sinai and another Horeb, why one patriarchal site has multiple names, why one cultic center is legitimate in one source but condemned in another, and why parallel routes or conquest traditions can coexist.

For **RJE after 722 BCE**, the sociogeographic problem is itself the agenda: northern traditions have arrived in Judah and must be preserved within a southern literary corpus. The resulting JE can keep Bethel, Shechem, Shiloh, Ephraim, and Joseph traditions while also preserving Judah, Hebron, and Davidic trajectories.

A later final R that combines JE, P, and D must reconcile **portable sanctuary, local altars, and centralized Jerusalem** without fully erasing any of them.

## 2. Names

R characteristically keeps **doublets and competing reputations** rather than eliminating one source's heroes.

Thus:
- Abraham can be both J's directly encountering patriarch and E's prophet/dream-linked figure.
- Jacob/Israel can alternate.
- Moses can coexist with Aaron in ways that preserve both E's tensions and P's elevation of Aaron.
- Joshua can be Moses' prophetic/military servant and P's co-administrator with Eleazar.
- Caleb traditions can coexist with Joshua-centered traditions.
- David can remain both ideal royal benchmark and catastrophically compromised human king.

A conservative redactor's distinctive "favorite person" may therefore be **the corpus itself**.

## 3. Laws

R's legal problem is harmonization. The Pentateuch preserves:
- local altar permission (Exod 20:24);
- centralized worship (Deut 12);
- a sanctuary system that assumes Aaronid administration (Lev/Num).

Rather than deleting contradictions, R often places them in a narrative chronology that makes them appear sequential or situational.

R may add bridging notices, genealogical links, resumptive repetitions, or contextualizing clauses that make adjacent legal systems readable as one Torah.

## 4. Numbers

R often inherits incompatible numbers and leaves them visible. The Flood famously preserves J-like 40-day/seven-day rhythms alongside P's exact calendrical chronology (Gen 7–8). Different census, age, duration, and itinerary systems remain side by side.

A redactor can also produce **sum totals, resumptions, and synchronizations** when integrating lists.

Post-Torah, the Dtr compiler does something analogous with regnal chronologies and older stories, but that should not automatically be equated with Pentateuchal RJE.

## 5. Words

There is no safe "R lexicon" comparable with P because redactors often imitate the source they are joining. Better indicators are **editorial syntax and seam behavior**:

- resumptive repetition after an insertion;
- mixed divine names within a short unit where source boundaries otherwise explain them;
- genealogical or chronological bridge clauses;
- explanatory glosses ("that is...", "which is...", place-name identifications);
- source-preserving contradictions softened by a connective clause;
- short harmonizing statements that presuppose two narratives;
- notices that convert a local story into a pan-Israelite one;
- phrases such as **עד היום הזה — "to this day"** can be editorial but are not uniquely redactional;
- **therefore it is called...** etiological formulas can belong to original sources or to R and must be judged case by case.

Joel Baden's work is methodologically important here: he argues that scholars have often made "the redactor" do too much and that many apparent redactional passages are better explained as parts of the underlying sources. That warning should govern this project. **R should be the explanation of last resort, not the wastebasket for difficult verses.**

## 6. War

R's role in war narratives is usually to make two accounts coexist. A battle may have:
- one tradition emphasizing divine miracle;
- another emphasizing tactical action;
- a Dtr frame explaining victory or defeat in covenantal terms.

The combined Jericho narrative is a useful test case. Josh 5:13–6 contains older vivid material, priestly/cultic elements, and Dtr framing. Instead of assigning every inconsistency to a free-composing author, R may have preserved an earlier conquest story and made minimal connective interventions.

## 7. Judgements

R appears to value **preservation over ideological purity**. The final Torah does not erase the embarrassing Aaron of Exod 32 even though P exalts Aaron elsewhere. It does not erase J's anthropomorphic YHWH even though P is more transcendent. It preserves multiple creation stories, wife-sister stories, revelation accounts, and law codes.

That tells us something important about the editor's values: contradiction was apparently a lower cost than **loss of authoritative ancestral tradition**.

R's aesthetic is therefore polyphonic. The final narrative can become richer than any single source because the seams create new ironies and associations.

## 8. Priesthood

R must negotiate **Mushite/prophetic, Levite, and Aaronid** claims.

RJE after 722 likely combines a southern J that is not Aaronid-centric with a northern E suspicious of Aaron. The later integration of P introduces a powerful Aaronid constitution. Deuteronomy contributes its "Levitical priests" formulation.

The final corpus does not resolve these systems cleanly. That failure is evidence of preservation, not necessarily incompetence.

## 9. Kings

RJE's political setting after 722 plausibly encourages a **pan-Israelite synthesis under Judahite survival**. Northern ancestral traditions are preserved inside a corpus that can culminate in David.

The final redaction after D/P integration must also reconcile anti-monarchical warnings (1 Sam 8; 12), divine election of David (2 Sam 7), priest-centered constitutions, and king-centered history.

In Kings, the Deuteronomistic compiler is a different but analogous editorial personality: he preserves prophetic tales, court annals, temple records, royal legends, and northern stories while wrapping them in a theological framework.

## 10. Dudetheyreontome

For **RJE**, the best social scenario is a Judahite scribe or scribal team working after 722 BCE with newly available northern textual/traditional material.

**Point estimate:** Jerusalem/Judah, c. **715 BCE ± 25 years** for RJE under the stipulated model. Adult scribal elite; sex/age essentially unknown, perhaps **30–60 ± very large error**.

The most plausible historical context is the reign of **Hezekiah**, when refugees and northern traditions could be incorporated into Judahite institutions. The compiler may have had access to both royal and temple scribal networks.

For the **final Pentateuchal R**, a later date and different person/team are likely. It is better to distinguish RJE from the editor who ultimately joins JE, P, and D rather than imagining one immortal redactor.

### Proposed large-scale parallel architecture

If the three early narrative sources were **complete histories with archaic and later halves**, the most productive reconstruction is not strict verse-by-verse chiasm but a recurrence of **institutional and character-types**:

| Archaic story | Later counterpart | Source logic |
|---|---|---|
| J: Cain/Abel; Jacob/Esau; Joseph/brothers | Amnon/Absalom/Solomon; royal sibling conflict | family rivalry becomes dynastic rivalry |
| J: sexual deception and threatened lineage | Bathsheba, Tamar, Absalom, succession crisis | sexuality repeatedly redirects inheritance |
| J: Judah learns responsibility | David/Judah becomes national center | southern lineage reaches monarchy |
| E: Abraham and Abimelech | Abimelech at Shechem | same royal name used to contrast just and disastrous kingship |
| E: Jacob at Bethel/Shechem | Joshua covenant at Shechem; Jeroboam at Bethel | northern holy geography becomes national political problem |
| E: Moses versus Aaron/calf | Samuel/Ahijah/man of God versus royal cult | prophetic authority confronts institutional cult |
| E: dreams reveal divine intent | Gideon dream; Samuel night revelation; later prophetic sign-stories | mediated revelation persists |
| P: creation ordered into kinds/boundaries | land ordered into tribes/boundaries | cosmic taxonomy becomes national cadastral taxonomy |
| P: genealogies and tribal descent | Joshua allotments and Levitical towns | ancestry becomes territorial administration |
| P: Aaron→Eleazar→Phinehas | Eleazar/Phinehas in Joshua | priestly succession completes conquest |
| P: unauthorized altar/fire crisis | Josh 22 altar crisis | cultic boundary violation threatens whole congregation |
| D: Moses' covenant sermon | Joshua/Samuel/royal farewell speeches | covenant interpretation structures epochs |
| D: law before land | Kings judged by law, land finally lost | Deuteronomic conditionality closes the history |

The strongest new prediction generated by this architecture is that **source identity should be sought at narrative correspondences, not only vocabulary**. A later passage that repeats a source's favorite geographical triangle, authority structure, moral problem, and revelation mechanism can be source-related even when a redactor has normalized some of its vocabulary.

## Research conclusions

1. **J has the strongest post-Torah continuation evidence into Samuel.** Friedman's vocabulary table is not trivial: rare expressions repeatedly reappear in Joshua, Judges, 1 Samuel B, 2 Samuel, and 1 Kings 1–2. The Court/Succession History hypothesis becomes considerably stronger when the lexical evidence is combined with J's sexual, familial, deceptive, ironic, and southern-monarchic narrative habits.

2. **E is recoverable more strongly than the conventional "fragmentary E" picture suggests if one allows a continuation into the Former Prophets.** Josh 24, Judg 6–9, 1 Sam 9–10, and 1 Kgs 11:29–14:18 form a coherent northern-prophetic corridor. The single most provocative candidate is **1 Kings 13**.

3. **P almost certainly has a Joshua continuation in this model.** Josh 13–22, and especially Josh 22, is too saturated with P's institutional and lexical world to dismiss as merely "bureaucratic Dtr prose." The combination of גוע, עדה, נשיא, Phinehas, Eleazar, cultic corporate guilt, and Numbers precedents is exceptionally strong.

4. **P in Kings is real as a style/school but harder to identify as the original P author.** 1 Kgs 6–8 contains demonstrable quasi-Priestly expansions; 2 Kgs 10 has mixed Priestly/Deuteronomistic ritual affinities; 2 Kgs 11–12 is a serious Hezekian-era candidate for priestly editorial interest. The Hezekiah narrative itself, however, is not lexically P-rich.

5. **D/Dtr is easiest to identify because its rhetoric functions as the visible editorial skeleton of Joshua–Kings.** It wraps older material in a covenantal causal model and makes Jerusalem, Torah, and king-evaluation the controlling coordinates.

6. **R should be invoked conservatively.** The better hypothesis is usually that redactors preserved and minimally connected substantial older sources. The continued visibility of contradiction is positive evidence that the compilers were reluctant to rewrite inherited documents wholesale.
