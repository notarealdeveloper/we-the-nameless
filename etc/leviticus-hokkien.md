Hokkien gives us a better game than Mandarin, but the spelling shifts: the closest productive first-half family is ia / iá / iâ / iā, while the second half is especially ue / ué / uè / uê / uē and ui / uì / uî / uī. I checked these against Taiwan's Ministry of Education Taiwanese dictionary rather than just projecting Mandarin readings onto Hokkien. For example, it actually gives iâ 爺/椰, iā 夜/也, uî 為/圍/違, uì 畏, uī 胃/位/為, and uē 話/衛.

The Hokkien jackpots

These are considerably better than I expected:

Pair	Hokkien	Literal reading	Leviticus jackpot

野違	iá-uî	wilderness/wild + transgression	Scapegoat carries transgressions into wilderness — Lev 16:21–22
爺違	iâ-uî	Lord + transgression/disobedience	Basically half of Leviticus
爺畏	iâ-uì	Lord + fear	“Fear YHWH” territory
野穢	iá-uè	wild/animal + impurity	Lev 11 animal purity laws
爺穢	iâ-uè	Lord + impurity	Sanctuary/priestly impurity
掖衛	iā-uē	sprinkle + protect/guard	Lev 14 purification sprinkling
野胃	iá-uī	animal + stomach	Dietary laws
野危	iá-uî	wilderness + danger	wilderness / wild beasts
野椏	iá-ue	wild + branch	Booths vegetation, Lev 23
爺煨	iâ-ue	Lord + slow-cook/burn	Sacrificial fire


Two are exceptional.

野違 iá-uî = “wilderness + transgression.” The dictionary independently has 野 iá (“wild”; cf. 野狼 “wild wolf”) and 違 among its uî entries.  That is almost a two-character summary of Leviticus 16:21–22: transgressions → goat → wilderness.

And 爺 iâ is even more ridiculous for this project. The Taiwanese MOE definition explicitly says it is an honorific that can be used for a deity or an honored/powerful person.  So 爺違 iâ-uî genuinely gives us something close to the target sound while reading approximately “Lord/deity + transgress/disobey.”

Hokkien therefore beat Mandarin pretty decisively for semantic Yahweh-punning.

Next I'd try Hakka. Its YA/WEI neighborhood should be phonologically different enough that we aren't just recycling these same characters.
