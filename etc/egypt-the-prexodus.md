## How I learned to stop worrying and hate Egypt

### Q

Preparing to start the Joseph section, so for the next 11 (Gen) + 15 (Exo) chapters, we the Authors of the book are Daniel Day Lewis-ly stuck in Egypt.

We need to learn just enough Egyptian to get mad at how complex it is, so that by the time we hit Song of the Sea, which the book renders in Proto-Sinaitic, we've essentially learned enough of the Egyptian system (not language, just the writing system) and its various quirks and complexities that we can knowledgeably write scathing takedowns of the unnecessary complexity to put in the Genesis Apocrypha and Exodus Apocrypha.

So, between Joseph being asked to "Lie with me" and Song of the Sea, we basically need to co-opt the Egyptian writing system like the Japanese did to Chinese Hanzi, and use that system to make a version of writing in a chang pong mixture of English and simple Hebrew, which itself becomes too complex, before we finally escape Egypt and refactor the system to just its basic essentials, which unsurprisingly will turn out to be Proto Sinaitic.

So nonfiction history showing up as a semi fictional narrative within the Egypt portions of Genesis and Exodus in We the Nameless, and playing out in the commentary.

---

### A

Yes. This gives the Egypt stretch an actual formal arc, rather than Egyptian hieroglyphs just being a recurring gag.

The joke becomes: we independently reinvent the history of alphabetic writing because we get increasingly pissed off at Egyptian orthography.

And the notebook page already has exactly the right beginning: The Prexodus: Or, Stuck in Egypt — with somebody trying to learn r, m, crying eye, etc. The Authors start as enthusiastic idiots drawing signs and end up as furious systems engineers.

The arc.

At Genesis 39, the Authors discover Egyptian writing because Joseph is in Egypt. Initially it's delightful. We learn the genuinely fun fundamentals alongside the reader:

uniliterals → biliterals → triliterals → phonetic complements → determinatives → quadrats → honorific transposition → defective/full spellings → multiple spellings of the same word.

Crucially, we don't teach much Egyptian grammar. We're learning how Egyptian represents language, because we're about to steal the machinery.

So around Potiphar's wife, somebody realizes:

> Wait. These aren't just little pictures of things.

You can spell with them.

That's the infection point.

Then comes the dangerous realization:

> Wait. If I don't know the Egyptian word for something, I can just use an Egyptian sign for its sound and write something else.

Now we're basically discovering the rebus principle ourselves.

Then we Japanese the shit out of it

We don't actually speak enough Egyptian to compose everything we want, so the Authors begin abusing the system to write Hebrew and English.

An Egyptian sign can consequently acquire several jobs:

semantic: the picture means what it depicts;

Egyptian logographic: the sign represents its Egyptian word;

Egyptian phonetic: it supplies one/two/three Egyptian consonants;

Hebrew rebus: we read the picture's Hebrew name and steal its initial/syllable;

English rebus: same trick with English;

determinative: pronounce nothing; merely classify the preceding word;

commentarial determinative: pronounce nothing but make a dirty joke;

phonetic complement: repeat information we've already written because apparently we hate ourselves.

And instead of recognizing that this is becoming insane, the Authors initially regard each additional convention as a clever optimization.

That lets the commentary gradually become infected.

Early Genesis 39:

> Oh neat! 𓂋 is r. That's easy.

Later Genesis:

> We have written house with the Egyptian house logogram, followed by Hebrew bayit phonetic complements, except that the final sign is being read acrophonically in English.

Early Exodus:

> For clarity we have added two redundant phonetic complements and a determinative.

A few chapters later:

> FOR CLARITY?!

And the historical joke underneath is surprisingly legitimate

This is what makes the whole bit much better than arbitrary parody.

Proto-Sinaitic really does appear to have arisen through people familiar with Egyptian signs taking a small subset and using them acrophonically for a Semitic language. The famous conceptual example is Egyptian:

𓉐 — house

Egyptian value/concept: pr, "house."

A Semitic speaker recognizes the picture as bayt/bēt, "house," and says, effectively:

> Fuck pr. I'm calling this bēt, and therefore it can stand for /b/.

Eventually:

𓉐 → Proto-Sinaitic bēt → Phoenician 𐤁 → Hebrew ב → Greek Β → Latin B

So our fictional Authors aren't merely making a goofy cipher. They're stumbling toward essentially the same conceptual hack that produced the alphabet.

That gives Song of the Sea a fantastic payoff.

Exodus becomes progressive technical debt

By Exodus 1 we've accumulated an absolutely appalling mixed writing system.

It should be usable. That's important. We shouldn't merely make random hieroglyph soup. We should establish rules and obey them.

Indeed, I'd make it increasingly overengineered but internally consistent.

So the reader can eventually encounter something and think:

> Okay, bird = Egyptian phonetic value here, but house is being read as Hebrew bayit, the eye is a semantic determinative, and those two signs merely disambiguate the consonants—

—and then experience the exact same rage as the Authors.

Even better, periodically somebody proposes a simplification and accidentally makes things worse.

> Proposal 14: Every Hebrew word shall receive an Egyptian semantic determinative so that readers cannot confuse homophones.

Approved.

Three chapters later:

> Proposal 23: Determinatives may themselves be read phonetically when enclosed by—

NO.

By the plagues, the writing system should look like a software project maintained by twelve people who have never rejected a pull request.

Then: the Exodus

The literary metaphor becomes wonderfully literal.

We're not merely escaping Egypt.

We're escaping Egyptian writing.

At the sea, somebody finally snaps.

Take all the signs we've been using phonetically.

Throw away:

the logograms,
the biliterals,
the triliterals,
the phonetic complements,
the classifiers,
the alternate readings,
the honorific transpositions,
the English readings,
the mixed Hebrew/Egyptian conventions—

and ask:

> What if one sign just meant one consonant?

Holy shit.

The entire grotesque Prexodus system collapses into ~two dozen signs.

And the Song of the Sea appears in Proto-Sinaitic.

Not because the book arbitrarily changes fonts.

The reader has just watched us derive it.

That's the killer part.

The Song itself becomes the first major text written after our orthographic liberation:

Egyptian signs → Semitic names → acrophony → consonantal alphabet.

So "escaping Egypt" simultaneously happens on four levels: Israel leaves Egypt; the Authors finally leave their Egypt section; the commentary escapes Egyptian scribal practice; and the writing system escapes Egyptian complexity into the alphabet.

Then we can immediately become unbearably smug about our elegant new invention.

Until, several centuries later, somebody invents niqqud and cantillation.

And the cycle begins again.
