## A

Yes. This reformulation produces a stronger and more coherent hypothesis than looking for isolated Hebrew puns.

The key move is:

> **Don't ask only, “What Hebrew word might lie behind Jesus' Greek?” Ask, “What Tanak scene is Mark reenacting, and what conclusion would someone who knows that scene immediately draw from the other details Mark has planted?”**

When I do that across Mark, a striking pattern appears. In many of the places where Jesus seems exasperated, the characters are not merely missing a metaphor. They are standing inside a recognizable **Moses / Exodus / Elijah-Elisha / Isaiah / Psalms / prophetic-judgment scene** and failing to recognize what role Jesus is playing. Mark repeatedly supplies enough details for a scripturally saturated reader to say:

> “Oh. I know this story.”

The characters frequently don't.

That makes Jesus' “Do you still not understand?” considerably less arbitrary.

I would distinguish three confidence levels below:

**A = strong/explicit intertext**, where Mark quotes or reproduces several distinctive details; **B = probable**, supported by multiple correspondences and scholarship; **C = suggestive**, worth noticing but too weak to carry the argument.

The really interesting result is that Mark's **highest-density misunderstanding section, roughly Mark 4–10, is also his highest-density Tanak reenactment section**.

# Now your universalization idea

This is where I think the hypothesis becomes genuinely interesting rather than merely clever.

Mark appears to be written for Greek-speaking readers who need certain Jewish practices explained and Semitic words translated. Mark 7 famously pauses to explain purification customs; throughout the Gospel Mark glosses Semitic expressions such as _Talitha koum_, _Ephphatha_, _Abba_, and Jesus' final cry. That makes a purely Palestinian Hebrew-reading audience unlikely. ([Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.andrews.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2837&context=dissertations&utm_source=chatgpt.com "A Narrative-Intertextual Study of Mark 7:1-23"))

Yet Mark's narrative is **saturated with Israel's scriptures**.

So Mark has a real literary problem:

> How do you universalize a story whose meaning depends heavily on knowing another people's textual memory?

His answer seems to be:

### reenact it.

Don't just quote Psalm 107.

**Put Jesus in the boat.**

Don't just explain Psalm 23/Numbers 27/Ezekiel 34.

**Put the sheep on green grass and feed them.**

Don't just teach Isaiah 35.

**Open deaf ears and blind eyes.**

Don't merely explain Isaiah 5.

**Tell the vineyard again.**

Don't merely cite Zechariah.

**Make the shepherd's followers scatter.**

Don't merely quote Psalm 22.

**Stage the crucifixion inside it.**

That allows a Greek-speaking outsider to **experience the Tanak as narrative**, even if much Hebrew lexical richness has disappeared.

# Which modifies your proposed joke in one important way

I would not quite formulate Mark's joke as:

> “This poor Hebrew-speaking guy went around explaining Tanak to Gentiles who didn't know Hebrew, lol.”

Because inside Mark, the disciples are Jews too.

Their failure cannot simply be explained by Greek translation.

And religious authorities sometimes understand scriptural allusions perfectly.

Instead, I think the more defensible reconstruction is:

> **Jesus tries to make Israel's scriptures happen again in front of everybody.**
> 
> He expects people who know those scriptures to recognize the patterns.
> 
> They frequently don't—not because they haven't memorized verses, but because they don't recognize a text once it has become an event.
> 
> Outsiders sometimes recognize the logic better than insiders.
> 
> Mark then retells this deeply Jewish semantic world in Greek for a wider audience, repeatedly confronting the same danger: the words can cross languages while the network of associations does not.

That is much better.

# Mark might be constructing a Tanak-recognition game

The rules seem to be:

1. I will give you a few distinctive features of an old story.
    
2. I will not always tell you the story's name.
    
3. Recognize it.
    
4. Once you recognize it, import the relevant expectations.
    
5. Notice where Jesus repeats, exceeds, reverses, or completes the old pattern.
    
6. Don't do what the disciples do and treat each detail independently.
    

That is very close to how allusion-heavy humor works.

A joke doesn't explain the reference.

Explaining it destroys the joke.

The pleasure comes from:

> **Ohhhhh. It's THAT.**

Mark's favorite characters repeatedly fail to experience the **ohhhhh**.

Hence:

> “Do you still not understand?”

---

# And this gives “parable” a much broader meaning

Mark's Jesus does not only tell parables.

He **does** them.

The feeding is a parable.

The fig tree is a parable.

The deaf man is a parable about the disciples.

The two-stage blind healing is almost certainly a parable about Peter.

The Temple action is a Jeremiah reenactment.

The anointing is an enacted definition of Messiah.

The crucifixion is Psalm 22 performed.

That explains why Mark can feel weirdly elliptical.

He doesn't tell you:

> “The blind man's two-stage recovery symbolizes Peter's partial insight.”

He puts them beside each other.

The reader has to **understand**.

---

# This also explains why Mark's characters are constantly asking the wrong question

They ask:

> Who is this?

when Psalm 107 just answered it.

They ask:

> Where can anybody get enough bread?

when 2 Kings 4 just answered it.

They say:

> We only have one loaf.

after two wilderness bread miracles.

Peter says:

> Messiah!

but rejects the story Jesus attaches to Messiah.

James and John say:

> glory seats!

after repeated suffering predictions.

Peter says:

> I won't scatter.

immediately after Jesus quotes a text saying sheep scatter.

Bystanders say:

> Elijah!

while Jesus is quoting Psalm 22.

These aren't random instances of stupidity.

They are variations on:

> **correct stimulus, wrong source story.**

---

# The most interesting possible overall reading

I think Mark may be doing **three things simultaneously**.

First, he is presenting Jesus as the place where Israel's old stories converge:

**Moses**  
**Joshua**  
**Elijah**  
**Elisha**  
**David**  
**shepherd**  
**prophet**  
**righteous sufferer**  
**Son of Man**  
and sometimes actions scripturally associated with **YHWH**.

Second, he dramatizes the difficulty of recognizing a pattern when it reappears in a transformed context.

Characters who expect:

> Moses to look like Moses

or

> Messiah to look like their Messiah schema

miss it.

Third, Mark himself faces exactly the same challenge in transferring this semantic system into Greek-speaking Christianity.

So his Gospel becomes an experiment:

> **Can I make people who didn't grow up inside the Hebrew Bible learn how to see the patterns?**

And perhaps the answer is:

> Yes—but I'm going to make them work for it.

---

# That would make Mark's apparent roughness part of the point

Mark has traditionally been regarded as comparatively crude Greek, terse, repetitive, awkward.

But as literature he has a sophisticated habit of withholding explanatory joints.

A polished translator might write:

> Jesus fed the crowd, thereby presenting himself as the eschatological shepherd predicted by Ezekiel and reenacting the miraculous feeding of Elisha.

Mark writes:

> sheep without shepherd.

> grass.

> sit.

> five loaves.

> five thousand.

> leftovers.

> boat.

> Jesus walks on sea.

> “They didn't understand the loaves.”

That's better literature.

It's also more Hebrew-Bible-like literature.

It makes you **do the connection**.

---

# My revised model of why Jesus is so frustrated

Previously I said:

> the disciples treat signs as things instead of signs.

I would now sharpen that to:

> **They keep treating Tanak reenactments as isolated contemporary events.**

Jesus appears to assume:

> If I reproduce enough of the pattern, you'll identify the source story and therefore understand what I'm showing you.

They don't.

So:

**bread** stays bread.

**sea** stays sea.

**blindness** stays ophthalmology.

**shepherd** stays agricultural vocabulary.

**anointing** stays perfume.

**cup** stays drinkware.

**Messiah** stays a title.

Whereas Jesus/Mark wants:

**bread → Moses + Elisha + divine provision**

**sea → Exodus + Psalms + YHWH**

**blindness → Isaiah + failed understanding**

**shepherd → Moses succession + Ezekiel + YHWH**

**anointing → king/Messiah + burial**

**cup → prophetic fate**

**Messiah → entire network of narratives Mark has been assembling**

That's the difference between knowing words and knowing a literature.

---

# And then the final joke is brutal

At the cross, Mark finally gives everyone a **literal quotation from the Hebrew Bible in Semitic speech**.

Jesus:

> **Eloi Eloi lama sabachthani.**

It's Psalm 22.

And someone says:

> **Oh, Elijah!**

If your hypothesis needed a punch line, that's awfully close.

For fourteen chapters people have been:

> hearing the clue  
> retrieving the wrong framework  
> misunderstanding Jesus.

At the climax, Mark turns the mechanism into an actual phonological mistake.

> Scripture is being spoken.

> They hear the sound.

> They retrieve the wrong biblical character.

> **They don't get it.**

And Mark turns to the Greek reader and translates it for them.

That does **not** prove that Mark consciously wrote a satire about translation.

But after looking across the whole book this way, I think something slightly broader is quite defensible:

> **Mark is intensely interested in what gets lost between text and interpretation, between sign and referent, between remembered Scripture and lived event, and—occasionally quite explicitly—between Semitic speech and Greek reception.**

Your proposed “universalizing the Tanak” angle fits that remarkably well.

My strongest formulation would therefore be:

> **Mark is trying to universalize Israel's story without flattening it.**
> 
> His solution is to turn the Tanak into a sequence of scenes and signs rather than stop constantly to explain it.
> 
> The recurrent misunderstanding characters dramatize the danger: you can hear every word, witness every reenactment, remember every fact, and still fail to recognize the story.
> 
> The successful characters demonstrate the alternative: hear, connect, infer, respond.
> 
> And Mark's reader is continuously being tested to see which kind of character they resemble.

That, I think, explains **both** why Mark is so allusive and why his Jesus is so exasperated. The book is essentially saying:

> **How many fucking clues do I have to give you before you realize which story this is?**

And, surprisingly often, once you put the surrounding Tanak story back into the scene, Jesus' frustration stops looking mysterious.
