Yes. I dug into this more carefully, and there is an important correction to the way I framed the experiment earlier:

Tur-Sinai does not actually give 20–30 equally explicit “here is the Aramaic original, and here is how the Hebrew translator mistranslated it” cases that I can securely recover from the published scholarly discussion. There is a smaller core of unusually explicit cases, surrounded by a much larger mass of Tur-Sinai conjectures involving Aramaic/Syriac cognates, revocalization, resegmentation, and textual repair.

That distinction matters enormously. Tur-Sinai himself really did maintain the strong theory: the poetic Job represented a Hebrew translation of an earlier Aramaic work brought back from Babylonia, and the translator sometimes misunderstood it.  But Gray summarizes the modern reaction pretty well: the theory doesn't adequately explain Job's fluent Hebrew literary artistry and its deeply Canaanite/Ugaritic poetic inheritance. 

So below are 25 of the strongest/most interesting Tur-Sinai readings I could verify, divided honestly into:

T = genuine alleged translation error from Aramaic

A = reading substantially dependent on Aramaic/Syriac

R = repair/resegmentation/revocalization, interesting but not evidence that Job was translated from Aramaic


My two grades are independent:

Local = Does Tur-Sinai probably improve this particular verse?
Aram. = Does it actually constitute evidence for an Aramaic Vorlage?

A strong; B serious; C possible; D weak; F basically no.

The explicit claim at 6:4 is especially useful because a dissertation quotes Tur-Sinai making the mechanism completely explicit: the extant Hebrew is sound as Hebrew text, he argues, but represents a wrong translation of an ancient Aramaic original. The problem is circularity: the lost Aramaic word is reconstructed because Tur-Sinai thinks “arrows” is wrong. 

Job 11:7 is interesting but weaker evidence for translation. The second תמצא occurring with עד naturally permits something like “reach/attain,” and scholars connect this with Aramaic מטא; you don't actually need to posit a lost Aramaic text to explain the Hebrew semantic extension. 

The two best cases are 24:5 and 24:10

At 24:5, Tur-Sinai's proposed error is wonderfully concrete:

hypothetical Aramaic

LTR: ʿārdîn — ערדין = they flee

↓ tiny reading/lexical mistake

LTR: ʿărōdîn — ערודין = wild asses

↓ translator writes ordinary Hebrew word for wild asses

LTR: pərāʾîm — פְּרָאִים

That's exactly the sort of thing an actual translator could do. A modern treatment reports Tur-Sinai's reconstruction explicitly—but rejects it because the ordinary wild-ass metaphor already makes perfectly good sense. 

And 24:10 is substantially better:

LTR: ʿārôm hilləḵû bəlî ləḇûš — עָרוֹם הִלְּכוּ בְּלִי לְבוּשׁ

MT:

> Naked they go, without clothing.



Tur-Sinai reconstructs an Aramaic ʾzl meaning spin, which a translator supposedly mistook for the enormously common Aramaic ʾzl go:

> Naked they spin/weave, without clothing.



Why is that attractive? Because look at the surrounding laborers:

> weavers: naked despite making cloth
harvesters: hungry despite handling grain
wine workers: thirsty despite pressing wine



Suddenly the passage becomes a vicious little three-part irony. A dissertation calls this one of Tur-Sinai's most striking proposals, while Pope's judgment was essentially “brilliant if the translation theory is true; difficult because the theory itself is unproved.” 

That's his best smoking gun, in my assessment.

Job 17:6 is genuinely Tur-Sinai's Aramaic proposal: he regarded תפת as corresponding to Hebrew שפתים/משפתים, “hearth(-stones),” rather than the conventional interpretation related to spitting. But it doesn't yield an obviously superior sentence. 

Job 21:33 is another clean example of his method: Clines records Tur-Sinai deriving מתק from an Aramaic/Syriac “suck/suckle” root and translating along the lines of “the moisture of the river suckles him.” Clines sees no need to abandon the ordinary Hebrew sense. 

And 39:14 illustrates the danger beautifully. “She leaves her eggs on the ground / she neglects them in the dust” makes tighter synonymous parallelism, but the supposed Aramaic lexical item is itself poorly attested, and the resulting two halves become nearly redundant. Clines accordingly rejects it. 

So none of these should be allowed to count as:

> “Look! Evidence it was translated from Aramaic!”



They establish, at most:

> “Reading Job with Aramaic in your head can occasionally produce interesting results.”



That's a very different claim.

There are a couple of really good readings in this pile.

Job 26:13 is genuinely excellent philology

MT:

LTR: bərûḥô šāmayim šiprāh — בְּרוּחוֹ שָׁמַיִם שִׁפְרָה

Conventionally:

> By his breath the heavens were made fair.



Tur-Sinai recognized a cognate of Akkadian saparru, net, in שפרה. Later scholars combined this with a resegmentation of שמים:

LTR: śām yām — שָׂם יָם

> he placed Yamm



yielding approximately:

> By his wind, he put Yamm in his net.



That is much more interesting in context because Job 26:12–13 is a divine combat passage:

> he churns/defeats Yam
he smashes Rahab
he traps Yam
he pierces the fleeing serpent



Suddenly the four cola form a coherent mythological sequence. The Akkadian saparru connection goes back to Tur-Sinai; the full resegmentation was developed further subsequently. 

I give the reading A−.

But as evidence for Aramaic translation?

F.

It has nothing to do with mistranslating Aramaic.

That's exactly why we need two grades.

Job 30:4 is another likely win

MT traditionally has the miserable outcasts eating:

> broom roots for food.



That's botanically and contextually odd; broom roots are famously useful as fuel.

Tur-Sinai connects לחמם differently, essentially:

> broom roots to warm themselves



rather than:

> broom roots were their bread.



I think something in this direction is considerably more likely than literally eating the roots.

Again:

good Tur-Sinai: yes.
evidence of Aramaic translation: zero.

And this changes my assessment of his overall theory.

The real result of the experiment

If Job really were a bad Hebrew translation of an Aramaic original, I would expect dozens of cases like Job 24:10:

> 1. Hebrew is conspicuously nonsensical.


2. A common Aramaic expression reconstructs naturally.


3. There is an obvious source-language ambiguity explaining exactly how the translator made the error.


4. The reconstructed reading dramatically improves syntax/parallelism/context.


5. The proposed Aramaic doesn't itself have to be invented to make the trick work.





Instead, after digging through Tur-Sinai's most discussed proposals, there are maybe 2–5 respectable candidates, and only 24:10 really makes me sit up.

Worse for the global hypothesis, some of Tur-Sinai's best interpretations are not Aramaic-translation explanations at all. Job 26:13 and 30:4 demonstrate that he was an unusually gifted Semitic philologist; they don't demonstrate an Aramaic Vorlage.

And there is a major alternative explanation for exactly the same data: the author of Job knew Aramaic extremely well and was deliberately writing exotic, multilingual Hebrew. That becomes especially attractive where Job places Hebrew and Aramaic equivalents poetically beside one another. A bad translator is a less natural explanation for deliberate bilingual artistry.

So my grades after actually doing the test would be:

Hypothesis	Grade

Job's poet knew Aramaic intimately	A+
Job deliberately contains Aramaizing/foreignizing language	A
Some Joban phrases reflect Aramaic interference or calquing	B+
A few MT cruxes may ultimately derive from misunderstood Aramaic	B−
Substantial poetic Job was translated from an Aramaic Vorlage	C− / D+
The bizarre Hebrew of Job is mainly the result of a bad Aramaic→Hebrew translation	D


So Tur-Sinai found a real phenomenon, but probably overfit it. His strongest observation survives: Job often makes much more sense if you think in Aramaic. His strongest causal inference—therefore this was an Aramaic book mistranslated into Hebrew—doesn't survive nearly as well.

The one place I'd keep a large red circle around is Job 24:10. That one deserves much more attention than it usually gets.
